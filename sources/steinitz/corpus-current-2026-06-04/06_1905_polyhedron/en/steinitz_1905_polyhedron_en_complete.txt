On a Remarkable Polyhedron with One-Sided Total Surface
                                       By Ernst Steinitz in Berlin.

   The purpose of the following pages is to pursue, for polyhedra and especially for one particular
polyhedron, several facts concerning one-sided surfaces which are known from analysis situs.


1. Derivation and description of the one-sided heptahedron.
We begin with the consideration of a regular octahedron. Let its center be O, and let its vertices,
grouped into three pairs of opposite vertices, be X− , X+ ; Y− , Y+ ; Z− , Z+ . For a more definite
picture, suppose the octahedron placed as in Fig. 1, so that the diagonal X− X+ is directed
from left to right, Y− Y+ from front to back, and Z− Z+ from below upward. We divide the eight
octahedral faces into two groups, the positive and the negative, assigning to each face the sign
of the product of the signs used in denoting its vertices. Thus the positive faces are

            α = X+ Y− Z− ,        β = X− Y+ Z− ,           γ = X− Y− Z+ ,        δ = X+ Y+ Z+ ,

and the negative faces are

           α′ = X− Y+ Z+ ,        β ′ = X+ Y− Z+ ,         γ ′ = X+ Y+ Z− ,       δ ′ = X− Y− Z− .

Beside these two groups of faces we consider a third, the group of squares

                   ξ = Y− Z− Y+ Z+ ,       η = Z− X− Z+ X+ ,          ζ = X− Y− X+ Y+ .

Through each edge of the octahedron there passes one face from each of these three groups.
Hence, if we omit any one of the three groups, the remaining faces form a closed polyhedron,
since two of them pass through each edge. Thus, in addition to the octahedron, by combining the
squares with the positive or the negative octahedral faces respectively, we obtain two mutually
similar heptahedra. All three polyhedra have the same vertices and edges. The octahedron can
be brought onto itself in 24 ways by rotations about O; in addition, there are 24 symmetric
operations, that is, reflections and rotatory reflections, which do the same. Of these operations,
one half – namely 12 rotations and 12 symmetric operations – carry each heptahedron into itself,
while the other half interchange the two heptahedra.
   In what follows we refer to the heptahedron formed from the faces

                 α = X+ Y− Z− ,      β = X− Y+ Z− ,       γ = X− Y− Z+ ,      δ = X+ Y+ Z+ ,

                 ξ = Y− Z− Y+ Z+ ,     η = Z− X− Z+ X+ ,        ζ = X− Y− X+ Y+

and call it, for short, the “one-sided heptahedron”. It is indeed one-sided. Suppose we move
on the side of the face X+ Y+ Z+ away from O (Fig. 1). Crossing the edge X+ Y+ , we reach
the lower side of X+ Y+ X− Y− ; then, crossing X− Y− , the side of X− Y− Z+ away from O; then,
crossing Y− Z+ , the right side of Y− Z− Y+ Z+ ; and finally, crossing Y+ Z+ , the side of the original
face X+ Y+ Z+ facing the center.

following way, after Möbius. Consider the sequence of traversed polygons, each adjacent to the
next, while the last coincides with the first. Give the first an arbitrary direction of circulation,
say X+ Y+ Z+ , and determine the direction of circulation of each following polygon so that the
common edge with the preceding polygon is traversed in the opposite sense. We then obtain

We see that the face appearing as the initial and final polygon receives opposite directions of

conversely; but in certain spaces of other kinds this is no longer so. A general theorem on
the relations between one-sidedness and two-sidedness on the one hand, and reversibility and
non-reversibility of the indicatrix on the other, for configurations of arbitrarily many dimensions,

    The fact that this transformation is effected by a single closed cut shows, as analysis situs
teaches, that the heptahedron, as a one-sided surface of least connectivity, is equivalent to the
projective plane, that is, it can be mapped onto it one-to-one and continuously in the sense
of projective geometry. This mapping will concern us below. In each of the diagonals X− X+ ,
Y− Y+ , Z− Z+ two squares intersect. The points of these diagonals are therefore double points of
the total surface, except for the vertices, which are simple, and for the point O, which counts
triple. In the mapping under discussion, a double point will correspond to two different points
of the plane, and O to three.

3. The general one-sided heptahedron.
Every polyhedron is transformed by any collineation into an isomorphic one. If we are to admit
arbitrary collineations, we must of course also admit polyhedra extending to infinity. Only for
a few polyhedra with small number of faces does the converse of this statement hold, namely
that isomorphic polyhedra must also be collinear. For the one-sided heptahedron it does hold,
provided we impose only the conditions that the polyhedron is genuinely a spatial figure and that
its faces are convex.1 If one tries to construct, in the most general way, a polyhedron with the
seven faces
                            X+ Y+ Z+ , X+ Y− Z− , X− Y+ Z− , X− Y− Z+ ,
                                Y+ Z+ Y− Z− , Z+ X+ Z− X− , X+ Y+ X− Y− ,

one sees that, since of the three lines X− X+ , Y− Y+ , Z− Z+ each pair is to lie in a quadrilateral
plane and hence to intersect, the three lines must either all pass through a point or all lie in
a plane. The latter is excluded by the requirement that the figure be spatial. The three lines
therefore have a common point O. This point must not coincide with any of the six vertices, if
genuine quadrilaterals are to arise. Thus, to construct the heptahedron, one first draws through
a point O three lines not lying in one plane and chooses on each of them two distinct points
different from O, otherwise arbitrary, X− , X+ ; Y− , Y+ ; Z− , Z+ . Once this has been done, the
rest of the construction can be carried out always and in only one way.
    At the construction of each edge, for example X− Y+ , there is at first an ambiguity as to which
of the two joining segments completing the whole line is to be taken. This ambiguity is removed
by the condition of convexity. For example, if X− Y− X+ Y+ is to be a convex quadrilateral, then
the intersection of the unlimited lines Y+ X− and Y− X+ must lie outside the bounded sides
Y+ X− and Y− X+ . Accordingly, the six opposite pairs of edges of the heptahedron

                                      Y+ Z+ ,   Y − Z− ,    Z+ X+ ,
                                      Z− X− ,   X+ Y+ ,     X− Y− ,
                                      Y+ Z− ,   Y − Z+ ,    Z+ X− ,
                                      Z− X+ ,   X + Y− ,    X− Y+

are to be constructed so that only the extensions of the edges of each pair intersect. These edges
then bound not only the three convex quadrilaterals

                           Y− Z− Y+ Z+ ,   Z− X− Z+ X+ ,       X− Y− X+ Y+ ,

but also the four triangles

                         X+ Y− Z− ,    X− Y+ Z− ,       X− Y− Z+ ,    X+ Y+ Z+ .

To show, for instance, that the edges Y+ Z+ , Z+ X+ , X+ Y+ bound a triangle and not an odd open
polygonal path, it suffices to exhibit a line or plane which meets not these segments themselves
but their complementary segments. By our construction this is the case for the plane X− Y− Z− ,
which contains the opposite edges Y+ Z+ , Z+ X+ , X+ Y+ .
   Given two such heptahedra, with their vertices denoted by the same letters X− , X+ ; Y− , Y+ ; Z− , Z+ ,
we must show that there is one and only one collineation which carries the first heptahedron
onto the second, taking each vertex to the equally named one. Such a collineation must carry
the planes X+ Y+ Z+ , X+ Y− Z− , X+ Y+ X− Y− , Y+ Z+ Y− Z− , Z+ X+ Z− X− of the first figure to the
equally named planes of the second. Since no four of these planes pass through one point, this
condition determines a collineation uniquely. As is immediately seen, this collineation takes the
vertices of the first heptahedron to the equally named vertices of the second; and, since convex
polygons can go only to convex polygons and, as we saw, the heptahedron is determined by its
vertices under the imposed convexity condition, it also takes the faces and edges of the first
heptahedron to the equally named faces and edges of the second.


4. Facewise collinear mapping of the one-sided heptahedron onto
the plane division determined by a complete quadrilateral.
Suppose two isomorphic polyhedra are mapped one-to-one and continuously onto each other in
such a way that every straight segment on a face of one corresponds to a straight segment on
the other; equivalently, each pair of corresponding faces is collinear. Such a mapping will be
called “facewise collinear”. It should be expressly emphasized, however, that one-to-one-ness and
continuity of the mapping are required also along the edges. Given a cube and any hexhedron

isomorphic to it and bounded by convex quadrilaterals, there is, for each pair of corresponding
faces, a definite collineation carrying one to the other. But the cube edges, belonging to two
faces each, then acquire two mappings, and these are in general not identical. Thus the mapping
considered in this example is not facewise collinear in our sense.
    A spatial collineation carrying one polyhedron to a second at the same time gives a facewise
collinear mapping of the two polyhedra onto each other. But it is evident that not every such
mapping must conversely be contained in a spatial collineation. In the case of the one-sided
heptahedron, however, the mapping furnished by the spatial collineation is the only facewise
collinear one. For first the collineations of the quadrilaterals are completely determined by the
assignment of the vertices, and the projective correspondences of all the edges thereby fixed in
turn determine the collineations in the triangles.
    If the phrase “facewise collinear mapping” is applied, analogously, not only to polyhedra but
also to divisions of the plane into regions, then a collineation of the whole plane at once supplies
a facewise collinear mapping of every region-division contained in it; conversely, however, not
every such mapping of an arbitrary region-division need arise from a collineation of the whole
plane. But if the division is specifically one of two planes ε and ε′ by complete quadrilaterals
abcd and a′ b′ c′ d′ , then exactly as for the heptahedra one proves that the collineation of the whole
planes determined by assigning a, b, c, d to a′ , b′ , c′ , d′ is the unique facewise collinear mapping of
the two divisions.

5. The double surface of the one-sided heptahedron.
To every one-sided surface there corresponds a definite double surface. Imagine first cutting out
a simply connected piece of the one-sided surface, placing a congruent piece on one side of it,
and then continuing this covering process continuously. In the end it forms a connected surface
which covers the original surface everywhere on both sides. This new surface is two-sided, since
on it one can distinguish the side with which it lies on the original surface from the other side.
In the case of a one-sided polyhedron the new surface is again a polyhedron, with twice the
number of vertices, edges, and
    faces. This polyhedron also has the peculiarity that its points coincide in pairs. But this
circumstance is irrelevant if one has only the type of the polyhedron in view, that is, if one does
not distinguish between isomorphic polyhedra; for among the many isomorphic polyhedra there
will in general certainly be some which do not have that peculiarity.
    To every one-sided polyhedron type there corresponds a definite two-sided one. In the case
of the one-sided heptahedron the double surface must be composed of six quadrilaterals and
eight triangles. One easily recognizes in it a type which can be represented by a very familiar
semiregular polyhedron. It is the cuboctahedron, which one can obtain, for instance, from the
cube by cutting off its corners by planes as far as the midpoints of the edges issuing from them.

    To settle the question quite generally, we first note that every convex polyhedron which is
facewise congruent to the double surface of a one-sided polyhedron must necessarily have a center.
Namely, if we map the double surface onto itself by assigning to every point the point coinciding
with it, this mapping is a facewise symmetrically congruent one. Hence the convex polyhedron
arising from the double surface is also facewise symmetrically congruent to itself. On the other
hand, by Cauchy’s polyhedron theorem, two isomorphic facewise congruent, or symmetrically
congruent, polyhedra are congruent, or symmetrically congruent, as wholes. Therefore the convex
polyhedron under consideration must be transformable into itself by a symmetric operation. This
symmetric operation, moreover, interchanges the polyhedron points in pairs, and is therefore
involutory. There are, however, only two involutory symmetric operations: reflection in a plane
and reflection in a point (inversion). If reflection in a plane occurred, the polyhedron would have
to lie on both sides of the reflecting plane and thus be cut by it. But then there would be points
on the polyhedron which coincide with their corresponding points. That would be possible only
if the polyhedron cut itself, and this is excluded by convexity. Thus only reflection in a point
remains, and this point is then the center.

6. Relations to elliptic geometry.
In the preceding discussion we considered two one-to-one correspondences: one between the
cuboctahedron and the double surface of the one-sided heptahedron, which is facewise congruent,
and one between the simple heptahedron and the plane divided by a complete quadrilateral.
The latter correspondence is facewise collinear; it is natural to ask whether in special cases
the collineations may become congruences. This can indeed occur, both for ordinary and for
elliptic measurement. The latter case is particularly interesting and is closely connected with
our last investigations. We shall discuss it more fully in one special case. First we note that the
considerations of the preceding paragraph remain essentially unchanged if, instead of ordinary
measurement, one takes elliptic or hyperbolic measurement as the basis. In particular, in all
three cases the following hold.
   1. If through a point O one draws three mutually perpendicular lines and describes about
O a sphere of radius r, then the sphere cuts the lines in 6 points, which form the vertices of a
one-sided heptahedron composed of regular triangles and quadrilaterals.
    2. If through a point M one draws three mutually perpendicular lines, and also the six lines
which bisect the angles formed by the first three, then these angle bisectors are cut by a sphere
about M of radius ρ in 12 points, which form the vertices of a convex cuboctahedron composed
of regular triangles and quadrilaterals. The 24 edges of the cuboctahedron form four regular
hexagons whose planes pass through M .
   3. Once the radius ρ has been chosen, the radius r can be chosen so that the cuboctahedron
and the double surface of the heptahedron become facewise congruent.
   Now assume elliptic measurement, and let π denote the length of the whole line. Let the radius
ρ then increase, and with it at the same time the radius r, in such a way that the corresponding
polyhedra – cuboctahedron and double heptahedron – always remain facewise congruent. At
the instant when ρ reaches the length π2 , every point of the sphere of radius ρ collides with

degenerates into the same double plane, the four regular hexagons becoming double lines. Two
coincident points of the double heptahedron again correspond to two coincident points of the

heptahedron and the simple plane which is facewise congruent. The radius r of the sphere
circumscribed about the heptahedron is equal to half the diagonal in the squares and hence, as

    If the heptahedron is now cut open, as in §1, along the edge path X+ Y− Z+ X− Y+ Z− X+ and
spread out in a plane, then the cut places fit together again and the total surface becomes a
plane. Henceforth we shall call this heptahedron, and all heptahedra sharing this property with
it, developable. It should be noted, however, that a continuous transformation of a heptahedron
into a plane without cutting is never possible, even if changes of length are allowed during the
motion. This rests on the fact that already among closed lines of projective space there exist
two essentially different kinds: lines of the first kind (even circuits) are cut by every plane,
and indeed by every closed surface, in an even number of points; lines of the second kind (odd
circuits) are cut by every plane in an odd number of points. Only lines of the same kind can be
continuously transformed into one another without cutting, and likewise a collineation cannot
change the kind of a line. Correspondingly one must distinguish surfaces of the first kind, which
contain only closed lines of the first kind, and surfaces of the second kind, which also contain
closed lines of the second kind. Every closed line or surface lying entirely in the finite part under
ordinary measurement, and therefore every line or surface collinear with such a one, is of the
first kind. Thus between the heptahedron and the plane there is the purely topological difference
that the heptahedron is a surface of the first kind whereas the plane is one of the second kind.
    The task with which we shall be concerned here is the determination of all developable one-
sided heptahedra, or equivalently of all region-divisions of a plane which can arise by developing
a heptahedron. In view of the discussion in §4 and of the fact that congruence is only a special
case of collineation, it is clear that only such region-divisions can come into question as are
produced by a complete quadrilateral.
    We first treat the problem for ordinary geometry, retain the previous notation, and assume

    Accordingly we obtain the following general construction. Take four points A′ , B ′ , C ′ , D′ at
mutual distances π2 , and take a plane ε′ passing through none of them and otherwise entirely
arbitrary. The planes α′ = B ′ C ′ D′ , β ′ = C ′ D′ A′ , γ ′ = D′ A′ B ′ , δ ′ = A′ B ′ C ′ divide space into 8
tetrahedra. Into one of these – call it T – the plane ε′ does not enter. By repeated reflection
of ε′ in the planes α′ , β ′ , γ ′ , δ ′ there arise 7 further planes. The parts of these planes lying in
T form a one-sided developable heptahedron for which ε∞ = ε′ ; the development gives the
complete-quadrilateral division which occurs both in ε′ and in the heptahedron planes.
   Of course, in each of the 8 tetrahedra formed by α′ , β ′ , γ ′ , δ ′ we obtain a developable
heptahedron by collecting the parts of the plane ε and of its mirror images contained there. All
these heptahedra pass into one another under the reflections, and the corresponding planes ε∞
are the 8 planes from whose parts they are assembled.

8 points distributed among the 8 tetrahedra formed by these planes. Again denote one of these
tetrahedra by T , and map the whole space eight-to-one onto T by assigning to each point that

one of its mirror points which lies in T . Then a straight line, since it is split by its intersections

segments. It is clear that this circuit describes the path of a light ray moving inside T and
reflected at the boundary faces of T . If a plane pencil of light rays is emitted from a point
inside T , then the rays, over their entire course, fill a surface which, as the image of a plane
of space, represents a one-sided developable heptahedron. Furthermore, if F is any connected
surface in space, its image Φ in T is also connected, and the individual parts of Φ are congruent
or symmetric to the corresponding parts of F . On the surface Φ, as in T generally, only even
line-circuits are possible.
    All these considerations apply, with only very slight modifications, also to the case of ordinary
measurement. In particular it follows here as well that the developability of the heptahedron
depends only on the associated negative octahedral planes. Of these, three must be mutually
perpendicular and the fourth must fall to infinity. Thus take three mutually perpendicular
planes α′ , β ′ , γ ′ , and also a plane ε′ which neither passes through their point of intersection nor
is parallel to one of their intersection lines. If one collects all those parts of ε′ and of the planes
arising from ε′ by repeated reflections in α′ , β ′ , γ ′ which fall in one and the same octant, then
one has constructed, in the most general way, a developable one-sided heptahedron.
