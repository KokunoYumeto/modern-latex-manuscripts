Continuity and the Differential Quotient
By Ernst Steinitz in Charlottenburg.
That there are continuous functions which possess a differential quotient at no point was first
proved by Weierstrass. Other authors have added a series of further examples to those given by
him. In what follows, some general considerations on continuity and differentiability of functions
will be set out, from which the existence of continuous non-differentiable functions arises quite
naturally, so that the ordinary character of the case is brought out in a clear way and, I hope,
in a form suitable for teaching. Throughout, only finite real single-valued functions of one real
variable will be considered.

§ 1.
Let p and q be two real numbers, q > p; let A be the domain of all numbers between p and q
inclusive; and let f (x) be a continuous real single-valued function defined on this domain. Then
f (x) is also uniformly continuous. If
a0 , a1 , a2 , . . .
is a fundamental sequence of numbers in A, then its limit a also belongs to the domain A, and
from the continuity of f (x) it follows that the corresponding function values
f (a0 ), f (a1 ), f (a2 ), . . .
form a fundamental sequence with limit f (a).
From this we can draw an important conclusion. Let B be a numerical domain contained in
A (so that every number of B is also in A), and suppose it has the property that every number
of A can be represented as the limit of a fundamental sequence of numbers from B. It follows
that the continuous function f (x) is determined on the whole domain A by the values which it
assumes at the points of the domain B. Thus, if φ(x) is a function defined on the domain B,
there can certainly be no more than one function f (x) whose domain of validity is A, which
everywhere there satisfies the condition of continuity, and which agrees with φ(x) at the points
of B.
Of course, not every function φ(x) defined on the domain B will admit a function f (x) of the
indicated kind. For if such a function exists, then it follows that φ(x) has the following property:
if ε > 0 is an arbitrarily small quantity, one can choose δ > 0 so that for any two points x1 and
x2 of the domain B whose difference is < δ, the difference of the corresponding function values
φ(x2 ) and φ(x1 ) is < ε. Conversely, if φ(x) satisfies this condition, it is also easy to prove that a
function f (x) of the indicated kind exists. Indeed, if a is any point of A, then the assumption
made about φ(x) shows that to every fundamental sequence of points of B
b0 , b1 , b2 , . . .
with limit a there corresponds a fundamental sequence
φ(b0 ), φ(b1 ), φ(b2 ), . . . ,
and that all these fundamental sequences have the same limit. Denoting this limit by f (a), one
obtains a uniquely defined function f (x) on the entire domain A, and it is easily seen that it is
continuous and agrees with φ(x) at the points of B.
I shall say that a function φ, defined on any domain, is continuous within this domain if, for
any two points x1 , x2 of the domain, as soon as their difference is sufficiently small, the difference
