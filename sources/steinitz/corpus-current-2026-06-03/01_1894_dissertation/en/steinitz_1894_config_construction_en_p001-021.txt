   The construction of configurations n3 has so far been treated in detail only in special cases.
Martinetti recognized that, in general, it leads to a problem of the second degree, for example
to the problem of determining the double elements of two incident point rows, and he also
gave an indication of the path leading to this.1 The subject is, however, interesting enough to
deserve a more detailed treatment. Such a treatment will be given below.
   Methods for the linear production of the configurations n3 will then be attached to this.
Schroeter2 was the first to give such a procedure for a special class of configurations; it can
also be applied to many other configurations. We shall see that for most configurations n3 a
linear construction is possible. The construction methods that we shall give cannot always be
carried out, but they fail only in a few cases.


§1. Definition
A plane configuration n3 (Cf. n3 ) is a plane geometric figure consisting of n lines and n points
so situated with respect to one another that every line contains three points and three lines
pass through every point.
   We denote the points of the configuration by n signs, for example by the numerals 1, 2, . . . , n,
and write these numerals in n columns, each of which is to represent a line of the configuration,
by containing the three numerals that denote the points lying on the line. In this way we arrive
at a schematic representation of the configuration. Each configuration can be denoted in many
ways. Apart from the fact that it is immaterial how we arrange the columns of the scheme
and the elements (numerals) inside any one column, the order in which we denote the points
of the configuration by the numerals 1, 2, . . . , n is arbitrary. We shall therefore regard two
configurations as the same if the scheme of one passes into that of the other by a substitution
of the signs 1, 2, . . . , n.
   From the definition of configurations n3 the following properties result for their notation:

   1. Every column contains three elements.

   2. Every element occurs in three columns.

    Every grouping of n elements into n columns that has these four properties will be called the

even if the scheme does not owe its origin to a geometric configuration. Conversely, starting from
an arbitrary such scheme, we shall now investigate how geometric configurations corresponding
to the given scheme can be produced.


§2. General decomposition into horizontal rows
A property that belongs not only to configurations n3 but to all configurations nµ helps us
to a construction valid for all configurations n3 ; we shall therefore prove the presence of this
property in general for configurations nµ .3
    Theorem. In the scheme of every configuration nµ , the elements inside the columns can be
arranged in such a way that every horizontal row4 contains each of the elements 1, 2, . . . , n
once, and hence only once.
    To prove this theorem, we first show that one can associate the points and lines of the
configuration in such a way that to each point there corresponds a line passing through it.
Suppose that such an association has already been made with a number of points and lines. Let
the complex of these lines be A, the complex of these points A, the complex of the remaining
lines B, and the complex of the remaining points B. Then to every point of A there corresponds
a line of A passing through it, and our task plainly consists in enlarging the complex A so that
it comprises all lines of the configuration. Then A will contain all points of the configuration,
and the complexes B and B will disappear.
    If a line b of B contains a point b of B, then we can associate b and b with one another. The
point b thereby passes from B into A, and the line b passes from B into A. This procedure is
not applicable if all lines of B contain only points of A.
    In that case we denote by a0 all points of A that lie on a line of B; when distinction is
needed we attach a second index. The corresponding lines, which must all belong to A, are
denoted by α0 . The points of A lying on these lines but not occurring among the points a0 are
denoted by a1 , their corresponding lines by α1 ; the points of A lying on these lines and not
belonging to a0 or a1 are denoted by a2 , their corresponding lines by α2 , and so on. Since n is
finite, we must finally arrive at points ak whose corresponding lines αk contain only points of
A already included among a0 , a1 , . . . , ak .
    We divide the complex A into two complexes A0 and A′ , the first containing all points
a0 , . . . , ak , while the other consists of the remaining points of A. If the lines corresponding to
the points of A0 are assigned to a complex A0 , and the lines corresponding to the points of A′
to a complex A′ , then A0 and A′ together form the complex A.
    Assume that a line of the complex A0 , say αi with i ≤ k, contains a point of the complex
B, say b′ . If a′i is the point corresponding to αi , then it is contained in at least one of the

§3. Closed traversals
The proof just given is general and also includes the case where the configuration nµ decomposes
into several configurations. The latter case occurs, for example, whenever the complex A′
disappears.
   If ν horizontal rows are omitted from the configuration nµ , then a configuration nµ−ν
remains, which of course may also decompose. For ν = µ − 2 one obtains a polygon or a cycle
of polygons, each side of which still contains µ − 2 additional points of the configuration, while
through each vertex there still pass µ − 2 lines. We see from this that every configuration nµ
can be traversed in several closed circuits in such a way that every line occurs once as a side
and every point once as a vertex.
   If the given configuration nµ decomposes, then every configuration nν contained in it, with
ν < µ, must also decompose. But the converse of this theorem is not valid. A configuration nµ
may be completely connected, so that one can pass from any one of its points to any other by
proceeding along configuration-lines, and nevertheless every configuration nµ−1 contained in it
may decompose. Such cases already occur for configurations n3 . Thus not every connected
configuration n3 can be traversed in a single closed circuit; rather, for any given number k one
can form configurations n3 that do not decompose but that cannot be traversed in fewer than
k circuits.


§4. Arrangement of the complexes
In the following investigations, where we deal only with configurations n3 , we assume that they
do not decompose.
   If the elements in the scheme of a configuration n3 are arranged so that every horizontal
row contains every element, then this property is preserved when the horizontal rows are
interchanged; after such an interchange the scheme also denotes the same configuration as
before. We now put an arbitrarily chosen horizontal row in first place; the two others denote a
number of polygons.
   To make this still clearer, and keeping the order of the elements within the columns
unchanged, we arrange the columns as follows. We choose any column as the first, and
determine the following columns in such a way that each second element of a column is identical

                                                5
with the third of the preceding one. This procedure can be continued until we arrive at a
column whose third element is identical with the second of the initially chosen column. The
totality of columns, or lines, obtained in this way is called a complex of the configuration. If
the complex does not yet comprise all columns of the configuration, we proceed to form a
second complex by again taking an arbitrary one of the remaining columns and determining
each following column in the same way as in the first complex. If the configuration is still not
exhausted by this second complex, we form a third, fourth, and so on. That such a mode of
formation is possible follows at once from the fact that every horizontal row of the scheme
contains every element once.
   If the first horizontal row of the configuration is omitted, one polygon remains from each
complex. The second, and likewise the third, horizontal row of the complex at the same time
gives the order in which the vertices of the polygon follow one another. This property is
preserved when the columns of a complex are cyclically interchanged; no other interchange is
permitted.
   Let the number of complexes be k. We arrange them as follows. First we determine an
arbitrary complex as the last one; call it Ck , its first horizontal row Rk , and the polygon
represented by the two other rows Pk . If the configuration does not consist only of this complex,
then not all elements of Pk can be contained in Rk ; at least one of them must occur in the first
row of another complex. Otherwise the complex Ck would by itself already form a configuration.
   We place such a complex in the next-to-last position and make a cyclic interchange of its
columns so that the first element of the last column belongs to the elements contained in Pk .
Call the complex thus determined Ck−1 ; let Rk−1 be its first horizontal row and Pk−1 the
polygon contained in it. If k > 2, not all points of Pk−1 and Pk can be represented in the
rows Rk−1 and Rk ; at least one of them must occur in the first row of another complex. We
choose such a complex as Ck−2 and, by cyclically interchanging its columns, arrange that the
element of Pk−1 or Pk contained in Rk−2 becomes the first element of the last column of Ck−2 .
It is clear how this procedure is to be continued, and that it leads to complexes C1 , C2 , . . . , Ck
with first horizontal rows R1 , R2 , . . . , Rk and polygons P1 , P2 , . . . , Pk , having the property that
the last point of Ri (i < k) occurs as a point of the second and third row only in one of the
following polygons Pi+1 , . . . , Pk .


§5. Normal notation
After the columns of the configuration have been arranged in this way, we replace the elements
of the first horizontal row of the scheme, in order, by the signs t1 , t2 , . . . , tn , and those of the
second row by p1 , p2 , . . . , pn . Each element of the third row is to be replaced by the sign that
was introduced for the same element in the second row. We further denote by ri (i = 1, 2, . . . , n)
the column whose first element is ti , and by pi the column whose first element denotes the point
also represented by the sign pi . The notation introduced for elements and columns will also be
used for the points and lines themselves. Thus each point and each line of the configuration
receives two names.
   If αi (i = 1, 2, . . . , k) is the number of lines belonging to the complex Ci , we have the
following scheme for the configuration:

§6. Construction after 3n − 1 conditions
The configuration n3 can be regarded as a grouping of n lines r1 , r2 , . . . , rn and n points
p1 , p2 , . . . , pn that arises by subjecting these initially arbitrary elements, one after another, to
3n conditions, not always independent, as if n lines and n points in the plane were moved
until they assumed the position prescribed by the configuration. We first seek to produce a
figure in which only 3n − 1 of the 3n conditions are satisfied: for the time being we omit the
requirement that the line rn contain the point tn . Notice that the omitted condition is not
distinguished from the others. Since we placed an arbitrary complex last, and since any cyclic
interchange was allowed among the lines of that complex, any line of the configuration can
occupy the last place; hence rn is an arbitrary line of the configuration. Since the horizontal
rows of the scheme could also be interchanged arbitrarily, tn is an arbitrary point of the line rn .
    We construct the figure in question by determining the points and lines in the normal order
                                       p1 , r1 , p2 , r2 , . . . , pn , rn
and ensuring that the remaining 3n − 1 conditions of the configuration are fulfilled. It is easy
to see that this is possible. Suppose that the elements
                                        p1 , r1 , p2 , r2 , . . . , pi , ri
have already been constructed. The point pi+1 is now to be determined.
   There are two cases. If pi+1 is not the first point of one of the polygons P1 , . . . , Pk , then
the configuration requires it to lie on the lines ri , ri+1 , pi+1 . Of these lines ri+1 is not yet
constructed, whereas ri is; for pi+1 both cases are possible. Accordingly, depending on whether
pi+1 belongs to the lines already constructed or not, the point pi+1 is to be placed at the
intersection of the lines pi+1 and ri , or it is to be taken as an arbitrary point of ri .
   If pi+1 is the first point of a polygon, and pj the last point of the same polygon, then
pi+1 must lie on the lines ri+1 , pj , pi+1 , of which at most pi+1 has already been constructed.
According to whether this is the case or not, pi+1 is to be taken arbitrarily on the line pi+1 , or
altogether arbitrarily.
   In constructing the following line ri+1 , two cases are again to be distinguished. If ri+1 is
not the last line of a polygon, it must contain the points pi+1 , pi+2 , ri+1 . Of these, the first has
already been found and the second has not. Depending on whether the point ri+1 belongs to
the already constructed points or not, ri+1 must be the joining line of pi+1 and ri+1 , or an
arbitrary line through the former point.
   If ri+1 is the last line of one of the polygons P1 , P2 , . . . , Pk , and rm is the first line of the
same polygon, then ri+1 must contain the points pn , ri+1 , pi+1 . The points pn and pi+1 have
already been determined, but ri+1 has not; for, in our arrangement of the configuration-lines,
those points that lie on the last line of a polygon but do not occur there as vertices of that
polygon must be vertices of one of the following polygons. Thus ri+1 must be constructed as
the joining line of pn and pi+1 .
   Finally, if pm is the first point of the last polygon and we construct rn as the joining line
pm pn , the constructed figure will satisfy 3n − 1 conditions of the configuration.

§7. The last condition
In order to satisfy the last condition of the configuration as well, we seek, in the sequence of
elements
                                     p1 , r1 , p2 , r2 , . . . , pn , rn ,
the last one that, after the preceding elements have been constructed and the last configuration-
condition has been ignored, is not yet completely determined. We then try to dispose of
this element in such a way that this condition also is fulfilled when the construction of the
configuration is completed by the procedure described in §6. It is clear that the element
sought belongs to the last polygon, as vertex or side, since the first point of this polygon,
pm , is not completely determined by the preceding elements. The element sought may be
partially determined by what precedes it: if it is a point, it may be forced to lie on an already
constructed line; if it is a line, it may be forced to pass through an already constructed point.
It is also possible, however, that after the construction of the preceding elements it is still
entirely undetermined.
   This last case, which by the discussion of §6 can occur only when pm , the first point of the
last polygon, is the element sought, can be avoided. Since the point tm−1 must be a vertex of
the last polygon, a cyclic interchange of the vertices and sides of the latter can be made so
that pm = tm−1 . Then pm lies on the already constructed line tm−1 .
   Let the element sought be a point pi , with i > m. If, in the manner of §6, we construct the
points and lines
                                      p1 , r1 , p2 , r2 , . . . , pi−1 , ri−1 ,
and denote their totality by A, while the rest are collected under the symbol B, then the
following is easily seen. The line pi belongs to B; for the point pi lies on the lines ri−1 and pi ,
of which the first belongs to A. Contrary to the hypothesis, it would already be completely
determined if pi also belonged to A. If now

                                         pi = tk ,          pk = tl ,

and k > i, then the elements
                                       tk , pk+1 , rk+1 , . . . , pn , rn
with the exception of the point tk all belong to A.
   This is seen as follows. Suppose that a point tj different from tk , with j > i, belongs to B,
and that tj = pl . Then l > i; l = i is impossible, because pl = pi would imply tj = tk . If l > j,
then the line rj , which contains the points tj , pj , pj+1 , would still not be completely determined
after all preceding elements p1 , r1 , . . . , pj had been constructed. This is impossible, since pi
was supposed to be the last such element and rj follows pi . The only remaining possibility is
j > l > i. Since rj belongs to B and rj = pl , j > l > i, the sequence

                                    ti , pi+1 , ri+1 , pi+2 , . . . , pn , rn

contains before tj an element different from tk , namely pl , that belongs to B.
   Similarly, from the existence in the sequence ti , pi+1 , . . . , pn , rn of a line belonging to B one
can infer the existence of a point, likewise belonging to B and different from tk , which stands
before the line in that sequence. Indeed, if pj = rl is the line in question, then through pj pass
the lines rj−1 , pj , rj . Since pj = rl is to occur in the sequence and belong to B, we first have
j > i and l > i. Since the point pj is to be determined after the preceding elements have been
constructed, because j > i, the line pj = rl on which it lies must precede it in the normal order.
Hence j > l. From j > i it follows that pj belongs to B; from j > l it follows that the point

pj = rl stands before the line pj in the sequence. Finally rl cannot be identical with tk , since
l = k would imply j = i, whereas j > i.
   Thus every element that belongs to the sequence ti , pi+1 , . . . , pn , rn , is different from tk , and
belongs to B, must be preceded in that sequence by an element with the same three properties.
This gives a contradiction, since only finitely many elements are involved.
   If the last condition of the configuration is now also taken into account, it follows from the
foregoing that the elements

                                  tk , pk+1 , rk+1 , . . . , pn , rn , pi , pi+1 , . . .

can be obtained in this order as joining lines and intersections of previously determined elements.
For instance,

              pm rn = rn , (rn , pn ) = pn ,      pn rn−1 = rn−1 ,           (rn−1 , pn−1 ) = pn−1 , . . .

On the other hand, as soon as pi has been assumed on the line ri−1 , one obtains pi ri = ri , and
analogously the following points and lines up to the element denoted by tk .
   The configuration still requires the points tk = pi , pk , pk+1 , or respectively tn , pn , pm if
k = n, to lie on a line. Put first the joining line pk+1 pk = rk′ and the intersection (rk′ , ri−1 ) = p′i .
As pi traverses the line ri−1 , the point p′i describes a projective point row on the same line.
For all conditions of the configuration to be fulfilled, p′i = pi must hold. Thus we arrive at the
problem of finding the double elements of two incident point rows. It is well known that there
are two such elements, and determining them usually requires a quadratic construction.
   The cases in which pi is identical with pm , or in which the last element not completely
determined by the preceding elements is a line, are dealt with in quite a similar way. A
discussion of these cases may therefore be omitted.
