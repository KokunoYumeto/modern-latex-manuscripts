§ 4. Isomorphism; the function χ.
Definition of isomorphism.

Suppose that between the residues ρ of a module a and the residues ρ′ of a module a′ one
can establish a one-to-one correspondence with the following property: whenever ρi , ρ′i and
ρk , ρ′k are two pairs of corresponding residues, then ρi + ρk and ρ′i + ρ′k also form a pair of
corresponding residues. Then the correspondence is called an “isomorphic” relation between a
and a′ . Two modules a, a′ which admit at least one isomorphic relation are called “isomorphic
to one another”.1 Thus the relation of isomorphism is always reciprocal.
    If ρ1 , ρ2 , . . . are all residues of a, and if ρ′1 , ρ′2 , . . . are, in this order, the residues of a′ assigned
to them isomorphically, then the isomorphic relation in question will be denoted by

                                                        ρi ∥ ρ′i .

It follows easily from the definition of isomorphism that, in general,

                                                         ρi ∥ ρ′′i

ρ′i ∥ ρ′′i ”. If one composes the isomorphic relation ρi ∥ ρ′i successively with all distinct isomorphic

following theorem holds:
       If a is isomorphic to a′ , and a′ is isomorphic to a′′ , then a is isomorphic to a′′ and

     We now examine more closely under what conditions two modules a and a′ are isomorphic.
It is easy to see that this is so when they belong to the same class. For if [E] is a unimodular
transformation carrying a into a′ , then (§ 2) it also carries every residue of a into a residue of a′
and in this way establishes a one-to-one correspondence between the residues of a and those of
a′ , which is immediately recognized as isomorphic.
    Conversely, let us now show that two isomorphic modules of the same degree, a, a′ , must
also belong to the same class. On the basis of the preceding considerations, and without loss of
generality, we may assume that a and a′ are the principal modules within their classes. We also
exclude the case in which the norms of a and a′ vanish, since it is irrelevant for our purpose. Let

                                                        ρi ∥ ρ′i

be an isomorphic relation between a and a′ , let m be any integer, and let ρi , ρ′i run successively
through all residues of a, a′ . Then the system mρi belongs to the module a just as often as the
system mρ′i belongs to the module a′ . The computation of the number in question is particularly
simple when a, a′ are principal modules. If a1 , a2 , . . . , an are the invariants of a, and a′1 , a′2 , . . . , a′n
those of a′ , one immediately sees that the number of residues ρi for which mρi is contained in a
is
                                     (m, a1 )(m, a2 ) · · · (m, an ),
and similarly, the number of residues ρ′i for which mρ′i belongs to a′ is

                                           (m, a′1 )(m, a′2 ) · · · (m, a′n ).

Hence for every integer m the equality

                          (m, a1 )(m, a2 ) · · · (m, an ) = (m, a′1 )(m, a′2 ) · · · (m, a′n )

must hold; and this, as a simple consideration shows, can occur only when

                                    a1 = a′1 ,    a2 = a′2 ,        ...,     an = a′n ,

that is, when a and a′ belong to the same class.
   Nevertheless two modules of different degrees may certainly be isomorphic; the question of
the isomorphism of two modules is settled by the following theorem.
   I. Two modules are isomorphic if and only if their systems of invariants are identical after
the invariants which reduce to 1 have been removed.

and under this assumption the proof is very simple. Let

§ 5. Relation among the functions φ, χ, ψ.
The functions φ, χ, ψ considered here stand in a very simple relation to one another, made clear
by the following argument.
   Let A be a class of degree n, let a be a module in A, and let R be an r-rowed residue of a.
Then, as a simple consideration shows, the following theorem holds: in order that

                                             (a, R) = e                                        (1)

hold, it is necessary and sufficient that for every row ρ of n elements one can find a row σ of r
elements satisfying the congruence

                                       σR ≡ ρ (mod a).                                         (2)

Now suppose that (1) is satisfied and that r = n. Then all σ for which σR is contained in a
form a module a′ of degree n; all σ satisfying a congruence of the form (2), where ρ is regarded
as given, are representatives of a definite residue of a′ ; and conversely, for all representatives
of a definite residue of a′ , σR represents one and the same residue of a. Thus one obtains a
one-to-one correspondence between the residues of a and those of a′ by making the residue σ of
a′ correspond to the residue σR of a. This correspondence is, as one easily sees, an isomorphism.
It follows that the module a′ belongs to the class A (§ 4, I). We shall say that the residue R of a
belongs to the module a′ , and we shall denote the isomorphic relation mediated by R by [R].
   If R′ is also an n-rowed residue of a satisfying condition (1), but distinct from R, then R′
may well belong to the same module a′ , but the isomorphic relation [R′ ] is different from [R].
Otherwise, for every row σ the congruence

§ 7. Introduction of several functions depending on several
classes.
The function ψ forms the basis for determining other functions which are important for the
theory of modules and which will be treated below. We shall consider only modules and classes
of positive norm.
    A module a of positive norm has, as one easily sees, only finitely many divisors, which (§ 2,
III) are distributed among those classes that are divisors of Cl a. We now focus on the divisors
of a which belong to a specified class B. If

                                                  b1 , b2 , . . . , bk

are these modules and E denotes a unimodular system, then (§ 2, VI)

                                              b1 E, b2 E, . . . , bk E

are all divisors of aE within the class B. It follows that the number of divisors of a within B
depends only on the classes A = Cl a and B. We denote this number by

                                                     t(A | B).

Similarly, the number of multiples which a module b of class B possesses within the class A is a
function solely of the classes A and B. Let it be denoted by

                                                     v(A | B).

The number of pairs of modules ai , bk satisfying

                            Cl ai = A,            Cl bk = B,             (ai , bk ) = bk

is represented by each of the expressions ψ(A)t(A | B) and ψ(B)v(A | B). Thus

                                I.          ψ(A)t(A | B) = ψ(B)v(A | B).

Furthermore § 2, XV gives the formulas
                                                               Y
                                     II.       t(A | B) =            t(Ap | Bp ),
                                                                 p

                                     III.      v(A | B) =            v(Ap | Bp ).

From § 2, III we know that t(A | B) and v(A | B) are nonzero or zero according as A is or is not
divisible by B. Finally, for every class A one has (§ 2, IV)

                                     IV.       t(A | A) = v(A | A) = 1.

   Other class-functions are reached by the following considerations.
   If the modules a1 , a2 , . . . , as , which need not all be distinct, are divisors of a module c, then
their least common multiple [a1 , a2 , . . . , as ] is also a divisor of c. One may therefore pose the
problem of determining, for a given c, s modules a1 , a2 , . . . , as such that

                                              [a1 , a2 , . . . , as ] = c.

This problem is of course always solvable, and in general in many ways. It is different, however,
if one imposes the condition that each module ai shall belong to a specified given class Ai .

From § 2, VII follows the theorem: if for some module c of class Γ it is possible to represent it
as the least common multiple of s modules a1 , a2 , . . . , as belonging respectively to the classes
A1 , A2 , . . . , As , then the same is possible for every module of the class Γ. More generally, the
number of systems of s modules a1 , a2 , . . . , as satisfying

                 Cl a1 = A1 ,    Cl a2 = A2 ,         ...      Cl as = As ;              [a1 , a2 , . . . , as ] = c

is the same for every module c of class Γ. We denote this number by

                                               γ̄(Γ; A1 , A2 , . . . , As ).

It is a function of the classes Γ, A1 , . . . , As , symmetric in A1 , . . . , As . The question whether a
module c of class Γ can be represented as the least common multiple of s modules a1 , . . . , as
belonging respectively to the classes A1 , . . . , As is therefore exactly the question whether
γ̄(Γ; A1 , . . . , As ) > 0. In any given case this can of course be decided by a finite number
of trials. The problem, however, is whether one can state general, readily surveyable relations
whose existence among the classes Γ, A1 , . . . , As is the necessary and sufficient condition for the
nonvanishing of the function γ̄(Γ; A1 , . . . , As ).
    First one sees that γ̄(Γ; A1 , . . . , As ) can be nonzero only when Γ is divisible by each of
the classes A1 , . . . , As . Thus, if the classes A1 , . . . , As are regarded as given, their least com-
mon multiple Γ′ = [A1 , . . . , As ] is, in a sense, the minimum among the classes Γ for which
