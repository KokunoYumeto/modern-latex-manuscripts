
Let $A_1,\ldots,A_s,\Gamma,\Delta$ be arbitrary classes of degree $n$, and let
\[
 \mathfrak a^{(i)}_1,\mathfrak a^{(i)}_2,\ldots,
 \mathfrak a^{(i)}_{\psi(A_i)}\qquad (i=1,2,\ldots,s)
\]
be all the modules of $A_i$. The number of systems of $s$ modules
\begin{equation}
 \mathfrak a^{(1)}_{q_1},\mathfrak a^{(2)}_{q_2},\ldots,
 \mathfrak a^{(s)}_{q_s},
\tag{1}
\end{equation}
which respectively belong to the classes $A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_s$ is
\[
 \psi(A_1)\psi(A_2)\cdots\psi(A_s).
\]
The number of systems (1) for which the least common multiple
\begin{equation}
 [\mathfrak a^{(1)}_{q_1},\mathfrak a^{(2)}_{q_2},\ldots,
 \mathfrak a^{(s)}_{q_s}]
\tag{2}
\end{equation}
is equal to a specified module of a class $\Theta$ is represented by the function $\bar\gamma(\Theta;A_1,\ldots,A_s)$. Hence, if $\mathfrak c$ is any module of $\Gamma$, the number of systems (1) for which (2) becomes some divisor of $\mathfrak c$ belonging to the class $\Theta$ is
\[
 t(\Gamma\mid\Theta)\bar\gamma(\Theta;A_1,\ldots,A_s).
\]
Finally, to obtain the number of all systems (1) for which (2) is some divisor of $\mathfrak c$, one has only to form the sum
\[
 \sum_{\Theta}t(\Gamma\mid\Theta)\bar\gamma(\Theta;A_1,\ldots,A_s),
\]
where $\Theta$ runs through all classes of degree $n$. But the module (2) is a divisor of $\mathfrak c$ if and only if all modules in the system (1) are divisors of $\mathfrak c$. Therefore this same number is also represented by
\[
 t(\Gamma\mid A_1)t(\Gamma\mid A_2)\cdots t(\Gamma\mid A_s).
\]
We therefore obtain the relation
\[
\text{I.}\qquad
 \sum_{\Theta}t(\Gamma\mid\Theta)\bar\gamma(\Theta;A_1,\ldots,A_s)
 =t(\Gamma\mid A_1)t(\Gamma\mid A_2)\cdots t(\Gamma\mid A_s).
\]
This relation, valid for every system of classes $\Gamma,A_1,\ldots,A_s$, is fully sufficient to define the function $\bar\gamma$.

First, I shows that $\bar\gamma(\Gamma;A_1,\ldots,A_s)$ vanishes if $\Gamma$ is not a multiple of each of the classes $A_1,\ldots,A_s$. In that case the product on the right-hand side of I vanishes. If we assume the assertion already proved for all divisors of $\Gamma$ except $\Gamma$ itself, then for every $\Theta\ne\Gamma$ one factor of the product under the summation sign vanishes, while $t(\Gamma\mid\Gamma)=1$. Thus
\[
 \bar\gamma(\Gamma;A_1,\ldots,A_s)=0.
\]
It follows that, for every class $\Gamma$, relation I can be given the form
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{\substack{(\Theta,\Gamma)=\Theta\\(\Theta,\Gamma')=\Gamma'}}
 t(\Gamma\mid\Theta)\bar\gamma(\Theta;A_1,\ldots,A_s)
 =t(\Gamma\mid A_1)\cdots t(\Gamma\mid A_s),
\tag{3}
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma'$ denotes the least common multiple of the classes $A_1,\ldots,A_s$, and the summation extends over all classes $\Theta$ lying between $\Gamma$ and $\Gamma'$, including the endpoints. If $\Gamma=\Gamma'$, this gives simply
\[
 \bar\gamma(\Gamma';A_1,\ldots,A_s)=t(\Gamma'\mid A_1)\cdots t(\Gamma'\mid A_s).
\]
If, however, $\Gamma$ is a multiple of $\Gamma'$, but distinct from $\Gamma'$, we suppose that before computing $\bar\gamma(\Gamma;A_1,\ldots,A_s)$ we have computed the finitely many expressions $\bar\gamma(\Theta;A_1,\ldots,A_s)$ in which $\Theta$ lies between $\Gamma$ and $\Gamma'$ and is different from $\Gamma$. Then (3) gives
\[
\text{Ia.}\quad
 \bar\gamma(\Gamma;A_1,\ldots,A_s)
 =t(\Gamma\mid A_1)\cdots t(\Gamma\mid A_s)
 -\sum_{\substack{(\Theta,\Gamma)=\Theta\\(\Theta,\Gamma')=\Gamma'\\\Theta\ne\Gamma}}
 t(\Gamma\mid\Theta)\bar\gamma(\Theta;A_1,\ldots,A_s),
\]
and only known quantities occur on the right-hand side.

In the same way one obtains for $\bar\delta$, putting
\[
 \Delta'=(A_1,\ldots,A_s),
\]
the formulae
\[
\text{II.}\qquad
 \sum_{\Theta}v(\Theta\mid\Delta)\bar\delta(\Theta;A_1,\ldots,A_s)
 =v(A_1\mid\Delta)\cdots v(A_s\mid\Delta),
\]
\[
\text{IIa.}\quad
 \bar\delta(\Delta;A_1,\ldots,A_s)=v(A_1\mid\Delta)\cdots v(A_s\mid\Delta)
 -\sum_{\substack{(\Theta,\Delta)=\Delta\\(\Theta,\Delta')=\Theta\\\Theta\ne\Delta}}
 v(\Theta\mid\Delta)\bar\delta(\Theta;A_1,\ldots,A_s).
\]
The recursion formulae Ia and IIa show that the functions $\bar\gamma$ and $\bar\delta$ can be put together from $t$-functions and $v$-functions respectively by addition, subtraction, and multiplication. This representation is, however, substantially more complicated than the representation of $t$ and $v$ by $\psi$-functions. In the latter representation the number of representing $\psi$-functions is bounded by the degree $n$ of the classes under consideration, whereas the number of $t$- and $v$-functions used in representing $\bar\gamma(\Gamma;A_1,\ldots,A_s)$ and $\bar\delta(\Delta;A_1,\ldots,A_s)$ is finite in each individual case but has no upper bound even when $n$ and $s$ are fixed. Moreover the subtraction occurring in these formulae makes the question of the vanishing of the $\bar\gamma$- and $\bar\delta$-functions very difficult. These difficulties are removed by transforming the expressions obtained for $\bar\gamma$ and $\bar\delta$ into products of functions which are easier to survey.

\section*{§ 10. Preliminary considerations.}

1. From now on we restrict ourselves to primary classes. Such a class
\[
 A=\Cl(p^{r_1}\mid p^{r_2}\mid\cdots\mid p^{r_n})
\]
is completely determined by the system of exponents
\begin{equation}
 r_1,r_2,\ldots,r_n
\tag{1}
\end{equation}
and the prime number $p$. If only the exponent system (1) is given, we are dealing with a so-called partially determined primary class. In what follows, lower-case Greek letters always denote partially determined primary classes. If $\alpha$ is such a class and (1) is its system of exponents, we also write
\[
 \alpha=\Cl(r_1\mid r_2\mid\cdots\mid r_n).
\]
If, as in § 6,
\[
\begin{gathered}
 r_1=\cdots=r_{s_1}=e_1,
 \quad r_{s_1+1}=\cdots=r_{s_1+s_2}=e_2,
 \quad \ldots,
 r_{n-s_k+1}=\cdots=r_n=e_k,\
 e_1<e_2<\cdots<e_k,
\end{gathered}
\]
then (§ 6, III; a, b) gives
\begin{align}
\psi(\alpha)
&=p^{\sum_{i>h}s_i s_h(e_i-e_h)}
 {\vartheta(p;n)\over \vartheta(p;s_1)\vartheta(p;s_2)\cdots\vartheta(p;s_k)} \notag\\
&=p^{\sum_{i>h}s_i s_h(e_i-e_h-1)}
 g(p;n,s_1)g(p;n-s_1,s_2)\cdots
 g(p;n-s_1-\cdots-s_{k-2},s_{k-1})
\tag{2}
\end{align}
as an integral function of the indeterminate prime number $p$.

When several classes $\alpha,\beta,\ldots$ are considered simultaneously, it is understood that the indeterminate prime number $p$ is the same for all of them. Thus expressions such as "$\alpha$ is divisible by $\beta$" have an unambiguous meaning. Suppose that we have $s$ classes
\[
 \alpha_i=\Cl(a_{i1}\mid a_{i2}\mid\cdots\mid a_{in})\qquad (i=1,\ldots,s),
\]
and let
\[
 \gamma'=[\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s]=\Cl(c'_1\mid\cdots\mid c'_n)
\]
be their least common multiple and
\[
 \delta'=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s)=\Cl(d'_1\mid\cdots\mid d'_n)
\]
their greatest common divisor. Then $c'_k$ is the greatest, and $d'_k$ the least, of the numbers $a_{1k},\ldots,a_{sk}$. The definition given in § 7 also immediately shows how to determine the limiting multiple
\[
 \gamma''=[|\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s|]
\]
and the limiting divisor
\[
 \delta''=(|\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s|)
\]
of the classes $\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s$: order the $ns$ exponents of $\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s$ by size, so that they form a non-decreasing sequence. If $d''_1,
\ldots,d''_n$ are the $n$ smallest and $c''_1,
\ldots,c''_n$ the $n$ largest, then
\[
 \gamma''=\Cl(c''_1\mid\cdots\mid c''_n),\qquad
 \delta''=\Cl(d''_1\mid\cdots\mid d''_n).
\]
We express the fact that $\alpha$ is divisible by $\beta$ by the inequality $\alpha\ge\beta$ or $\beta\le\alpha$; the notations $\alpha>\beta$ and $\beta<\alpha$ exclude equality.

If $\alpha\ge\beta$ is not satisfied, then $t(\alpha\mid\beta)$ and $v(\alpha\mid\beta)$ vanish identically, i.e. for every $p$. Otherwise their meaning is given by § 8, III, IV, and the functions $\bar\gamma(\gamma;\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s)$ and $\bar\delta(\delta;\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s)$ are similarly given by § 9, Ia, IIa. All these functions are, as is easy to see, integral functions of $p$. The presentation becomes clearer if we regard $p$ as an arbitrarily variable real quantity. We indicate this externally by replacing the letter $p$ by $x$.

2. Thus now
\[
 \psi(\alpha)=x^{\sum_{i>h}s_i s_h(e_i-e_h)}
 {\vartheta(x;n)\over \vartheta(x;s_1)\cdots\vartheta(x;s_k)}
 =x^{\sum_{i>h}s_i s_h(e_i-e_h-1)}
 g(x;n,s_1)\cdots g(x;n-s_1-\cdots-s_{k-2},s_{k-1}),
\]
where $s_1,
\ldots,s_k,e_1,
\ldots,e_k$ have their earlier meanings. Thus, if the exponents of $\alpha$ are not numerically given, one must still know the numbers $s_1,
\ldots,s_k$ in order to form $\psi(\alpha)$; that is, one must know whether two successive exponents are equal or not. Even greater inconveniences arise for $t$ and $v$, and the treatment of $\bar\gamma$ and $\bar\delta$ becomes completely opaque. These difficulties can be removed by replacing the exponents by other quantities, which we introduce under the name "indices".

By the "sequence of indices" of a class $\alpha$ we mean an infinite sequence of integers
\[
 a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots,
\]
whose $h$th element $a_h$ states how many exponents of $\alpha$ are smaller than $h$. For example, to the classes
\[
 \Cl(2\mid3\mid3\mid5\mid8),\qquad \Cl(0\mid4\mid7)
\]
there correspond the sequences
\[
 0,0,1,3,3,4,4,4,5,5,5,\ldots;
 \qquad
 1,1,1,1,2,2,2,3,3,\ldots.
\]
The following assertions are easy to verify.

1) The sequence of indices of a class of degree $n$ is an infinite non-decreasing sequence of non-negative integers which rises to the number $n$.

2) Conversely, every sequence having the properties stated in 1) is the sequence of indices of a certain class $\alpha$ of degree $n$.

3) By definition, the $h$th index of $\alpha$ gives the number of exponents $<h$.

4) Conversely, the $h$th exponent of $\alpha$ gives the number of indices $\le h$.

The sequence of indices $a_1,a_2,\ldots$ of a class may also be continued in the negative direction. One then sets $a_0=0$, $a_{-1}=0$, $a_{-2}=0,
\ldots$.

We add to assertions 1)--4) the following, whose proof is also easy.

5) If $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are classes of degree $n$, with indices $a_h$ and $b_h$, then $\alpha\ge\beta$ holds if and only if $a_h\le b_h$ for every $h$.

Let now $\alpha_1,
\alpha_2,
\ldots,
\alpha_s$ be classes of degree $n$, and put
\[
 [\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\ldots,\alpha_s]=\gamma',\quad
 (\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\ldots,\alpha_s)=\delta',
\]
\[
 [|\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\ldots,\alpha_s|]=\gamma'',\quad
 (|\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\ldots,\alpha_s|)=\delta''.
\]
Let $a_h^{(i)}$ $(i=1,2,\ldots,s)$, $c'_h,d'_h,c''_h,d''_h$ denote the indices of the classes $\alpha_i$, $\gamma'$, $\delta'$, $\gamma''$, $\delta''$. From 5) it follows that:

6) The $h$th index $c'_h$ of the least common multiple $\gamma'$ of $\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s$ is the least, and the $h$th index $d'_h$ of the greatest common divisor $\delta'$ is the greatest, among the $h$th indices $a_h^{(1)},\ldots,a_h^{(s)}$.

For the sequences of indices of the limiting multiple and limiting divisor one obtains:

7) The $h$th index $c''_h$ of the limiting multiple $\gamma''$ is the greater of the two numbers
\[
 0\quad\hbox{and}\quad n-\sum_{i=1}^s(n-a_h^{(i)})=\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^s a_h^{(i)}\biggr)-(s-1)n,
\]
and the $h$th index $d''_h$ of their limiting divisor $\delta''$ is the smaller of
\[
 n\quad\hbox{and}\quad \sum_{i=1}^s a_h^{(i)}.
\]
Here $a_h^{(i)}$ denotes the indices of $\alpha_i$.

From 4) one easily obtains:

8) Among other relations, the indices $a_h$ and the exponents $\bar a_1,
\ldots,\bar a_n$ of a class $\alpha$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
 (1-n)\bar a_1+(3-n)\bar a_2+(5-n)\bar a_3+\cdots+(n-1)\bar a_n
 =\sum_h a_h(n-a_h).
\tag{3}
\end{equation}
No bounds for the summation need be stated, since the relation is correct when $h$ runs through all integers.

3. In § 6 the function $g(x;n,m)$ was introduced and defined for all integral values of $n$ and $m$. We collect here several formulae and facts about this function which will be needed later.
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
1)&\displaystyle g(x;n,m)={ (1-x^n)(1-x^{n-1})\cdots(1-x^{n-m+1})\over(1-x)(1-x^2)\cdots(1-x^m)}\quad(m>0),\\[1.0ex]
2)&g(x;n,0)=1,\\
3)&g(x;n,m)=0\quad(m<0),\\
4)&g(x;0,m)=0\quad(m\ne0),\qquad g(x;0,0)=1,\\
5)&g(x;n,m)=g(x;n-1,m)+x^{n-m}g(x;n-1,m-1),\\
6)&g(x;n,m)=g(x;n-1,m-1)+x^m g(x;n-1,m),\\
7)&g(x;n,m)=x^{m(n-m)}g(1/x;n,m),\\
8)&g(x;s,q)g(x;s-q,r)=g(x;s,r)g(x;s-r,q),\\
9)&g(x;-n,m)=(-1)^m x^{-mn-{m(m-1)\over2}}g(x;n+m-1,m),\\
10)&g(x;n,m)=g(x;n,n-m)\quad(n\ge0),\\[0.5ex]
11)&\displaystyle g(1/x;n,m)={\vartheta(x;n)\over\vartheta(x;m)\vartheta(x;n-m)}\quad(n\ge m\ge0).
\end{array}
\]
12) In all cases the expansion of $g$ in powers of $x$ contains only finitely many positive and negative powers, and the coefficients are non-negative integers.

13) For $n\ge0$, $g(x;n,m)$ is an integral function of $x$; for $n>0$, $m\ge0$, it does not vanish identically.

\section*{§ 11. The functions $\psi(\alpha)$, $t(\alpha\mid\beta)$, $v(\alpha\mid\beta)$ represented with the aid of the indices.}

If the class
\[
 \alpha=\Cl(\bar a_1\mid\bar a_2\mid\cdots\mid\bar a_n)
\]
has indices $a_h$, then $a_h-a_{h-1}$ is the number of exponents equal to $h-1$. Taking account of $\vartheta(x;0)=1$, § 6, III gives
\begin{equation}
 \psi(\alpha)=x^{(1-n)\bar a_1+(3-n)\bar a_2+\cdots+(n-1)\bar a_n}
 {\vartheta(x;n)\over\prod_h\vartheta(x;a_h-a_{h-1})}.
\tag{1}
\end{equation}
But by § 10, 2, 8),
\[
 (1-n)\bar a_1+(3-n)\bar a_2+\cdots+(n-1)\bar a_n=\sum_h(n-a_h)a_h,
\]
and
\begin{align*}
 {\vartheta(x;n)\over\prod_h\vartheta(x;a_h-a_{h-1})}
 &=\prod_h {\vartheta(x;n-a_{h-1})\over\vartheta(x;n-a_h)\vartheta(x;a_h-a_{h-1})}\\
 &=\prod_h g(1/x;n-a_{h-1},n-a_h)\\
 &=\prod_h g(1/x;a_h,a_{h-1}).
\end{align*}
Therefore, using § 10, 3, 7),
\begin{align*}
\text{I.}\quad
\psi(\alpha)&=x^{\sum_h(n-a_h)a_h}\prod_h g(1/x;n-a_{h-1},n-a_h)\\
&=x^{\sum_h(n-a_h)a_h}\prod_h g(1/x;a_h,a_{h-1})\\
&=x^{\sum_h(n-a_h)a_{h-1}}\prod_h g(x;n-a_{h-1},n-a_h)\\
&=x^{\sum_h(n-a_h)a_{h-1}}\prod_h g(x;a_h,a_{h-1}).
\end{align*}

Suppose that of the classes
\[
 \alpha=\Cl(a'_1\mid\cdots\mid a'_n),\qquad
 \beta=\Cl(b'_1\mid\cdots\mid b'_n)=\beta_0,
\]
the first is divisible by the second. Then (§ 8, III)
\begin{equation}
 t(\alpha\mid\beta)=
 {\psi(\beta)\psi(\beta_1)\cdots\psi(\beta_{n-2})
  \over
  \psi(\bar\beta)\psi(\bar\beta_1)\cdots\psi(\bar\beta_{n-2})}
 = {\psi(\beta)\psi(\beta_1)\cdots\psi(\beta_{n-1})
  \over
  \psi(\bar\beta)\psi(\bar\beta_1)\cdots\psi(\bar\beta_{n-1})}.
\tag{2}
\end{equation}
The meaning of the classes $\bar\beta=\bar\beta_0,\beta_i,\bar\beta_i$ $(i=1,
\ldots,n-1)$ is given in § 8, (5). Denote the indices of the classes $\alpha,\beta=\beta_0,\bar\beta=\bar\beta_0,\beta_i,\bar\beta_i$ by
\[
 a_h,
 \quad b_h=b_h^{(0)},\quad \bar b_h=\bar b_h^{(0)},
 \quad b_h^{(i)},\quad \bar b_h^{(i)}\qquad(i=1,\ldots,n-1).
\]
Since $\beta_i$ and $\bar\beta_i$ $(i=0,
\ldots,n-1)$ have degree $n-i$, one obtains
\begin{align}
 t(\alpha\mid\beta)
&=x^{\sum_h\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\{(n-i-b_h^{(i)})b_h^{(i)}-(n-i-\bar b_h^{(i)})\bar b_h^{(i)}\}}
 \prod_h\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}
 {g(1/x;b_h^{(i)},b_{h-1}^{(i)})\over
 g(1/x;\bar b_h^{(i)},\bar b_{h-1}^{(i)})}.
\tag{3}
\end{align}
We now transform this expression so that it contains only the indices of $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

For $a_h<i$, the exponent of $\alpha$ is smaller than $h$, and hence, by § 8, (5),
\[
 b_h^{(i)}=0,
 \qquad \bar b_h^{(i)}=0.
\]
For $a_h=i$, one has $a'_i<h\le a'_{i+1}$, and therefore
\[
 \bar b_h^{(i)}=0,
 \qquad b_h^{(i)}=b_h-i=b_h-a_h.
\]
For $a_h>i$, one has $a'_{i+1}<h$, and therefore
\[
 b_h^{(i)}=\bar b_h^{(i)}=b_h-i.
\]
It follows that
\begin{equation}
 \sum_h\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\{(n-i-b_h^{(i)})b_h^{(i)}-(n-i-\bar b_h^{(i)})\bar b_h^{(i)}\}
 =\sum_h(n-b_h)(b_h-a_h),
\tag{4}
\end{equation}
and also
\[
\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}
 {g(1/x;b_h^{(i)},b_{h-1}^{(i)})\over
 g(1/x;\bar b_h^{(i)},\bar b_{h-1}^{(i)})}
 =g(1/x;b_h-a_{h-1},b_{h-1}-a_{h-1}).
\]
Consequently
\begin{align*}
\text{II.}\quad
 t(\alpha\mid\beta)
 &=x^{\sum_h(n-b_h)(b_h-a_h)}
 \prod_h g(1/x;b_h-a_{h-1},b_{h-1}-a_{h-1})\\
 &=x^{\sum_h(n-b_h)(b_{h-1}-a_{h-1})}
 \prod_h g(x;b_h-a_{h-1},b_{h-1}-a_{h-1})\\
 &=x^{\sum_h(n-b_h)(b_h-a_h)}
 \prod_h g(1/x;b_h-a_{h-1},b_h-b_{h-1})\\
 &=x^{\sum_h(n-b_h)(b_{h-1}-a_{h-1})}
 \prod_h g(x;b_h-a_{h-1},b_h-b_{h-1}).
\end{align*}
By § 7, I,
\[
 v(\alpha\mid\beta)={\psi(\alpha)\over\psi(\beta)}t(\alpha\mid\beta).
\]
Using the formulae for $\psi$, this gives
\begin{align*}
\text{III.}\quad
 v(\alpha\mid\beta)&=x^{\sum_h(b_h-a_h)a_h}
 \prod_h g(1/x;b_h-a_{h-1},b_h-a_h)\\
 &=x^{\sum_h(b_h-a_h)a_{h-1}}
 \prod_h g(x;b_h-a_{h-1},b_h-a_h)\\
 &=x^{\sum_h(b_h-a_h)a_h}
 \prod_h g(1/x;b_h-a_{h-1},a_h-a_{h-1})\\
 &=x^{\sum_h(b_h-a_h)a_{h-1}}
 \prod_h g(x;b_h-a_{h-1},a_h-a_{h-1}).
\end{align*}
All these expressions were derived under the assumption $\alpha\ge\beta$. If that assumption is not fulfilled, one can choose $h$ so that $b_{h-1}<a_{h-1}\le b_h$; then the four expressions in II vanish identically, and the same holds for the four expressions in III. Since in this case in fact $t(\alpha\mid\beta)=0$ and $v(\alpha\mid\beta)=0$, the formulae hold without restriction.

\section*{§ 12. The degrees of the functions $\psi$, $t$, $v$, $\bar\gamma$, $\bar\delta$.}

Since henceforth it will nowhere be necessary to consider classes of different degrees side by side, let the degree of all occurring classes be denoted by $n$.

The formulae § 11, I show that $\psi(\alpha)$ is an integral function of $x$ with integral coefficients, that the coefficient of the highest power of $x$ is 1, and that the degree of $\psi(\alpha)$ is
\begin{equation}
 \sum_h(n-a_h)a_h,
\tag{1}
\end{equation}
where $a_h$ are the indices of $\alpha$. If $\beta$ is a divisor of $\alpha$, then (§ 11, II, III) $t(\alpha\mid\beta)$ and $v(\alpha\mid\beta)$ are likewise integral functions of $x$ with integral coefficients, again with leading coefficient 1. Their degrees are respectively
\begin{equation}
 \sum_h(n-b_h)(b_h-a_h),
\tag{2}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \sum_h(b_h-a_h)a_h,
\tag{3}
\end{equation}
where $b_h$ denotes the indices of $\beta$.

For $\bar\gamma(\gamma;\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s)$ and $\bar\delta(\delta;\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s)$ we have, by § 9, IIa, IIIa,
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
 \bar\gamma(\gamma;\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s)
 &=t(\gamma\mid\alpha_1)\cdots t(\gamma\mid\alpha_s)
 -\sum_{\gamma'\le\mu<\gamma}t(\gamma\mid\mu)\bar\gamma(\mu;\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s),\\
 \bar\delta(\delta;\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s)
 &=v(\alpha_1\mid\delta)\cdots v(\alpha_s\mid\delta)
 -\sum_{\delta'\ge\mu>\delta}v(\mu\mid\delta)\bar\delta(\mu;\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s).
\end{aligned}
\right.
\tag{4}
\end{equation}
Here $\gamma'=[\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s]$, $\delta'=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s)$, and the summation is to be extended over all classes $\mu$ satisfying the conditions written under the summation signs. From (4) and the properties of $t$ and $v$, one sees that $\bar\gamma$ and $\bar\delta$ are integral functions of $x$ with integral coefficients, and also that $\bar\gamma$ vanishes when $\gamma\ge\gamma'$ is not satisfied and $\bar\delta$ vanishes when $\delta\le\delta'$ is not satisfied.

We first examine the degree of $\bar\delta$ more closely and assume $\delta\le\delta'$. The coefficient of the highest power of $x$ cannot be negative in any $\bar\delta$-function, since for a prime value of $x$ the function counts a number. From (4), the degree of $\bar\delta(\delta;\alpha_1,\ldots,
\alpha_s)$ is therefore at most the degree of $v(\alpha_1\mid\delta)\cdots v(\alpha_s\mid\delta)$, namely
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{i=1}^s\sum_h(d_h-a_h^{(i)})a_h^{(i)},
\tag{5}
\end{equation}
where $a_h^{(i)}$ and $d_h$ are the indices of $\alpha_i$ and $\delta$. Only two cases are possible. Either the degrees of the functions
\begin{equation}
 v(\mu\mid\delta)\bar\delta(\mu;\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s)
\tag{6}
\end{equation}
for $\delta'\ge\mu>\delta$ are all smaller than (5); then $\bar\delta$ reaches the stated maximal degree and the leading coefficient is 1. Or one of the functions (6) rises to degree (5), in which case the degree of $\bar\delta$ falls below this expression.

We prove that $\bar\delta(\delta;\alpha_1,\ldots,
\alpha_s)$ reaches the maximal degree (5) if and only if the class $\delta$, already assumed to be a divisor of $\delta'$, is a multiple of the limiting divisor $\delta''$ of $\alpha_1,
\ldots,
\alpha_s$. Let $m_h$ denote the indices of the variable class $\mu$. The maximum for the degree of
\[
 v(\mu\mid\delta)\bar\delta(\mu;\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s)
\]
is
\begin{align}
&\sum_h(d_h-m_h)m_h+\sum_{i=1}^s\sum_h(m_h-a_h^{(i)})a_h^{(i)} \notag\\
&\qquad =\sum_{i=1}^s\sum_h(d_h-a_h^{(i)})a_h^{(i)}
 -\sum_h\biggl\{(d_h-m_h)\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^s a_h^{(i)}-m_h\biggr)\biggr\}.
\tag{7}
\end{align}
Moreover (§ 10, 2, 7) and 5)) $\delta$ is a multiple of $\delta''$ if and only if, for every $h$,
\begin{equation}
 d_h\le\sum_{i=1}^s a_h^{(i)}.
\tag{8}
\end{equation}
If this inequality is always fulfilled, then from $\mu>\delta$ no term of
\begin{equation}
 \sum_h\biggl\{(d_h-m_h)\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^s a_h^{(i)}-m_h\biggr)\biggr\}
\tag{9}
\end{equation}
can be negative. At least one term must be positive, because equality of $\mu$ and $\delta$ is excluded. Hence the expression (7) is, for every $\mu$, smaller than (5).

Conversely, if the conditions (8) are not all fulfilled, put
\[
 [\delta,\delta'']=\mu'.
\]
The index $m'_h$ of $\mu'$ is the smaller of $\sum_i a_h^{(i)}$ and $d_h$, and the class $\mu'$ occurs among the classes $\mu$. By the preceding argument, $\bar\delta(\mu';\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s)$ reaches the degree
\[
 \sum_{i=1}^s\sum_h(m'_h-a_h^{(i)})a_h^{(i)},
\]
and the function
\[
 v(\mu'\mid\delta)\bar\delta(\mu';\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s)
\]
rises to degree
\[
 \sum_{i=1}^s\sum_h(d_h-a_h^{(i)})a_h^{(i)}
 -\sum_h\biggl\{(d_h-m'_h)\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^s a_h^{(i)}-m'_h\biggr)\biggr\}.
\]
Since the product
\[
 (d_h-m'_h)\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^s a_h^{(i)}-m'_h\biggr)
\]
vanishes for every $h$, the degree of
\[
 v(\mu'\mid\delta)\bar\delta(\mu';\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s)
\]
is precisely (5).

In entirely similar fashion, for $\bar\gamma(\gamma;\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s)$ one obtains the following result. If $\gamma$ is a multiple of $\gamma'=[\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s]$, then $\bar\gamma(\gamma;\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s)$ has degree at most
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{i=1}^s\sum_h(n-a_h^{(i)})(a_h^{(i)}-c_h),
\tag{10}
\end{equation}
where $c_h$ denotes the indices of $\gamma$. The function reaches this degree if $\gamma$ is a divisor of $\gamma''=[|\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s|]$, and the leading coefficient is then 1; otherwise the maximal degree is not reached.

Since $\bar\gamma$ and $\bar\delta$ do not vanish identically in the cases where the stated maximal degree is reached, they must remain positive as soon as $x$ exceeds a certain positive bound. It will later be shown that this already occurs for $x>1$, so that the functions do not vanish for any prime value of $x$. It will also emerge that in all other cases, where so far it has only been shown that the maximal degree is not reached, the functions $\bar\gamma$ and $\bar\delta$ vanish identically.

This will complete the proof of Theorem V of § 7. In order to reach that point, however, it is not enough to consider the degrees of $\bar\gamma$ and $\bar\delta$. We must examine their nature more closely, first considering the functions from which, as will be shown later, $\bar\gamma$ and $\bar\delta$ can be constructed by multiplication, up to the insertion of a factor that is only a power of $x$, in the same way that $\psi$, $t$, and $v$ were expressed by means of the $g$-functions.

\section*{§ 13. The $\omega$-functions.}

1. We denote by
\[
 \omega(x\mid k\mid e\mid q_1,r_1;q_2,r_2;\ldots;q_s,r_s)
\]
the sum
\begin{equation}
 \sum_m(-1)^m x^{{m(m-1)\over2}-km}
 g(x;e,m)g(x;q_1-m,r_1)g(x;q_2-m,r_2)\cdots g(x;q_s-m,r_s).
\tag{1}
\end{equation}
The quantity $x$ is the argument, $k$ the first parameter, $e$ the second parameter, and $q_1,r_1$ form the first parameter pair, $q_2,r_2$ the second, and so on. All parameters are to be integers, and the sum extends over all integers $m$. If $e\ge0$, to which case we may restrict ourselves here, only finitely many terms of the sum are non-zero. The $\omega$-function is then a rational function of $x$, which can possibly become infinite only for $x=0$ and $x=\infty$.

The function (1) is to be called a proper $\omega$-function if no parameter is negative and moreover
\[
 q_i\ge \begin{cases}e,\\ r_i,
 \end{cases}\qquad(i=1,\ldots,s)
\]
holds. In this case
\[
 k+r_1+\cdots+r_s-e
\]
is called the critical number of the $\omega$-function.

2. We first treat the $\omega$-function without parameter pairs:
\[
 \omega(x\mid k\mid e)=\sum_m(-1)^m x^{{m(m-1)\over2}-km}g(x;e,m).
\]
We have
\begin{equation}
 \omega(x\mid k\mid0)=1,
\tag{2}
\end{equation}
and for $e>0$, using
\[
 g(x;e,m)=g(x;e-1,m-1)+x^m g(x;e-1,m),
\]
one obtains
\begin{align}
\omega(x\mid k\mid e)
&=\sum_m(-1)^m x^{{m(m-1)\over2}-km}g(x;e-1,m-1)\notag\\
&\quad+\sum_m(-1)^m x^{{m(m-1)\over2}-(k-1)m}g(x;e-1,m).
\tag{3}
\end{align}
Since
\[
 {m(m-1)\over2}-km={(m-1)(m-2)\over2}-(k-1)(m-1)-k,
\]
it follows that
\begin{equation}
 \omega(x\mid k\mid e)=(1-x^{-k})\omega(x\mid k-1\mid e-1),
\tag{4}
\end{equation}
and therefore, using (2),
\begin{equation}
 \omega(x\mid k\mid e)=(1-x^{-k})(1-x^{-k+1})\cdots(1-x^{-k+e-1}).
\tag{5}
\end{equation}
If this $\omega$-function is proper, so that $k\ge0$ and $e\ge0$, then $k-e$ is its critical number. From (5) one sees that the function vanishes identically when the critical number is negative, while otherwise it has a positive non-zero value for every $x>1$.

3. For an $\omega$-function with $s$ parameter pairs, assuming only that its second parameter $e$ is not negative, application of
\[
 g(x;q_s-m,r_s)=g(x;q_s-1-m,r_s-1)+x^{r_s}g(x;q_s-1-m,r_s)
\]
gives
\begin{align}
&\omega(x\mid k\mid e\mid q_1,r_1;\ldots;q_s,r_s)\notag\\
&\quad=x^{r_s}\omega(x\mid k\mid e\mid q_1,r_1;\ldots;q_{s-1},r_{s-1};q_s-1,r_s)\notag\\
&\qquad+\omega(x\mid k\mid e\mid q_1,r_1;\ldots;q_{s-1},r_{s-1};q_s-1,r_s-1),
\tag{6}
\end{align}
and hence, more generally,
\begin{align}
&\omega(x\mid k\mid e\mid q_1,r_1;\ldots;q_s,r_s)\notag\\
&\quad=\sum_{l=0}^t C'_l\,
 \omega(x\mid k\mid e\mid q_1,r_1;\ldots;q_{s-1},r_{s-1};q_s-t,r_s-l),
\tag{7}
\end{align}
where $t$ is any non-negative integer. By induction one finds
\[
 C'_l=x^{l(r_s-t)}g(x;t,l),
\]
and what matters here is that this coefficient is positive for every positive value of $x$.

4. In the special case $q_s=e$, with $e\ge0$, the identity
\[
 g(x;e,m)g(x;e-m,r_s)=g(x;e,r_s)g(x;e-r_s,m)
\]
gives
\begin{align}
&\omega(x\mid k\mid e\mid q_1,r_1;\ldots;q_{s-1},r_{s-1};e,r_s)\notag\\
&\quad=g(x;e,r_s)\omega(x\mid k\mid e-r_s\mid q_1,r_1;\ldots;q_{s-1},r_{s-1}).
\tag{8}
\end{align}
The $\omega$-function on the right contains one fewer parameter pair. The factor $g(x;e,r_s)$ vanishes if $r_s<0$ or $r_s>e$; otherwise, for positive $x$, it has a positive value.

5. Let now
\[
 \omega(x\mid k\mid e\mid q_1,r_1;\ldots;q_s,r_s)
\]
be a proper $\omega$-function. Then $q_s-e\ge0$, and applying (7) with $t=q_s-e$ and then (8) gives
\begin{align}
&\omega(x\mid k\mid e\mid q_1,r_1;\ldots;q_s,r_s)\notag\\
&=\sum_{\substack{r_s+e-q_s\le h\le r_s\\0\le h\le e}}
 C_h\,\omega(x\mid k\mid e-h\mid q_1,r_1;\ldots;q_{s-1},r_{s-1}).
\tag{10}
\end{align}
The coefficients $C_h$ are positive for every positive $x$. The $\omega$-functions on the right are proper and contain only $s-1$ parameter pairs. Thus every proper $\omega$-function can be expressed linearly and homogeneously in proper $\omega$-functions without parameter pairs, with coefficients positive for positive $x$.

The critical number of the function considered is $k+r_1+\cdots+r_s-e$, while that of
\[
 \omega(x\mid k\mid e-h\mid q_1,r_1;\ldots;q_{s-1},r_{s-1})
\]
is
\[
 k+r_1+\cdots+r_{s-1}+h-e,
\]
which is never larger than $k+r_1+\cdots+r_s-e$ for the values of $h$ under consideration. Hence a proper $\omega$-function with negative critical number is a homogeneous linear combination of such functions without parameter pairs and therefore vanishes.

If the critical number $k+r_1+\cdots+r_s-e$ is non-negative, then at least one term on the right of (10) remains whose critical number is not negative, namely the term obtained by taking $h$ equal to the smaller of $e$ and $r_s$. Hence the given $\omega$-function can be expressed as a homogeneous linear combination of proper $\omega$-functions without parameter pairs of non-negative critical number, and at least one such function actually occurs. Since the coefficients are positive for positive $x$, and the $\omega$-functions are positive for $x>1$, the original $\omega$-function is also positive for $x>1$.

We have reached the important conclusion:

I. A proper $\omega$-function whose critical number is $<0$ vanishes identically.

II. A proper $\omega$-function whose critical number is $\ge0$ has a positive value for $x>1$.

\section*{§ 14. The functions $\bar t$ and $\bar v$.}

For $e>0$,
\begin{equation}
 0=\omega(x\mid0\mid e)=\sum_m(-1)^m x^{m(m-1)/2}g(x;e,m).
\tag{1}
\end{equation}
If $a,b,c$ are integers satisfying
\begin{equation}
 0\le c\le a<b,
\tag{2}
\end{equation}
then replacing $e$ by $b-a$ and $m$ by $b-m$ in (1), multiplying by
\[
 x^{c(b-a)}g(x;b-c,a-c),
\]
and using
\begin{align*}
 g(x;b-a,b-m)g(x;b-c,a-c)
 &=g(x;m-c,a-c)g(x;b-c,b-m)\\
 &=g(x;m-c,m-a)g(x;b-c,b-m),
\end{align*}
one obtains
\begin{equation}
 \sum_m(-1)^{b-m}x^{(b-m)(b-m-1)/2+c(b-a)}
 g(x;m-c,m-a)g(x;b-c,b-m)=0.
\tag{3}
\end{equation}
Let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be two classes with indices $a_h,b_h$. Put
\begin{align}
F_h&=\sum_{m_h}(-1)^{b_h-m_h}
 x^{(b_h-m_h)(b_h-m_h-1)/2+(m_h-a_h)a_{h-1}+(b_h-m_h)b_{h-1}}\notag\\
&\quad\cdot g(x;m_h-a_{h-1},m_h-a_h)g(x;b_h-b_{h-1},b_h-m_h).
\tag{4}
\end{align}
For each $h$ this sum has only finitely many non-zero terms, and $F_h=1$ if $h\le0$ or if $h$ is larger than all exponents of $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

The summation may be restricted to values of $m_h$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
 b_h\ge m_h\ge\begin{cases}b_{h-1},\\ a_h,
 \end{cases}
\tag{5}
\end{equation}
so $F_h$ vanishes when $a_h>b_h$. It also vanishes when $a_{h-1}=b_{h-1}$ and at the same time $a_h<b_h$. In contrast, if $a_{h-1}=b_{h-1}$ and $a_h=b_h$, then $F_h=1$. Consequently
\[
 \prod_hF_h
\]
has value 1 or 0 according as $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are equal or different. In the special case where the number $s$ of classes appearing in $\bar\gamma(\gamma;\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s)$ or $\bar\delta(\delta;\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s)$ is reduced to 1, the definitions of § 7 give exactly the same alternatives. Thus
\begin{equation}
 \prod_hF_h=\bar\delta(\alpha;\beta).
\tag{6}
\end{equation}
Expanding the product gives
\begin{align}
\prod_hF_h
&=\sum_{\ldots,m_0,m_1,\ldots}
\biggl\{(-1)^{\sum_h(b_h-m_h)}
 x^{\sum_h{(b_h-m_h)(b_h-m_h-1)\over2}+\sum_h(b_h-m_h)b_{h-1}}
 \prod_h g(x;b_h-b_{h-1},b_h-m_h)\notag\\
&\qquad\qquad\cdot
 x^{\sum_h(m_h-a_h)a_{h-1}}
 \prod_h g(x;m_h-a_{h-1},m_h-a_h)\biggr\}.
\tag{7}
\end{align}
It suffices to sum over sequences $m_h$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
 b_h\ge m_h\ge a_h,
 \qquad m_h\ge m_{h-1},
\tag{8}
\end{equation}
or even over all sequences satisfying
\begin{equation}
 m_h\ge m_{h-1}\ge0,
 \qquad \lim_{h=\infty}m_h=n.
\tag{9}
\end{equation}
The latter conditions say exactly that the $m_h$ are the indices of a class $\mu$ of degree $n$, while (8) also says that
\[
 \alpha\ge\mu\ge\beta.
\]
Under this assumption the second factor in (7) is $v(\alpha\mid\mu)$, while the first defines a function of $\beta$ and $\mu$, denoted $\bar v(\mu\mid\beta)$:
\begin{equation}
 \bar v(\mu\mid\beta)=(-1)^{\sum_h(b_h-m_h)}
 \prod_h x^{(b_h-m_h)(b_h-m_h-1)/2+(b_h-m_h)b_{h-1}}
 g(x;b_h-b_{h-1},b_h-m_h).
\tag{10}
\end{equation}
Thus (6) becomes
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{\mu}v(\alpha\mid\mu)\bar v(\mu\mid\beta)=\bar\delta(\alpha;\beta).
\tag{11}
\end{equation}
From this relation, by induction over the classes below $\alpha$, one obtains the inverse relation
\begin{equation}
 \sum_\mu\bar v(\alpha\mid\mu)v(\mu\mid\beta)=\bar\delta(\alpha;\beta).
\tag{14}
\end{equation}
Indeed $\bar v(\alpha\mid\beta)$ is determined by
\begin{equation}
 \bar v(\alpha\mid\beta)=\bar\delta(\alpha;\beta)
 -\sum_{\mu<\alpha}v(\alpha\mid\mu)\bar v(\mu\mid\beta),
\tag{13}
\end{equation}
and the cases $\alpha\not\ge\beta$ and $\alpha=\beta$ give $\bar v(\alpha\mid\beta)=0$ and $1$, respectively. Subtracting the already known lower-class terms proves (14) in general.

For
\begin{equation}
 \bar t(\mu\mid\beta)={\psi(\beta)\over\psi(\mu)}\bar v(\mu\mid\beta)
\tag{16}
\end{equation}
one obtains, using (11) and (14),
\begin{equation}
 \sum_\mu t(\alpha\mid\mu)\bar t(\mu\mid\beta)=\bar\gamma(\alpha;\beta),
\tag{17}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \sum_\mu\bar t(\alpha\mid\mu)t(\mu\mid\beta)=\bar\gamma(\alpha;\beta).
\tag{18}
\end{equation}
Direct calculation from (16) gives
\begin{equation}
 \bar t(\mu\mid\beta)=(-1)^{\sum_h(b_h-m_h)}
 \prod_h x^{(b_h-m_h)(b_h-m_h-1)/2+(n-m_h)(b_{h-1}-m_{h-1})}
 g(x;m_h-m_{h-1},b_{h-1}-m_{h-1}).
\tag{19}
\end{equation}
By § 9,
\begin{equation}
 \sum_\mu t(\nu\mid\mu)\bar\gamma(\mu;\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s)
 =t(\nu\mid\alpha_1)t(\nu\mid\alpha_2)\cdots t(\nu\mid\alpha_s),
\tag{20}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \sum_\mu v(\mu\mid\lambda)\bar\delta(\mu;\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s)
 =v(\alpha_1\mid\lambda)v(\alpha_2\mid\lambda)\cdots v(\alpha_s\mid\lambda).
\tag{21}
\end{equation}
Multiplying by $\bar t(\gamma\mid\nu)$ and $\bar v(\lambda\mid\delta)$ respectively, and summing over $\nu$ and $\lambda$, gives
\begin{equation}
 \bar\gamma(\gamma;\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s)=\sum_\nu \bar t(\gamma\mid\nu)t(\nu\mid\alpha_1)\cdots t(\nu\mid\alpha_s),
\tag{22}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \bar\delta(\delta;\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s)=\sum_\lambda \bar v(\lambda\mid\delta)v(\alpha_1\mid\lambda)\cdots v(\alpha_s\mid\lambda).
\tag{23}
\end{equation}

\section*{§ 15. Representation of $\bar\gamma$ and $\bar\delta$ by $\omega$-functions.}

The last two equations lead directly to representations of $\bar\gamma(\gamma;\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s)$ and $\bar\delta(\delta;\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s)$ by $\omega$-functions. Let $a_h^{(i)},c_h,d_h$ denote the indices of $\alpha_i$ $(i=1,
\ldots,s)$, $\gamma$, and $\delta$.

We discuss $\bar\delta$ somewhat more fully. Substitution of the formulae for $v$ and $\bar v$ gives
\begin{align}
&\bar v(\mu\mid\delta)v(\alpha_1\mid\mu)\cdots v(\alpha_s\mid\mu)\notag\\
&=\prod_h\biggl\{(-1)^{d_h-m_h}
 x^{(d_h-m_h)(d_h-m_h-1)/2+(d_h-m_h)d_{h-1}+(m_h-a_h^{(1)})a_{h-1}^{(1)}+\cdots+(m_h-a_h^{(s)})a_{h-1}^{(s)}}
\notag\\
&\qquad\cdot g(x;d_h-d_{h-1},d_h-m_h)g(x;m_h-a_{h-1}^{(1)},a_h^{(1)}-a_{h-1}^{(1)})
\cdots g(x;m_h-a_{h-1}^{(s)},a_h^{(s)}-a_{h-1}^{(s)})\biggr\}.
\tag{1}
\end{align}
Here the $m_h$ are the indices of $\mu$. To obtain $\bar\delta(\delta;\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s)$ one sums all such products over the possible sequences $m_h$. Since any sequence which is not the sequence of indices of a class of degree $n$ gives a vanishing product, that restriction may be dropped. Thus
\begin{align}
&\bar\delta(\delta;\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s)
 =\sum_\mu \bar v(\mu\mid\delta)v(\alpha_1\mid\mu)\cdots v(\alpha_s\mid\mu)\notag\\
&=\prod_h\biggl\{\sum_{m_h}\biggl[(-1)^{d_h-m_h}
 x^{(d_h-m_h)(d_h-m_h-1)/2+(d_h-m_h)d_{h-1}+(m_h-a_h^{(1)})a_{h-1}^{(1)}+\cdots+(m_h-a_h^{(s)})a_{h-1}^{(s)}}\notag\\
&\qquad\cdot g(x;d_h-d_{h-1},d_h-m_h)g(x;m_h-a_{h-1}^{(1)},a_h^{(1)}-a_{h-1}^{(1)})\cdots g(x;m_h-a_{h-1}^{(s)},a_h^{(s)}-a_{h-1}^{(s)})\biggr]\biggr\}.
\tag{2}
\end{align}
Putting
\begin{align}
(d_h-a_h^{(1)})a_{h-1}^{(1)}+\cdots+(d_h-a_h^{(s)})a_{h-1}^{(s)}&=f_h,
\tag{3}\\
a_{h-1}^{(1)}+\cdots+a_{h-1}^{(s)}-d_{h-1}&=k_{h-1},
\tag{4}\\
d_h-d_{h-1}&=e_h,
\tag{5}\\
d_h-a_{h-1}^{(i)}=q_h^{(i)},\qquad a_h^{(i)}-a_{h-1}^{(i)}&=r_h^{(i)}\quad(i=1,\ldots,s),
\tag{6}
\end{align}
the sum occurring in (2) becomes
\[
 x^{f_h}\omega(x\mid k_{h-1}\mid e_h\mid q_h^{(1)},r_h^{(1)};\ldots;q_h^{(s)},r_h^{(s)}),
\]
and hence
\[
\text{I.}\qquad
 \bar\delta(\delta;\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s)=\prod_h x^{f_h}\,
 \omega(x\mid k_{h-1}\mid e_h\mid q_h^{(1)},r_h^{(1)};\ldots;q_h^{(s)},r_h^{(s)}).
\]
Each $\omega$-function here is
\begin{equation}
 \omega_h=\omega(x\mid k_{h-1}\mid e_h\mid q_h^{(1)},r_h^{(1)};\ldots;q_h^{(s)},r_h^{(s)}).
\tag{7}
\end{equation}
Only finitely many factors differ from 1. From (5) and (6),
\begin{equation}
 e_h\ge0,
 \qquad r_h^{(i)}\ge0.
\tag{8}
\end{equation}
For $\bar\delta(\delta;\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s)$ not to vanish identically it is necessary and sufficient that no $\omega_h$ vanish. Assuming this, one obtains successively
\begin{equation}
 q_h^{(i)}\ge r_h^{(i)}\qquad(i=1,
\ldots,s),
\tag{9}
\end{equation}
which is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
 d_h\ge a_h^{(i)}\qquad(i=1,
\ldots,s),
\tag{10}
\end{equation}
and to
\begin{equation}
 q_h^{(i)}\ge e_h\qquad(i=1,
\ldots,s).
\tag{11}
\end{equation}
Furthermore the critical number of $\omega_h$ is
\[
 k_{h-1}+r_h^{(1)}+\cdots+r_h^{(s)}-e_h=k_h,
\]
so non-vanishing requires
\begin{equation}
 k_h\ge0.
\tag{12}
\end{equation}
Conversely, (8)--(12) imply that each $\omega_h$ is a proper $\omega$-function with non-negative critical number, and therefore $\bar\delta$ is not identically zero and is positive for every $x>1$. Relation (12) is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
 d_h\le\sum_{i=1}^s a_h^{(i)}.
\tag{13}
\end{equation}
Thus the necessary and sufficient conditions for non-vanishing of
\[
 \bar\delta(\delta;\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s)
\]
are (10) and (13): the first says that $\delta$ is a divisor of $\delta'=(\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s)$, and the second that $\delta$ is a multiple of $\delta''=(|\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s|)$.

For $\bar\gamma(\gamma;\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s)$ one obtains similarly, by putting
\[
\begin{gathered}
 (n-a_{h+1}^{(1)})(a_h^{(1)}-c_h)+\cdots+(n-a_{h+1}^{(s)})(a_h^{(s)}-c_h)=f_h,\\
 (n-a_{h+1}^{(1)})+\cdots+(n-a_{h+1}^{(s)})-(n-c_{h+1})=k_{h+1},\\
 c_{h+1}-c_h=e_h,\\
 a_{h+1}^{(i)}-c_h=q_h^{(i)},
 \qquad a_{h+1}^{(i)}-a_h^{(i)}=r_h^{(i)}\quad(i=1,\ldots,s),
\end{gathered}
\]
the formula
\[
\text{II.}\qquad
 \bar\gamma(\gamma;\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s)=\prod_h x^{f_h}\,
 \omega(x\mid k_{h+1}\mid e_h\mid q_h^{(1)},r_h^{(1)};\ldots;q_h^{(s)},r_h^{(s)}).
\]
For $\bar\gamma$ not to vanish, all these $\omega$-functions must be proper with non-negative critical number. Then $\bar\gamma$ is positive for every $x>1$. The necessary and sufficient conditions are
\[
 c_h\le a_h^{(i)},
 \qquad
 c_h\ge n-\sum_{i=1}^s(n-a_h^{(i)}).
\]
The first says that $\gamma$ is a multiple of $\gamma'=[\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s]$, and the second that $\gamma$ is a divisor of $\gamma''=[|\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_s|]$.

This completes the proof of Theorem V, § 7.

Charlottenburg, 9 April 1898.
