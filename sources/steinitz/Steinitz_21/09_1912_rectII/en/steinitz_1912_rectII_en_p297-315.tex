\section*{\S 6. Fundamental modules}

\paragraph{35. Least common multiple and greatest common divisor.}
Let $S$ denote a system of finitely or infinitely many modules of the same degree $s$. The rows that occur in every one of these modules--and there is at least one such row, namely the row $0$--form a module $\mathfrak A$, as follows immediately from the definition of modules. This module $\mathfrak A$ is a common multiple of all modules in $S$, and indeed the least common multiple; for every common multiple $\mathfrak A'$ of the modules in $S$ may contain only rows that occur in all modules of $S$, and hence also in $\mathfrak A$, so that it is a multiple of $\mathfrak A$.

The modules of $S$ have at least one common divisor, namely the unit module $\mathfrak E_s$, which comprises all rows of degree $s$ and therefore divides every module of degree $s$. Let $S'$ be the system of all common divisors of the modules in $S$, and let $\mathfrak T$ be the least common multiple of the modules in $S'$. Then $\mathfrak T$ is not only divisible by every common divisor of the modules in $S$; it is itself a common divisor. For if $\mathfrak U$ is any module from $S$, then $\mathfrak U$ is a common multiple of all modules in $S'$, and is therefore divisible by their least common multiple $\mathfrak T$. Thus $\mathfrak T$ will be called the greatest common divisor of the modules in the system $S$.

If $S$ consists of finitely many modules $\mathfrak A^{(1)},\mathfrak A^{(2)},\ldots,\mathfrak A^{(k)}$, we denote their greatest common divisor by
\[
(\mathfrak A^{(1)},\mathfrak A^{(2)},\ldots,\mathfrak A^{(k)}).
\]
If $\mathfrak A^{(i)}=\Md A_i$, then a basis of the greatest common divisor is obtained by adjoining the rows of the rectangles $A_i$ into one rectangle. Every row of this module can be written in the form $\alpha_1+
\alpha_2+
\cdots+
\alpha_k$, where $\alpha_i$ is taken from $\mathfrak A^{(i)}$.

For two modules $\mathfrak A$ and $\mathfrak B$, the equation
\[
(\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B)=\mathfrak A
\]
is plainly the assertion that $\mathfrak A$ divides $\mathfrak B$. If $A$ is a basis of $\mathfrak A$, $B$ a basis of $\mathfrak B$, and if $r$ is the rank and $\mathfrak d_r$ the $r$-th determinant-divisor of $A$, then by Nos. 21 and 28d) $\mathfrak B$ is divisible by $\mathfrak A$ if and only if the rectangle formed from all rows of $A$ and $B$ has rank $r$ and $r$-th determinant-divisor $\mathfrak d_r$. If these conditions are fulfilled, and if also $\Rg\mathfrak B=r$ and the $r$-th determinant-divisor of $B$ is $\mathfrak d_r$, then $\mathfrak A$ is likewise divisible by $\mathfrak B$, hence $\mathfrak A=\mathfrak B$. Therefore:

If the modules $\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B$ have the same rank $r$ and the same $r$-th determinant-divisor, and if $\mathfrak B$ is divisible by $\mathfrak A$, then $\mathfrak A=\mathfrak B$.

If further rows $\sigma_1,
\sigma_2,
\ldots,
\sigma_k$ are adjoined to a basis of a module $\mathfrak M$, one obtains a basis of the module $\mathfrak M'$ that is the greatest common divisor of $\mathfrak M$ and $\Md(\sigma_1,
\sigma_2,
\ldots,
\sigma_k)$. We write
\[
\mathfrak M'=(\mathfrak M,\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\ldots,\sigma_k).
\]
This module is unchanged if the rows $\sigma_i$ are replaced by rows congruent to them modulo $\mathfrak M$.

\paragraph{36. Fundamental modules.}
Let $C$ be a rectangle of degree $n$ and rank $r$, consisting of rows $\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_m$ whose entries are integral or fractional numbers of $K$. Let $Z$ be the system of all rows $\sigma$ of the form
\[
\sigma=c_1\sigma_1+c_2\sigma_2+\cdots+c_m\sigma_m,
\]
where arbitrary integral or fractional numbers of $K$ are admitted as coefficients. A row belongs to $Z$ if and only if it can be adjoined to $C$ without increasing the rank. From the rows of $Z$ infinitely many rectangles $A$ of rank $r$ can be formed, and every such $A$, just like $C$, determines the whole system $Z$. If $A$ is integral, it is a basis of an integral module $\mathfrak A$ of rank $r$. Thus one obtains a system $S$ of infinitely many modules of rank $r$. Every module $\mathfrak A$ in $S$ determines $Z$ and hence $S$. A module $\mathfrak B$ of rank $r$ belongs to the system determined by $\mathfrak A$ if and only if
\[
\Rg(\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B)=r=\Rg\mathfrak A.
\]
Among all these modules there is one that contains every integral row in $Z$ and therefore divides every module in $S$. This module is called the \emph{fundamental module} belonging to $S$, or to any module of $S$. Conversely, each module in $S$ is said to belong to this same fundamental module. If $\mathfrak A$ is any module, then $\overline{\mathfrak A}$ will henceforth denote the corresponding fundamental module. Thus
\[
\overline{\mathfrak A}=\overline{\mathfrak B}
\]
is equivalent to
\[
\Rg\mathfrak A=\Rg\mathfrak B=\Rg(\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B).
\]
The fundamental module belonging to $\mathfrak A$ can be characterized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[a)] $\overline{\mathfrak A}$ consists of all integral rows $\sigma$ satisfying $\Rg(\mathfrak A,\sigma)=\Rg\mathfrak A$;
\item[b)] $\overline{\mathfrak A}$ consists of all integral rows that can be composed from a basis of $\mathfrak A$ with integral or fractional coefficients;
\item[c)] $\overline{\mathfrak A}$ consists of $0$ and all rows proportional to some row of $\mathfrak A$.
\end{enumerate}
If $\mathfrak A$ is itself a fundamental module, then $\mathfrak A=\overline{\mathfrak A}$. Hence a fundamental module $\mathfrak A$ can be characterized by any of the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[1)] every row satisfying $\Rg(\mathfrak A,\sigma)=\Rg\mathfrak A$ belongs to $\mathfrak A$;
\item[2)] every integral row that can be composed from a basis of $\mathfrak A$ with fractional numbers can also be composed with integral numbers;
\item[3)] every row proportional to a row of $\mathfrak A$ belongs to $\mathfrak A$.
\end{enumerate}
If $\mathfrak e_r$ is the $r$-th elementary divisor of a module $\mathfrak A$ of rank $r$ and $\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_r,
\sigma_r'$ is a normal basis of $\mathfrak A$, then every row of $\mathfrak A$ proportional to $\mathfrak e_r$ has the form $c_r\sigma_r+c\sigma_r'$ and therefore has the factor $\mathfrak e_r$. Thus a fundamental module must have $\mathfrak e_r=1$. Conversely, if $\mathfrak e_r=1$, and therefore the $r$-th determinant-divisor is $1$, then every row $\sigma$ with $\Rg(\mathfrak A,
\sigma)=\Rg\mathfrak A$ belongs to $\mathfrak A$, since adjoining $\sigma$ to a basis of $\mathfrak A$ cannot change the $r$-th determinant-divisor. Hence:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[4)] A module is a fundamental module if and only if it has no elementary divisor different from $0$ and $1$.
\end{enumerate}

Let $\mathfrak A$ and $\mathfrak B$ be modules of the same rank, with $\mathfrak A$ divisible by $\mathfrak B$, so that $(\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B)=\mathfrak B$. Then $\Rg(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak B)=\Rg\mathfrak A=\Rg\mathfrak B$, hence
\[
\overline{\mathfrak A}=\overline{\mathfrak B}.
\]
If $\mathfrak B$ is assumed to be a fundamental module, then $\mathfrak B=\mathfrak A$: $\mathfrak A$ is the only fundamental module of rank $r$ divided by $\mathfrak A$. Conversely, if $\mathfrak A$ is assumed to be a fundamental module, the same equality follows. Therefore:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[5)] A fundamental module is characterized by having no divisor of the same rank except itself.
\end{enumerate}

If $\mathfrak A$ is a fundamental module and $\mathfrak m$ an ideal, $\mathfrak m\mathfrak A$ denotes the module consisting of all rows of $\mathfrak A$ that have $\mathfrak m$ as a factor. If $\mathfrak m=0$, then $\mathfrak m\mathfrak A$ means the zero module; otherwise $\mathfrak m\mathfrak A$ is a module belonging to the fundamental module $\mathfrak A$.

\paragraph{37. Fundamental modules of rank 1.}
A fundamental module of rank $1$ consists of all rows proportional to a fixed nonzero row $\sigma$, together with the zero row. It is denoted by $\overline{\sigma}$. If $\mathfrak A$ is a module of rank $1$, $\mathfrak e_1$ its first elementary divisor, and $\sigma\ne0$ any row in $\mathfrak A$, then $\overline{\mathfrak A}=\overline{\sigma}$. Every row of $\mathfrak A$ belongs to $\overline{\sigma}$ and has the factor $\mathfrak e_1$; conversely every row of $\overline{\sigma}$ with factor $\mathfrak e_1$ belongs to $\mathfrak A$. Thus
\[
\mathfrak A=\mathfrak e_1\overline{\sigma}.
\]
This gives a one-to-one correspondence between all nonzero ideals $\mathfrak e$ and all modules $\mathfrak e\overline{\sigma}$ belonging to the same fundamental module. The relation
\begin{equation}
(\mathfrak a\overline{\sigma},\mathfrak b\overline{\sigma})=(\mathfrak a,\mathfrak b)\overline{\sigma}
\tag{46}
\end{equation}
is immediate.

\paragraph{38.}
Let $\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_r$ be $r$ linearly independent rows of degree $n$. Let $\mathfrak q_i$ be the greatest factor of $\sigma_i$, let $\sigma_i'$ be a row proportional to $\sigma_i$, let $\mathfrak q_i'$ be the greatest factor of $\sigma_i'$, and put
\begin{equation}
(\mathfrak q_i,\mathfrak q_i')=\mathfrak g_i.
\tag{47}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\Md(\sigma_i,\sigma_i')=\mathfrak g_i\overline{\sigma_i}.
\tag{48}
\end{equation}
If the $r$-th determinant-divisor of $\Md(\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_r)$ is written as $\mathfrak q_1\cdots\mathfrak q_r\mathfrak b$, then for each of the $2^r$ rectangles obtained by choosing one row from each pair $\sigma_i,
\sigma_i'$, the $r$-th determinant-divisor equals $\mathfrak b$ times the product of the greatest factors of the chosen rows. The gcd of these products is
\begin{equation}
\mathfrak q_1\cdots\mathfrak q_r\mathfrak b\cdot(\mathfrak g_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak g_r).
\tag{49}
\end{equation}
It is also the $r$-th determinant-divisor of
\begin{equation}
\mathfrak M=\Md(\sigma_1,
\sigma_1',
\ldots,
\sigma_r,
\sigma_r').
\tag{49'}
\end{equation}
By (48), this module is the greatest common divisor of the modules $\mathfrak g_i\overline{\sigma_i}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathfrak M=(\mathfrak g_1\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak g_r\overline{\sigma_r}).
\tag{50}
\end{equation}
If only the rows $\sigma_i$ are fixed, the proportional rows $\sigma_i'$ may be chosen so that all $\mathfrak g_i$ are $1$. Then $\mathfrak M=(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_r})$ and has $\mathfrak b$ as $r$-th determinant-divisor. Therefore $(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_r})$ is a fundamental module if and only if the $r$-th determinant-divisor of $\Md(\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_r)$ equals the product of the greatest factors of the rows $\sigma_i$; in that case it is the fundamental module belonging to $\mathfrak A=\Md(\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_r)$.

It also follows that if $(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_r})$ is a fundamental module of rank $r$, then any $s$ of the modules $\overline{\sigma_i}$ have as greatest common divisor a fundamental module of rank $s$.

\paragraph{39.}
Keep the notation of No. 38 and assume that $(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_r})$ is fundamental, but do not assume that the ideals $\mathfrak a_i$ in
\[
\Md(\sigma_i,
\sigma_i')=\mathfrak a_i\overline{\sigma_i}
\]
are all $1$. Then
\[
\mathfrak A=(\mathfrak a_1\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak a_r\overline{\sigma_r})
\]
has $r$-th determinant-divisor $\mathfrak a_1\cdots\mathfrak a_r$. Hence, if $\mathfrak e_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak e_r$ are successive ideals and $(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_r})$ is a fundamental module of rank $r$, then $(\mathfrak e_1\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak e_r\overline{\sigma_r})$ has elementary divisors $\mathfrak e_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak e_r$.

Conversely, any module $\mathfrak A$ of rank $r$ with elementary divisors $\mathfrak e_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak e_r$ can be written
\[
\mathfrak A=(\mathfrak e_1\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak e_r\overline{\sigma_r}),
\qquad
(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_r})=\overline{\mathfrak A}.
\]
In any representation of this form one has
\begin{equation}
\Kkl\mathfrak A=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\Kkl(\mathfrak e_i\overline{\sigma_i}).
\tag{52}
\end{equation}
The proof uses the fact that all modules belonging to the same fundamental module have the same row-class, and hence the column-classes of two modules of the same rank and same fundamental module are in the ratio of the classes of their $r$-th determinant-divisors. If $\sigma_i'$ is a nonzero row chosen from $\overline{\sigma_i}$ and $\mathfrak b_i$ is its greatest factor, then
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Kkl\mathfrak A}{\Kkl\mathfrak B}
=\Kkl\frac{\mathfrak e_1\cdots\mathfrak e_r}{\mathfrak b_1\cdots\mathfrak b_r}
=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\Kkl\frac{\mathfrak e_i}{\mathfrak b_i}.
\tag{51}
\end{equation}
Since $\mathfrak B$ has a basis of $r$ rows and each $\mathfrak b_i\overline{\sigma_i}$ a basis of one row, these modules belong to the principal class; this gives (52).

\paragraph{40.}
The only fundamental module of degree and rank $n$ is the unit module $\mathfrak E_n$ containing all rows of degree $n$. If it is represented as
\[
\mathfrak E_n=(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_n}),
\]
and if $\sigma_i$ is a nonzero row of $\overline{\sigma_i}$ with greatest factor $\mathfrak q_i$, then $\mathfrak q_1\cdots\mathfrak q_n$ is the $n$-th determinant-divisor of the square system formed by $\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_n$; equivalently, it is the principal ideal represented by its determinant.

Conversely, given nonzero ideals $\mathfrak q_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak q_n$ whose product is a principal ideal, one may choose rows $\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_n$ with greatest factors $\mathfrak q_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak q_n$ such that
\[
(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_n})=\mathfrak E_n.
\]
Indeed, after choosing the first $n-1$ rows so that the $(n-1)$-st determinant-divisor is $\mathfrak q_1\cdots\mathfrak q_{n-1}$, the determinant obtained after adjoining a variable row $\sigma_n=(x_1,
\ldots,
 x_n)$ has the form
\begin{equation}
 c_1x_1+
\cdots+c_nx_n,
\tag{53}
\end{equation}
with $(c_1,
\ldots,
 c_n)=\mathfrak q_1\cdots\mathfrak q_{n-1}$. Restricting the $x_i$ to numbers divisible by $\mathfrak q_n$ represents exactly the numbers divisible by $\mathfrak q_1\cdots\mathfrak q_n$, so the determinant can be made to represent the given principal ideal.

If $\mathfrak A=(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_r})$ is a fundamental module of degree $n$ and rank $r<n$, then one can choose a complementary fundamental module $\mathfrak B=(\overline{\sigma_{r+1}},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_n})$ of rank $n-r$ with
\[
(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak B)=\mathfrak E_n.
\]
Moreover the classes of $\mathfrak A$ and $\mathfrak B$ are reciprocal.

\paragraph{41.}
Every module $\mathfrak A$ of degree $n$ with elementary divisors $\mathfrak e_i$ can be written
\begin{equation}
\mathfrak A=(\mathfrak e_1\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak e_n\overline{\sigma_n})
\tag{54}
\end{equation}
in such a way that $(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_n})=\mathfrak E_n$. If $\Rg\mathfrak A=r$, one first writes
\begin{equation}
\mathfrak A=(\mathfrak e_1\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak e_r\overline{\sigma_r}),
\tag{55}
\end{equation}
where $(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_r})=\overline{\mathfrak A}$, and then complements this fundamental module to $\mathfrak E_n$ as in No. 40; for $i>r$ the elementary divisors are $0$.

If $\mathfrak m$ is a nonzero ideal, then a module whose first $s$ factors are $\mathfrak m\overline{\sigma_i}$ and whose remaining factors are $\overline{\sigma_i}$ contains exactly the rows with factor $\mathfrak m$; hence
\[
(\mathfrak m\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak m\overline{\sigma_n})=\mathfrak m\mathfrak E_n.
\]
Consequently, if $\mathfrak A$ has elementary divisors $\mathfrak e_i$, then $(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak m\mathfrak E_n)$ has elementary divisors $(\mathfrak e_i,
\mathfrak m)$.

\paragraph{42. Relative basis.}
For modules $\mathfrak A$ and $\mathfrak M$ of the same degree, rows
\begin{equation}
\alpha_1,
\ldots,
\alpha_m
\tag{56}
\end{equation}
belonging to $\mathfrak A$ are called a relative basis of $\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M$ if every row $\alpha$ of $\mathfrak A$ satisfies a congruence
\[
\alpha\equiv c_1\alpha_1+
\cdots+c_m\alpha_m\pmod{\mathfrak M}.
\]
Equivalently, $\mathfrak A$ is divisible by
\begin{equation}
\mathfrak A'=(\alpha_1,
\ldots,
\alpha_m,
\mathfrak M).
\tag{57}
\end{equation}
This is exactly the condition
\begin{equation}
(\mathfrak M,
\alpha_1,
\ldots,
\alpha_m)=(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M).
\tag{58}
\end{equation}
The same rows then also form a basis of $(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M)\mid\mathfrak M$. Conversely, from a basis $\alpha_1',
\ldots,
\alpha_m'$ of $(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M)\mid\mathfrak M$ one may write $\alpha_i'=\alpha_i+
\mu_i$, with $\alpha_i\in\mathfrak A$ and $\mu_i\in\mathfrak M$; then the $\alpha_i$ form a basis of $\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M$.

The least number of rows of $\mathfrak A$ that must be adjoined to $\mathfrak M$ in order to obtain $(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M)$ is $0$ if $\mathfrak A$ is divisible by $\mathfrak M$; otherwise it is the least possible number of rows in a basis of $\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M$. This number is unchanged if $\mathfrak M$ is replaced by $(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M)$.

\paragraph{43. Relative rank.}
Define
\[
\Rg\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M=r
\]
to mean that $r$ is the least number such that between any $r+1$ rows $\alpha_1,
\ldots,
\alpha_{r+1}$ of $\mathfrak A$ there is a congruence
\begin{equation}
 c_1\alpha_1+
\cdots+c_{r+1}\alpha_{r+1}\equiv0\pmod{\mathfrak M}
\tag{60}
\end{equation}
with not all coefficients zero. Then
\begin{equation}
\Rg\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M=
\Rg(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M)\mid\mathfrak M.
\tag{62}
\end{equation}
If $\mathfrak A$ divides $\mathfrak M$, $\Rg\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M=r$, and $\Rg\mathfrak M=s$, then
\[
\Rg\mathfrak A=r+s.
\]
The proof chooses rows $\alpha_1,
\ldots,
\alpha_r$ in $\mathfrak A$ without a linear congruence modulo $\mathfrak M$ and rows $\mu_1,
\ldots,
\mu_s$ in $\mathfrak M$ without a linear equation. There is no linear equation among the combined $r+s$ rows; conversely every row $\alpha$ of $\mathfrak A$ can be expressed fractionally from them by using a congruence
\begin{equation}
 c\alpha\equiv c_1\alpha_1+
\cdots+c_r\alpha_r\pmod{\mathfrak M}
\tag{65}
\end{equation}
and then an ordinary linear equation in $\mathfrak M$. For arbitrary modules of the same degree this yields
\begin{equation}
\Rg\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M=
\Rg(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M)-\Rg\mathfrak M.
\tag{69}
\end{equation}
Replacing each module by another belonging to the same fundamental module leaves the relative rank unchanged:
\begin{equation}
\Rg\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M=
\Rg\overline{\mathfrak A}\mid\overline{\mathfrak M}.
\tag{70}
\end{equation}
Thus $\Rg\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M=0$ is precisely the condition that $\mathfrak A$ be a multiple of $\mathfrak M$; and if it holds, then $\overline{\mathfrak A}$ is divisible by $\overline{\mathfrak M}$.

\section*{\S 7. The divisibility conditions}

\paragraph{44.}
A module of rank $r$ has a basis of $r+1$ rows. If it belongs to the principal class, an irreducible basis may consist of only $r$ rows. In general, when forming a basis of $r+1$ rows for a module $\mathfrak A$ of rank $r$, one row within $\mathfrak A$ may be chosen arbitrarily; if $\mathfrak A$ is not in the principal class, that row must be chosen nonzero.

Let $\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_{r+1}$ be a basis of $\mathfrak A$ and let $\alpha\ne0$ be a row of $\mathfrak A$. Then
\begin{align}
\alpha&=a_1\sigma_1+
\cdots+a_{r+1}\sigma_{r+1},
\tag{71}\\
0&=b_1\sigma_1+
\cdots+b_{r+1}\sigma_{r+1},
\tag{72}
\end{align}
with not all $b_i$ zero. The $b_i$ may be normalized so that their gcd is relatively prime to the gcd of the $a_i$. The linear forms $a_ix+b_iy$ can then be made coprime by a suitable choice $x=x_0$, $y=y_0$:
\begin{equation}
\beta_i=a_ix_0+b_iy_0.
\tag{73}
\end{equation}
Hence $\mathfrak A$ has a basis consisting of the prescribed row $\alpha$ and $r$ further rows of $\mathfrak A$.

\paragraph{45. Level of a module relative to another.}
The notation $\St\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M$ is used only when $\Rg\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M=0$, that is, when $\mathfrak A$ is divisible by $\mathfrak M$. It denotes the least number of rows that must be adjoined to $\mathfrak M$ to obtain $(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M)$. It satisfies
\begin{equation}
\St\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M=\St(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M)\mid\mathfrak M,
\tag{74}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\St\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M\le \Rg\mathfrak A.
\tag{75}
\end{equation}
The latter follows by choosing a nonzero row $\alpha$ of $\mathfrak A$, multiplying it so that $c\alpha\in\mathfrak M$, and using No. 44 to form a basis of $\mathfrak A$ from $c\alpha$ and at most $r=\Rg\mathfrak A$ further rows.

\paragraph{46.}
Let $\Rg\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M=0$ and $\Rg\mathfrak B\mid\mathfrak M=0$. If $\mathfrak A$ divides $\mathfrak B$, then
\begin{equation}
\St\mathfrak B\mid\mathfrak M\le \St\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M.
\tag{76}
\end{equation}
After replacing $\mathfrak A$ and $\mathfrak B$ by $(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M)$ and $(\mathfrak B,
\mathfrak M)$, choose bases $\alpha_1,
\ldots,
\alpha_s$ of $\mathfrak A'\mid\mathfrak M$ and $\beta_1,
\ldots,
\beta_t$ of $\mathfrak B'\mid\mathfrak M$. Since the $\beta_i$ also belong to $\mathfrak A'$, they are congruent modulo $\mathfrak M$ to linear combinations of the $\alpha_k$. These combinations span a module $\mathfrak B''$ of rank at most $s$, but by (75) its level over $\mathfrak M$ is $t$. Hence $t\le s$.

\paragraph{47.}
Let $\mathfrak A$ be a module of degree $n$ and let $\mathfrak m\ne0$ be an ideal. Then $\Rg\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak m\mathfrak E_n=0$. If $\mathfrak e_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak e_n$ are the elementary divisors of $\mathfrak A$, then
\[
\St\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak m\mathfrak E_n
=
\#\{k:\mathfrak e_k\text{ is not divisible by }\mathfrak m\}.
\]
Writing $\mathfrak A$ in the normal form
\[
\mathfrak A=(\mathfrak e_1\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak e_n\overline{\sigma_n}),
\quad
(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_n})=\mathfrak E_n,
\]
reduces the assertion to counting those elementary divisors of $(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak m\mathfrak E_n)$ that are proper divisors of $\mathfrak m$.

\paragraph{48.}
Let $\mathfrak A$ and $\mathfrak B$ be modules of degree $n$, and suppose that $\mathfrak A$ divides $\mathfrak B$. If $\mathfrak a_k$ and $\mathfrak b_k$ are the corresponding elementary divisors of $\mathfrak A$ and $\mathfrak B$, then every $\mathfrak b_k$ is divisible by $\mathfrak a_k$.

Indeed, if $\mathfrak a_k\ne0$, then by No. 46
\begin{equation}
\St\mathfrak B\mid\mathfrak a_k\mathfrak E_n
\le
\St\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak a_k\mathfrak E_n.
\tag{81}
\end{equation}
The right-hand side is at most $k-1$, so at most $k-1$ elementary divisors of $\mathfrak B$ can fail to be divisible by $\mathfrak a_k$. Thus $\mathfrak b_k$ is divisible by $\mathfrak a_k$.

\paragraph{49. The divisibility conditions.}
For the rectangle $B$ to be divisible by the rectangle $A$, it is necessary that every elementary divisor of $B$ be divisible by the corresponding elementary divisor of $A$. This condition is also sufficient if $\Rg A>\Rg B$. If $\Rg A=\Rg B=r$, one further condition is necessary and sufficient: if $\mathfrak d_r$ and $\mathfrak b_r$ are the $r$-th determinant-divisors of $A$ and $B$, then the integral ideal $\mathfrak b_r:\mathfrak d_r$ must have a divisor belonging to the class $\Kkl B:\Kkl A$.

Necessity follows by assuming
\begin{equation}
B=PAQ.
\tag{82}
\end{equation}
For transposed rectangles $B^*=Q^*(PA)^*$, so each elementary divisor of $B$ is divisible by the corresponding one of $A$. If the ranks are both $r$, then also $\Rg(PA)=r$ and
\begin{equation}
\Zkl A=\Zkl PA,
\qquad
\Kkl PA=\Kkl B.
\tag{83}
\end{equation}
Together with the determinant class relations
\begin{equation}
\Zkl A\cdot\Kkl A=\Kl\mathfrak d,
\quad
\Zkl PA\cdot\Kkl PA=\Kl\mathfrak d'',
\quad
\Zkl B\cdot\Kkl B=\Kl\mathfrak d',
\tag{84}
\end{equation}
this gives a divisor of $\mathfrak d':\mathfrak d$ in the class $\Kkl B:\Kkl A$.

For sufficiency, let $\mathfrak a_k,
\mathfrak d_k$ be the elementary and determinant-divisors of $A$, and $\mathfrak b_k,
\mathfrak b_k'$ those of $B$. Let $\Rg A=r$, $\Rg B=r'$, $\Kkl A=x$, $\Kkl B=x'$. Assume that $\mathfrak a_k$ divides $\mathfrak b_k$, and that either $r>r'$ or, when $r=r'$, $\mathfrak b_r':\mathfrak d_r$ has a divisor in the class $x':x$.

In the first case $r>r'$, choose ideals $\mathfrak q_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak q_r$ with
\begin{equation}
\Kl(\mathfrak q_1\cdots\mathfrak q_r)=\Kl\mathfrak d_r:x,
\qquad
\Kl\mathfrak q_i=\Kl\mathfrak a_i.
\tag{85}
\end{equation}
Using No. 40, choose rows of a sufficiently large degree with these greatest factors and the prescribed determinant-divisor. The associated principal-class modules can then be arranged so that one obtains bases $A'$ and $B'$ equivalent to $A$ and $B$, with $B'$ divisible by $A'$.

In the second case $r=r'$, take a divisor $\mathfrak t$ of $\mathfrak b_r':\mathfrak d_r$ in the class $x':x$. Choose ideals $\mathfrak c_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak c_r$ such that $\mathfrak c_k$ is divisible by $\mathfrak a_k$ and divides $\mathfrak b_k$, the $\mathfrak c_k$ divide successively, and $\mathfrak c_1\cdots\mathfrak c_r=\mathfrak d_r\mathfrak t$. Then write
\[
\mathfrak A=(\mathfrak a_1\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak a_r\overline{\sigma_r}),
\qquad
\mathfrak B=(\mathfrak b_1\overline{\tau_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak b_r\overline{\tau_r}),
\]
and set
\[
\mathfrak C=(\mathfrak c_1\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak c_r\overline{\sigma_r}),
\qquad
\mathfrak D=(\mathfrak c_1\overline{\tau_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak c_r\overline{\tau_r}).
\]
Then $\mathfrak C$ is a multiple of $\mathfrak A$, $\mathfrak D$ a divisor of $\mathfrak B$, and the corresponding rectangles $C$ and $D^*$ have the same column-class $x'$ and the same elementary divisors. They are therefore equivalent. Since $B$ is divisible by $D^*$ and $C$ by $A$, $B$ is divisible by $A$.

\section*{\S 8. Ideal systems}

\paragraph{50. Ideal systems.}
In No. 22 Steinitz defined the addition of two rows of degree $n$ and the multiplication of a row by a number. Thus all rows of degree $n$ constitute a system $\mathfrak E$ with two laws of composition. First, two elements $\alpha,
\beta$ determine a sum $\alpha+
\beta$; addition is associative and commutative, and for every pair $\alpha,
\beta$ there is a unique $\gamma$ with $\beta+
\gamma=\alpha$, written $\alpha-
\beta$. Second, an element $\alpha$ and an integer $a$ of $K$ determine $a\alpha$, with
\[
1\alpha=\alpha,
\quad a(b\alpha)=(ab)\alpha,
\quad a(\alpha+
\beta)=a\alpha+a\beta,
\quad (a+b)\alpha=a\alpha+b\alpha.
\]
Steinitz henceforth calls an \emph{ideal system belonging to the field $K$} any system whose elements possess these two operations--addition and multiplication by an integer of $K$--with the properties just listed.
