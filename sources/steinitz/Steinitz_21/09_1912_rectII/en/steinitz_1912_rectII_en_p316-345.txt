50. Ideal systems. The laws listed in No. 50 imply, for every ideal system J, the
existence of an element 0 such that
α+0=0+α=α
for all α. If J consists only of this element, we also write J = 0.
It is clear from No. 23 that every module is an ideal system, and that the modules of
degree n are the only ideal systems obtainable from rows of degree n, provided the laws of
composition of No. 22 are used. In the case n = 1 the rows are replaced by the individual
integers of K; a system of integers of K is an ideal system in the present sense precisely
when it forms an ideal in Dedekind’s usual sense.
51. Isomorphism. Two ideal systems J, J1 are called isomorphic, or of the same type,
when there is a one-to-one correspondence between them satisfying the following two
requirements:
1. If the elements α, β of J correspond to α1 , β1 of J1 , then α +β and α1 +β1 correspond.
2. If α and α1 correspond, and a is any integer of K, then aα and aα1 correspond.
The module-theoretic notions introduced above are now to be phrased so that they
make sense for arbitrary ideal systems. Of special importance are those properties which
depend only on the type of the system, and hence remain unchanged after replacing the
system by an isomorphic one.
52. Ideal subsystems. A subsystem T of an ideal system J is itself an ideal system if
and only if each multiple aα of an element α ∈ T and each sum of two elements of T again
belongs to T. The system J itself is counted among its subsystems. When J is a module, its
ideal subsystems are exactly its multiples. An isomorphism between ideal systems carries
every ideal subsystem of the first to an ideal subsystem of the second.
Let S be a finite or infinite family of ideal systems which are all subsystems of one and
the same ideal system J. Then the system B of all elements common to the systems in S
is again an ideal system. If the systems in S are modules of degree n, hence subsystems of
En , then B is their least common multiple.

53. Congruences and residue-class systems. Let M be an ideal subsystem of the
ideal system J. Two elements α, β of J are called congruent modulo M if α − β belongs to
M. The elements of J thereby fall into residue classes. If α runs through a residue class A
and β through a residue class B, then α + β lies in a fixed residue class, denoted A + B.
If α runs through A and a is a fixed integer, then aα runs through a fixed residue class,
denoted aA. Thus the residue classes of J modulo M themselves form an ideal system,
denoted J/M.
If A is an ideal subsystem of J, the residue classes modulo M that contain elements of
A form an ideal subsystem of J/M, denoted A/M. In particular,
(A, M)/M = A/M.

56. Two distinguished species. Two classes of ideal systems are singled out: systems
of rank 0, in which no element has order 0, and systems in which every non-zero element
has order 0. The zero system belongs to both classes.
The elements of an ideal system J whose order is not 0 form an ideal subsystem, denoted
J′ . In J/J′ , every non-zero element has order 0. An ideal system is called primary if the
orders of all its elements are powers of one and the same prime ideal. The elements of J′
whose orders are powers of a given prime ideal p form an ideal system Jp , called a primary
component of J′ . Every element of J′ has a unique representation as a sum of elements
from the components Jp .

57. Addition of types of ideal systems. When only the type of an ideal system
matters, write [J]. Thus [J] = [J1 ] means that J and J1 are isomorphic.
Let A, B, C be ideal subsystems of an ideal system J, and suppose every element ω of J
has a unique representation
ω = α + β + γ,
(94)
with α ∈ A, β ∈ B, γ ∈ C. Replacing the three subsystems by isomorphic ones yields an
isomorphic ideal system. Hence [J] is determined by [A], [B], and [C], and Steinitz writes
[J] = [A] + [B] + [C].
Conversely, this addition may be defined for arbitrary types by forming rows (α, β, γ) with
components from representatives of the three types. The operation is associative and
commutative, and [0] is its identity element.

67. Extensions. The restriction to finite algebraic number fields may be removed. If K
is any field generated by algebraic numbers, the criteria for equivalence and divisibility of
rectangular systems remain the same. Only the definition of module must explicitly require
a finite basis. Accordingly, an ideal is any ideal subsystem of the integers of K with finite
basis. The division into ideal classes follows the same principle as for finite fields, although
the number of classes need not be finite. If K is the field of all algebraic numbers, the
number of ideal classes is 1. In such cases, divisibility of a rectangle B by a rectangle A
is necessary and sufficient exactly when every elementary divisor of B is divisible by the
corresponding elementary divisor of A.

68. Earlier literature. The oldest investigations related to divisibility of rectangular
systems go back to Stephen Smith. In On Systems of Linear Indeterminate Equations and
Congruences, London Philosophical Transactions 151 (1861), pp. 293–326, the concepts later
called determinant-divisors and elementary divisors first appear for rectangular systems
with integral entries in the rational number field; the proof that each elementary divisor
divides the next is already present there. In 1873 Smith proved that equality of elementary
divisors is not only necessary but also sufficient for equivalence of rectangular systems. The
divisibility conditions were later completely settled by Frobenius.
The investigations in the section on ideal systems are, in the rational case, only formally
different from the theory of groups with commuting elements, developed by Kronecker and
by Frobenius–Stickelberger. The connection with module theory is already present there.
In algebraic fields, the present paper appears to be the first to carry out the corresponding
investigation. The importance of this extension is also shown by I. Schur’s recent work on
linear substitution groups, which uses results from the first part of the present paper.
