\section*{\S 6. Fundamental modules}

\paragraph{35. Least common multiple and greatest common divisor.}
Let $S$ denote a system of finitely or infinitely many modules of the same degree $s$. The rows that occur in every one of these modules--and there is at least one such row, namely the row $0$--form a module $\mathfrak A$, as follows immediately from the definition of modules. This module $\mathfrak A$ is a common multiple of all modules in $S$, and indeed the least common multiple; for every common multiple $\mathfrak A'$ of the modules in $S$ may contain only rows that occur in all modules of $S$, and hence also in $\mathfrak A$, so that it is a multiple of $\mathfrak A$.

The modules of $S$ have at least one common divisor, namely the unit module $\mathfrak E_s$, which comprises all rows of degree $s$ and therefore divides every module of degree $s$. Let $S'$ be the system of all common divisors of the modules in $S$, and let $\mathfrak T$ be the least common multiple of the modules in $S'$. Then $\mathfrak T$ is not only divisible by every common divisor of the modules in $S$; it is itself a common divisor. For if $\mathfrak U$ is any module from $S$, then $\mathfrak U$ is a common multiple of all modules in $S'$, and is therefore divisible by their least common multiple $\mathfrak T$. Thus $\mathfrak T$ will be called the greatest common divisor of the modules in the system $S$.

If $S$ consists of finitely many modules $\mathfrak A^{(1)},\mathfrak A^{(2)},\ldots,\mathfrak A^{(k)}$, we denote their greatest common divisor by
\[
(\mathfrak A^{(1)},\mathfrak A^{(2)},\ldots,\mathfrak A^{(k)}).
\]
If $\mathfrak A^{(i)}=\Md A_i$, then a basis of the greatest common divisor is obtained by adjoining the rows of the rectangles $A_i$ into one rectangle. Every row of this module can be written in the form $\alpha_1+
\alpha_2+
\cdots+
\alpha_k$, where $\alpha_i$ is taken from $\mathfrak A^{(i)}$.

For two modules $\mathfrak A$ and $\mathfrak B$, the equation
\[
(\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B)=\mathfrak A
\]
is plainly the assertion that $\mathfrak A$ divides $\mathfrak B$. If $A$ is a basis of $\mathfrak A$, $B$ a basis of $\mathfrak B$, and if $r$ is the rank and $\mathfrak d_r$ the $r$-th determinant-divisor of $A$, then by Nos. 21 and 28d) $\mathfrak B$ is divisible by $\mathfrak A$ if and only if the rectangle formed from all rows of $A$ and $B$ has rank $r$ and $r$-th determinant-divisor $\mathfrak d_r$. If these conditions are fulfilled, and if also $\Rg\mathfrak B=r$ and the $r$-th determinant-divisor of $B$ is $\mathfrak d_r$, then $\mathfrak A$ is likewise divisible by $\mathfrak B$, hence $\mathfrak A=\mathfrak B$. Therefore:

If the modules $\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B$ have the same rank $r$ and the same $r$-th determinant-divisor, and if $\mathfrak B$ is divisible by $\mathfrak A$, then $\mathfrak A=\mathfrak B$.

If further rows $\sigma_1,
\sigma_2,
\ldots,
\sigma_k$ are adjoined to a basis of a module $\mathfrak M$, one obtains a basis of the module $\mathfrak M'$ that is the greatest common divisor of $\mathfrak M$ and $\Md(\sigma_1,
\sigma_2,
\ldots,
\sigma_k)$. We write
\[
\mathfrak M'=(\mathfrak M,\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\ldots,\sigma_k).
\]
This module is unchanged if the rows $\sigma_i$ are replaced by rows congruent to them modulo $\mathfrak M$.

\paragraph{36. Fundamental modules.}
Let $C$ be a rectangle of degree $n$ and rank $r$, consisting of rows $\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_m$ whose entries are integral or fractional numbers of $K$. Let $Z$ be the system of all rows $\sigma$ of the form
\[
\sigma=c_1\sigma_1+c_2\sigma_2+\cdots+c_m\sigma_m,
\]
where arbitrary integral or fractional numbers of $K$ are admitted as coefficients. A row belongs to $Z$ if and only if it can be adjoined to $C$ without increasing the rank. From the rows of $Z$ infinitely many rectangles $A$ of rank $r$ can be formed, and every such $A$, just like $C$, determines the whole system $Z$. If $A$ is integral, it is a basis of an integral module $\mathfrak A$ of rank $r$. Thus one obtains a system $S$ of infinitely many modules of rank $r$. Every module $\mathfrak A$ in $S$ determines $Z$ and hence $S$. A module $\mathfrak B$ of rank $r$ belongs to the system determined by $\mathfrak A$ if and only if
\[
\Rg(\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B)=r=\Rg\mathfrak A.
\]
Among all these modules there is one that contains every integral row in $Z$ and therefore divides every module in $S$. This module is called the \emph{fundamental module} belonging to $S$, or to any module of $S$. Conversely, each module in $S$ is said to belong to this same fundamental module. If $\mathfrak A$ is any module, then $\overline{\mathfrak A}$ will henceforth denote the corresponding fundamental module. Thus
\[
\overline{\mathfrak A}=\overline{\mathfrak B}
\]
is equivalent to
\[
\Rg\mathfrak A=\Rg\mathfrak B=\Rg(\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B).
\]
The fundamental module belonging to $\mathfrak A$ can be characterized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[a)] $\overline{\mathfrak A}$ consists of all integral rows $\sigma$ satisfying $\Rg(\mathfrak A,\sigma)=\Rg\mathfrak A$;
\item[b)] $\overline{\mathfrak A}$ consists of all integral rows that can be composed from a basis of $\mathfrak A$ with integral or fractional coefficients;
\item[c)] $\overline{\mathfrak A}$ consists of $0$ and all rows proportional to some row of $\mathfrak A$.
\end{enumerate}
If $\mathfrak A$ is itself a fundamental module, then $\mathfrak A=\overline{\mathfrak A}$. Hence a fundamental module $\mathfrak A$ can be characterized by any of the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[1)] every row satisfying $\Rg(\mathfrak A,\sigma)=\Rg\mathfrak A$ belongs to $\mathfrak A$;
\item[2)] every integral row that can be composed from a basis of $\mathfrak A$ with fractional numbers can also be composed with integral numbers;
\item[3)] every row proportional to a row of $\mathfrak A$ belongs to $\mathfrak A$.
\end{enumerate}
If $\mathfrak e_r$ is the $r$-th elementary divisor of a module $\mathfrak A$ of rank $r$ and $\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_r,
\sigma_r'$ is a normal basis of $\mathfrak A$, then every row of $\mathfrak A$ proportional to $\mathfrak e_r$ has the form $c_r\sigma_r+c\sigma_r'$ and therefore has the factor $\mathfrak e_r$. Thus a fundamental module must have $\mathfrak e_r=1$. Conversely, if $\mathfrak e_r=1$, and therefore the $r$-th determinant-divisor is $1$, then every row $\sigma$ with $\Rg(\mathfrak A,
\sigma)=\Rg\mathfrak A$ belongs to $\mathfrak A$, since adjoining $\sigma$ to a basis of $\mathfrak A$ cannot change the $r$-th determinant-divisor. Hence:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[4)] A module is a fundamental module if and only if it has no elementary divisor different from $0$ and $1$.
\end{enumerate}

Let $\mathfrak A$ and $\mathfrak B$ be modules of the same rank, with $\mathfrak A$ divisible by $\mathfrak B$, so that $(\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B)=\mathfrak B$. Then $\Rg(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak B)=\Rg\mathfrak A=\Rg\mathfrak B$, hence
\[
\overline{\mathfrak A}=\overline{\mathfrak B}.
\]
If $\mathfrak B$ is assumed to be a fundamental module, then $\mathfrak B=\mathfrak A$: $\mathfrak A$ is the only fundamental module of rank $r$ divided by $\mathfrak A$. Conversely, if $\mathfrak A$ is assumed to be a fundamental module, the same equality follows. Therefore:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[5)] A fundamental module is characterized by having no divisor of the same rank except itself.
\end{enumerate}

If $\mathfrak A$ is a fundamental module and $\mathfrak m$ an ideal, $\mathfrak m\mathfrak A$ denotes the module consisting of all rows of $\mathfrak A$ that have $\mathfrak m$ as a factor. If $\mathfrak m=0$, then $\mathfrak m\mathfrak A$ means the zero module; otherwise $\mathfrak m\mathfrak A$ is a module belonging to the fundamental module $\mathfrak A$.

\paragraph{37. Fundamental modules of rank 1.}
A fundamental module of rank $1$ consists of all rows proportional to a fixed nonzero row $\sigma$, together with the zero row. It is denoted by $\overline{\sigma}$. If $\mathfrak A$ is a module of rank $1$, $\mathfrak e_1$ its first elementary divisor, and $\sigma\ne0$ any row in $\mathfrak A$, then $\overline{\mathfrak A}=\overline{\sigma}$. Every row of $\mathfrak A$ belongs to $\overline{\sigma}$ and has the factor $\mathfrak e_1$; conversely every row of $\overline{\sigma}$ with factor $\mathfrak e_1$ belongs to $\mathfrak A$. Thus
\[
\mathfrak A=\mathfrak e_1\overline{\sigma}.
\]
This gives a one-to-one correspondence between all nonzero ideals $\mathfrak e$ and all modules $\mathfrak e\overline{\sigma}$ belonging to the same fundamental module. The relation
\begin{equation}
(\mathfrak a\overline{\sigma},\mathfrak b\overline{\sigma})=(\mathfrak a,\mathfrak b)\overline{\sigma}
\tag{46}
\end{equation}
is immediate.

\paragraph{38.}
Let $\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_r$ be $r$ linearly independent rows of degree $n$. Let $\mathfrak q_i$ be the greatest factor of $\sigma_i$, let $\sigma_i'$ be a row proportional to $\sigma_i$, let $\mathfrak q_i'$ be the greatest factor of $\sigma_i'$, and put
\begin{equation}
(\mathfrak q_i,\mathfrak q_i')=\mathfrak g_i.
\tag{47}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\Md(\sigma_i,\sigma_i')=\mathfrak g_i\overline{\sigma_i}.
\tag{48}
\end{equation}
If the $r$-th determinant-divisor of $\Md(\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_r)$ is written as $\mathfrak q_1\cdots\mathfrak q_r\mathfrak b$, then for each of the $2^r$ rectangles obtained by choosing one row from each pair $\sigma_i,
\sigma_i'$, the $r$-th determinant-divisor equals $\mathfrak b$ times the product of the greatest factors of the chosen rows. The gcd of these products is
\begin{equation}
\mathfrak q_1\cdots\mathfrak q_r\mathfrak b\cdot(\mathfrak g_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak g_r).
\tag{49}
\end{equation}
It is also the $r$-th determinant-divisor of
\begin{equation}
\mathfrak M=\Md(\sigma_1,
\sigma_1',
\ldots,
\sigma_r,
\sigma_r').
\tag{49'}
\end{equation}
By (48), this module is the greatest common divisor of the modules $\mathfrak g_i\overline{\sigma_i}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathfrak M=(\mathfrak g_1\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak g_r\overline{\sigma_r}).
\tag{50}
\end{equation}
If only the rows $\sigma_i$ are fixed, the proportional rows $\sigma_i'$ may be chosen so that all $\mathfrak g_i$ are $1$. Then $\mathfrak M=(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_r})$ and has $\mathfrak b$ as $r$-th determinant-divisor. Therefore $(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_r})$ is a fundamental module if and only if the $r$-th determinant-divisor of $\Md(\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_r)$ equals the product of the greatest factors of the rows $\sigma_i$; in that case it is the fundamental module belonging to $\mathfrak A=\Md(\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_r)$.

It also follows that if $(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_r})$ is a fundamental module of rank $r$, then any $s$ of the modules $\overline{\sigma_i}$ have as greatest common divisor a fundamental module of rank $s$.

\paragraph{39.}
Keep the notation of No. 38 and assume that $(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_r})$ is fundamental, but do not assume that the ideals $\mathfrak a_i$ in
\[
\Md(\sigma_i,
\sigma_i')=\mathfrak a_i\overline{\sigma_i}
\]
are all $1$. Then
\[
\mathfrak A=(\mathfrak a_1\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak a_r\overline{\sigma_r})
\]
has $r$-th determinant-divisor $\mathfrak a_1\cdots\mathfrak a_r$. Hence, if $\mathfrak e_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak e_r$ are successive ideals and $(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_r})$ is a fundamental module of rank $r$, then $(\mathfrak e_1\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak e_r\overline{\sigma_r})$ has elementary divisors $\mathfrak e_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak e_r$.

Conversely, any module $\mathfrak A$ of rank $r$ with elementary divisors $\mathfrak e_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak e_r$ can be written
\[
\mathfrak A=(\mathfrak e_1\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak e_r\overline{\sigma_r}),
\qquad
(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_r})=\overline{\mathfrak A}.
\]
In any representation of this form one has
\begin{equation}
\Kkl\mathfrak A=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\Kkl(\mathfrak e_i\overline{\sigma_i}).
\tag{52}
\end{equation}
The proof uses the fact that all modules belonging to the same fundamental module have the same row-class, and hence the column-classes of two modules of the same rank and same fundamental module are in the ratio of the classes of their $r$-th determinant-divisors. If $\sigma_i'$ is a nonzero row chosen from $\overline{\sigma_i}$ and $\mathfrak b_i$ is its greatest factor, then
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Kkl\mathfrak A}{\Kkl\mathfrak B}
=\Kkl\frac{\mathfrak e_1\cdots\mathfrak e_r}{\mathfrak b_1\cdots\mathfrak b_r}
=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\Kkl\frac{\mathfrak e_i}{\mathfrak b_i}.
\tag{51}
\end{equation}
Since $\mathfrak B$ has a basis of $r$ rows and each $\mathfrak b_i\overline{\sigma_i}$ a basis of one row, these modules belong to the principal class; this gives (52).

\paragraph{40.}
The only fundamental module of degree and rank $n$ is the unit module $\mathfrak E_n$ containing all rows of degree $n$. If it is represented as
\[
\mathfrak E_n=(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_n}),
\]
and if $\sigma_i$ is a nonzero row of $\overline{\sigma_i}$ with greatest factor $\mathfrak q_i$, then $\mathfrak q_1\cdots\mathfrak q_n$ is the $n$-th determinant-divisor of the square system formed by $\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_n$; equivalently, it is the principal ideal represented by its determinant.

Conversely, given nonzero ideals $\mathfrak q_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak q_n$ whose product is a principal ideal, one may choose rows $\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_n$ with greatest factors $\mathfrak q_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak q_n$ such that
\[
(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_n})=\mathfrak E_n.
\]
Indeed, after choosing the first $n-1$ rows so that the $(n-1)$-st determinant-divisor is $\mathfrak q_1\cdots\mathfrak q_{n-1}$, the determinant obtained after adjoining a variable row $\sigma_n=(x_1,
\ldots,
 x_n)$ has the form
\begin{equation}
 c_1x_1+
\cdots+c_nx_n,
\tag{53}
\end{equation}
with $(c_1,
\ldots,
 c_n)=\mathfrak q_1\cdots\mathfrak q_{n-1}$. Restricting the $x_i$ to numbers divisible by $\mathfrak q_n$ represents exactly the numbers divisible by $\mathfrak q_1\cdots\mathfrak q_n$, so the determinant can be made to represent the given principal ideal.

If $\mathfrak A=(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_r})$ is a fundamental module of degree $n$ and rank $r<n$, then one can choose a complementary fundamental module $\mathfrak B=(\overline{\sigma_{r+1}},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_n})$ of rank $n-r$ with
\[
(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak B)=\mathfrak E_n.
\]
Moreover the classes of $\mathfrak A$ and $\mathfrak B$ are reciprocal.

\paragraph{41.}
Every module $\mathfrak A$ of degree $n$ with elementary divisors $\mathfrak e_i$ can be written
\begin{equation}
\mathfrak A=(\mathfrak e_1\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak e_n\overline{\sigma_n})
\tag{54}
\end{equation}
in such a way that $(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_n})=\mathfrak E_n$. If $\Rg\mathfrak A=r$, one first writes
\begin{equation}
\mathfrak A=(\mathfrak e_1\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak e_r\overline{\sigma_r}),
\tag{55}
\end{equation}
where $(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_r})=\overline{\mathfrak A}$, and then complements this fundamental module to $\mathfrak E_n$ as in No. 40; for $i>r$ the elementary divisors are $0$.

If $\mathfrak m$ is a nonzero ideal, then a module whose first $s$ factors are $\mathfrak m\overline{\sigma_i}$ and whose remaining factors are $\overline{\sigma_i}$ contains exactly the rows with factor $\mathfrak m$; hence
\[
(\mathfrak m\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak m\overline{\sigma_n})=\mathfrak m\mathfrak E_n.
\]
Consequently, if $\mathfrak A$ has elementary divisors $\mathfrak e_i$, then $(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak m\mathfrak E_n)$ has elementary divisors $(\mathfrak e_i,
\mathfrak m)$.

\paragraph{42. Relative basis.}
For modules $\mathfrak A$ and $\mathfrak M$ of the same degree, rows
\begin{equation}
\alpha_1,
\ldots,
\alpha_m
\tag{56}
\end{equation}
belonging to $\mathfrak A$ are called a relative basis of $\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M$ if every row $\alpha$ of $\mathfrak A$ satisfies a congruence
\[
\alpha\equiv c_1\alpha_1+
\cdots+c_m\alpha_m\pmod{\mathfrak M}.
\]
Equivalently, $\mathfrak A$ is divisible by
\begin{equation}
\mathfrak A'=(\alpha_1,
\ldots,
\alpha_m,
\mathfrak M).
\tag{57}
\end{equation}
This is exactly the condition
\begin{equation}
(\mathfrak M,
\alpha_1,
\ldots,
\alpha_m)=(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M).
\tag{58}
\end{equation}
The same rows then also form a basis of $(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M)\mid\mathfrak M$. Conversely, from a basis $\alpha_1',
\ldots,
\alpha_m'$ of $(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M)\mid\mathfrak M$ one may write $\alpha_i'=\alpha_i+
\mu_i$, with $\alpha_i\in\mathfrak A$ and $\mu_i\in\mathfrak M$; then the $\alpha_i$ form a basis of $\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M$.

The least number of rows of $\mathfrak A$ that must be adjoined to $\mathfrak M$ in order to obtain $(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M)$ is $0$ if $\mathfrak A$ is divisible by $\mathfrak M$; otherwise it is the least possible number of rows in a basis of $\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M$. This number is unchanged if $\mathfrak M$ is replaced by $(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M)$.

\paragraph{43. Relative rank.}
Define
\[
\Rg\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M=r
\]
to mean that $r$ is the least number such that between any $r+1$ rows $\alpha_1,
\ldots,
\alpha_{r+1}$ of $\mathfrak A$ there is a congruence
\begin{equation}
 c_1\alpha_1+
\cdots+c_{r+1}\alpha_{r+1}\equiv0\pmod{\mathfrak M}
\tag{60}
\end{equation}
with not all coefficients zero. Then
\begin{equation}
\Rg\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M=
\Rg(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M)\mid\mathfrak M.
\tag{62}
\end{equation}
If $\mathfrak A$ divides $\mathfrak M$, $\Rg\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M=r$, and $\Rg\mathfrak M=s$, then
\[
\Rg\mathfrak A=r+s.
\]
The proof chooses rows $\alpha_1,
\ldots,
\alpha_r$ in $\mathfrak A$ without a linear congruence modulo $\mathfrak M$ and rows $\mu_1,
\ldots,
\mu_s$ in $\mathfrak M$ without a linear equation. There is no linear equation among the combined $r+s$ rows; conversely every row $\alpha$ of $\mathfrak A$ can be expressed fractionally from them by using a congruence
\begin{equation}
 c\alpha\equiv c_1\alpha_1+
\cdots+c_r\alpha_r\pmod{\mathfrak M}
\tag{65}
\end{equation}
and then an ordinary linear equation in $\mathfrak M$. For arbitrary modules of the same degree this yields
\begin{equation}
\Rg\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M=
\Rg(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M)-\Rg\mathfrak M.
\tag{69}
\end{equation}
Replacing each module by another belonging to the same fundamental module leaves the relative rank unchanged:
\begin{equation}
\Rg\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M=
\Rg\overline{\mathfrak A}\mid\overline{\mathfrak M}.
\tag{70}
\end{equation}
Thus $\Rg\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M=0$ is precisely the condition that $\mathfrak A$ be a multiple of $\mathfrak M$; and if it holds, then $\overline{\mathfrak A}$ is divisible by $\overline{\mathfrak M}$.

\section*{\S 7. The divisibility conditions}

\paragraph{44.}
A module of rank $r$ has a basis of $r+1$ rows. If it belongs to the principal class, an irreducible basis may consist of only $r$ rows. In general, when forming a basis of $r+1$ rows for a module $\mathfrak A$ of rank $r$, one row within $\mathfrak A$ may be chosen arbitrarily; if $\mathfrak A$ is not in the principal class, that row must be chosen nonzero.

Let $\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_{r+1}$ be a basis of $\mathfrak A$ and let $\alpha\ne0$ be a row of $\mathfrak A$. Then
\begin{align}
\alpha&=a_1\sigma_1+
\cdots+a_{r+1}\sigma_{r+1},
\tag{71}\\
0&=b_1\sigma_1+
\cdots+b_{r+1}\sigma_{r+1},
\tag{72}
\end{align}
with not all $b_i$ zero. The $b_i$ may be normalized so that their gcd is relatively prime to the gcd of the $a_i$. The linear forms $a_ix+b_iy$ can then be made coprime by a suitable choice $x=x_0$, $y=y_0$:
\begin{equation}
\beta_i=a_ix_0+b_iy_0.
\tag{73}
\end{equation}
Hence $\mathfrak A$ has a basis consisting of the prescribed row $\alpha$ and $r$ further rows of $\mathfrak A$.

\paragraph{45. Level of a module relative to another.}
The notation $\St\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M$ is used only when $\Rg\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M=0$, that is, when $\mathfrak A$ is divisible by $\mathfrak M$. It denotes the least number of rows that must be adjoined to $\mathfrak M$ to obtain $(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M)$. It satisfies
\begin{equation}
\St\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M=\St(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M)\mid\mathfrak M,
\tag{74}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\St\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M\le \Rg\mathfrak A.
\tag{75}
\end{equation}
The latter follows by choosing a nonzero row $\alpha$ of $\mathfrak A$, multiplying it so that $c\alpha\in\mathfrak M$, and using No. 44 to form a basis of $\mathfrak A$ from $c\alpha$ and at most $r=\Rg\mathfrak A$ further rows.

\paragraph{46.}
Let $\Rg\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M=0$ and $\Rg\mathfrak B\mid\mathfrak M=0$. If $\mathfrak A$ divides $\mathfrak B$, then
\begin{equation}
\St\mathfrak B\mid\mathfrak M\le \St\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak M.
\tag{76}
\end{equation}
After replacing $\mathfrak A$ and $\mathfrak B$ by $(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak M)$ and $(\mathfrak B,
\mathfrak M)$, choose bases $\alpha_1,
\ldots,
\alpha_s$ of $\mathfrak A'\mid\mathfrak M$ and $\beta_1,
\ldots,
\beta_t$ of $\mathfrak B'\mid\mathfrak M$. Since the $\beta_i$ also belong to $\mathfrak A'$, they are congruent modulo $\mathfrak M$ to linear combinations of the $\alpha_k$. These combinations span a module $\mathfrak B''$ of rank at most $s$, but by (75) its level over $\mathfrak M$ is $t$. Hence $t\le s$.

\paragraph{47.}
Let $\mathfrak A$ be a module of degree $n$ and let $\mathfrak m\ne0$ be an ideal. Then $\Rg\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak m\mathfrak E_n=0$. If $\mathfrak e_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak e_n$ are the elementary divisors of $\mathfrak A$, then
\[
\St\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak m\mathfrak E_n
=
\#\{k:\mathfrak e_k\text{ is not divisible by }\mathfrak m\}.
\]
Writing $\mathfrak A$ in the normal form
\[
\mathfrak A=(\mathfrak e_1\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak e_n\overline{\sigma_n}),
\quad
(\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\overline{\sigma_n})=\mathfrak E_n,
\]
reduces the assertion to counting those elementary divisors of $(\mathfrak A,
\mathfrak m\mathfrak E_n)$ that are proper divisors of $\mathfrak m$.

\paragraph{48.}
Let $\mathfrak A$ and $\mathfrak B$ be modules of degree $n$, and suppose that $\mathfrak A$ divides $\mathfrak B$. If $\mathfrak a_k$ and $\mathfrak b_k$ are the corresponding elementary divisors of $\mathfrak A$ and $\mathfrak B$, then every $\mathfrak b_k$ is divisible by $\mathfrak a_k$.

Indeed, if $\mathfrak a_k\ne0$, then by No. 46
\begin{equation}
\St\mathfrak B\mid\mathfrak a_k\mathfrak E_n
\le
\St\mathfrak A\mid\mathfrak a_k\mathfrak E_n.
\tag{81}
\end{equation}
The right-hand side is at most $k-1$, so at most $k-1$ elementary divisors of $\mathfrak B$ can fail to be divisible by $\mathfrak a_k$. Thus $\mathfrak b_k$ is divisible by $\mathfrak a_k$.

\paragraph{49. The divisibility conditions.}
For the rectangle $B$ to be divisible by the rectangle $A$, it is necessary that every elementary divisor of $B$ be divisible by the corresponding elementary divisor of $A$. This condition is also sufficient if $\Rg A>\Rg B$. If $\Rg A=\Rg B=r$, one further condition is necessary and sufficient: if $\mathfrak d_r$ and $\mathfrak b_r$ are the $r$-th determinant-divisors of $A$ and $B$, then the integral ideal $\mathfrak b_r:\mathfrak d_r$ must have a divisor belonging to the class $\Kkl B:\Kkl A$.

Necessity follows by assuming
\begin{equation}
B=PAQ.
\tag{82}
\end{equation}
For transposed rectangles $B^*=Q^*(PA)^*$, so each elementary divisor of $B$ is divisible by the corresponding one of $A$. If the ranks are both $r$, then also $\Rg(PA)=r$ and
\begin{equation}
\Zkl A=\Zkl PA,
\qquad
\Kkl PA=\Kkl B.
\tag{83}
\end{equation}
Together with the determinant class relations
\begin{equation}
\Zkl A\cdot\Kkl A=\Kl\mathfrak d,
\quad
\Zkl PA\cdot\Kkl PA=\Kl\mathfrak d'',
\quad
\Zkl B\cdot\Kkl B=\Kl\mathfrak d',
\tag{84}
\end{equation}
this gives a divisor of $\mathfrak d':\mathfrak d$ in the class $\Kkl B:\Kkl A$.

For sufficiency, let $\mathfrak a_k,
\mathfrak d_k$ be the elementary and determinant-divisors of $A$, and $\mathfrak b_k,
\mathfrak b_k'$ those of $B$. Let $\Rg A=r$, $\Rg B=r'$, $\Kkl A=x$, $\Kkl B=x'$. Assume that $\mathfrak a_k$ divides $\mathfrak b_k$, and that either $r>r'$ or, when $r=r'$, $\mathfrak b_r':\mathfrak d_r$ has a divisor in the class $x':x$.

In the first case $r>r'$, choose ideals $\mathfrak q_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak q_r$ with
\begin{equation}
\Kl(\mathfrak q_1\cdots\mathfrak q_r)=\Kl\mathfrak d_r:x,
\qquad
\Kl\mathfrak q_i=\Kl\mathfrak a_i.
\tag{85}
\end{equation}
Using No. 40, choose rows of a sufficiently large degree with these greatest factors and the prescribed determinant-divisor. The associated principal-class modules can then be arranged so that one obtains bases $A'$ and $B'$ equivalent to $A$ and $B$, with $B'$ divisible by $A'$.

In the second case $r=r'$, take a divisor $\mathfrak t$ of $\mathfrak b_r':\mathfrak d_r$ in the class $x':x$. Choose ideals $\mathfrak c_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak c_r$ such that $\mathfrak c_k$ is divisible by $\mathfrak a_k$ and divides $\mathfrak b_k$, the $\mathfrak c_k$ divide successively, and $\mathfrak c_1\cdots\mathfrak c_r=\mathfrak d_r\mathfrak t$. Then write
\[
\mathfrak A=(\mathfrak a_1\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak a_r\overline{\sigma_r}),
\qquad
\mathfrak B=(\mathfrak b_1\overline{\tau_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak b_r\overline{\tau_r}),
\]
and set
\[
\mathfrak C=(\mathfrak c_1\overline{\sigma_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak c_r\overline{\sigma_r}),
\qquad
\mathfrak D=(\mathfrak c_1\overline{\tau_1},
\ldots,
\mathfrak c_r\overline{\tau_r}).
\]
Then $\mathfrak C$ is a multiple of $\mathfrak A$, $\mathfrak D$ a divisor of $\mathfrak B$, and the corresponding rectangles $C$ and $D^*$ have the same column-class $x'$ and the same elementary divisors. They are therefore equivalent. Since $B$ is divisible by $D^*$ and $C$ by $A$, $B$ is divisible by $A$.

\section*{\S 8. Ideal systems}

\paragraph{50. Ideal systems.}
In No. 22 Steinitz defined the addition of two rows of degree $n$ and the multiplication of a row by a number. Thus all rows of degree $n$ constitute a system $\mathfrak E$ with two laws of composition. First, two elements $\alpha,
\beta$ determine a sum $\alpha+
\beta$; addition is associative and commutative, and for every pair $\alpha,
\beta$ there is a unique $\gamma$ with $\beta+
\gamma=\alpha$, written $\alpha-
\beta$. Second, an element $\alpha$ and an integer $a$ of $K$ determine $a\alpha$, with
\[
1\alpha=\alpha,
\quad a(b\alpha)=(ab)\alpha,
\quad a(\alpha+
\beta)=a\alpha+a\beta,
\quad (a+b)\alpha=a\alpha+b\alpha.
\]
Steinitz henceforth calls an \emph{ideal system belonging to the field $K$} any system whose elements possess these two operations--addition and multiplication by an integer of $K$--with the properties just listed.

\section*{\S 8. Ideal systems, continued}

\paragraph{50. Ideal systems.}
The laws listed in No. 50 imply, for every ideal system $\mathfrak J$, the existence of an element $0$ such that
\[
  \alpha+0=0+\alpha=\alpha
\]
for all $\alpha$. If $\mathfrak J$ consists only of this element, we also write $\mathfrak J=0$.

It is clear from No. 23 that every module is an ideal system, and that the modules of degree $n$ are the only ideal systems obtainable from rows of degree $n$, provided the laws of composition of No. 22 are used. In the case $n=1$ the rows are replaced by the individual integers of $K$; a system of integers of $K$ is an ideal system in the present sense precisely when it forms an ideal in Dedekind's usual sense.

\paragraph{51. Isomorphism.}
Two ideal systems $\mathfrak J,\mathfrak J_1$ are called \emph{isomorphic}, or \emph{of the same type}, when there is a one-to-one correspondence between them satisfying the following two requirements:

1. If the elements $\alpha,\beta$ of $\mathfrak J$ correspond to $\alpha_1,\beta_1$ of $\mathfrak J_1$, then $\alpha+\beta$ and $\alpha_1+\beta_1$ correspond.

2. If $\alpha$ and $\alpha_1$ correspond, and $a$ is any integer of $K$, then $a\alpha$ and $a\alpha_1$ correspond.

The module-theoretic notions introduced above are now to be phrased so that they make sense for arbitrary ideal systems. Of special importance are those properties which depend only on the type of the system, and hence remain unchanged after replacing the system by an isomorphic one.

\paragraph{52. Ideal subsystems.}
A subsystem $\mathfrak T$ of an ideal system $\mathfrak J$ is itself an ideal system if and only if each multiple $a\alpha$ of an element $\alpha\in\mathfrak T$ and each sum of two elements of $\mathfrak T$ again belongs to $\mathfrak T$. The system $\mathfrak J$ itself is counted among its subsystems. When $\mathfrak J$ is a module, its ideal subsystems are exactly its multiples. An isomorphism between ideal systems carries every ideal subsystem of the first to an ideal subsystem of the second.

Let $S$ be a finite or infinite family of ideal systems which are all subsystems of one and the same ideal system $\mathfrak J$. Then the system $\mathfrak B$ of all elements common to the systems in $S$ is again an ideal system. If the systems in $S$ are modules of degree $n$, hence subsystems of $\mathfrak E_n$, then $\mathfrak B$ is their least common multiple.

If $\mathfrak C$ is an ideal or non-ideal subsystem of $\mathfrak J$, then among the ideal subsystems of $\mathfrak J$ that contain $\mathfrak C$ there is a smallest one. If $\mathfrak C$ consists of finitely many elements
\[
  \alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m,
\]
this smallest ideal subsystem consists of all elements of the form
\begin{equation}
  c_1\alpha_1+\cdots+c_m\alpha_m,
\tag{87}
\end{equation}
where the $c_i$ run through all integers of $K$. Generalizing the earlier definition, a subsystem $\mathfrak C$ of an ideal system $\mathfrak J$ is called a \emph{basis} of $\mathfrak J$ when no ideal subsystem of $\mathfrak J$ other than $\mathfrak J$ itself contains all elements of $\mathfrak C$.

If $\mathfrak J$ contains the ideal subsystems $\mathfrak A^{(1)},\ldots,\mathfrak A^{(k)}$, then
\[
  (\mathfrak A^{(1)},\ldots,\mathfrak A^{(k)})
\]
denotes the smallest ideal subsystem of $\mathfrak J$ that contains them all. If $\mathfrak J$ is an ideal system and $\mathfrak m$ an ideal, then $\mathfrak m\mathfrak J$ denotes the smallest ideal subsystem of $\mathfrak J$ containing all elements $m\alpha$, with $\alpha\in\mathfrak J$ and $m$ running through the numbers divisible by $\mathfrak m$. In the module case this agrees with the earlier definition.

\paragraph{53. Congruences and residue-class systems.}
Let $\mathfrak M$ be an ideal subsystem of the ideal system $\mathfrak J$. Two elements $\alpha,\beta$ of $\mathfrak J$ are called \emph{congruent modulo $\mathfrak M$} if $\alpha-\beta$ belongs to $\mathfrak M$. The elements of $\mathfrak J$ thereby fall into residue classes. If $\alpha$ runs through a residue class $A$ and $\beta$ through a residue class $B$, then $\alpha+\beta$ lies in a fixed residue class, denoted $A+B$. If $\alpha$ runs through $A$ and $a$ is a fixed integer, then $a\alpha$ runs through a fixed residue class, denoted $aA$. Thus the residue classes of $\mathfrak J$ modulo $\mathfrak M$ themselves form an ideal system, denoted $\mathfrak J/\mathfrak M$.

If $\mathfrak A$ is an ideal subsystem of $\mathfrak J$, the residue classes modulo $\mathfrak M$ that contain elements of $\mathfrak A$ form an ideal subsystem of $\mathfrak J/\mathfrak M$, denoted $\mathfrak A/\mathfrak M$. In particular,
\[
  (\mathfrak A,\mathfrak M)/\mathfrak M=\mathfrak A/\mathfrak M.
\]

\paragraph{54. Rank and stage.}
Let $R(\alpha)$ denote the residue class modulo $\mathfrak M$ containing $\alpha$. A congruence
\[
  a_1\alpha_1+\cdots+a_n\alpha_n\equiv0\pmod{\mathfrak M}
\]
is equivalent to
\[
  a_1R(\alpha_1)+\cdots+a_nR(\alpha_n)=0.
\]
If each element $\alpha$ of $\mathfrak A$ has a representation
\[
  \alpha\equiv c_1\alpha_1+\cdots+c_m\alpha_m\pmod{\mathfrak M},
\]
then each element $R(\alpha)$ of $\mathfrak A/\mathfrak M$ has the representation
\[
  R(\alpha)=c_1R(\alpha_1)+\cdots+c_mR(\alpha_m).
\]
Thus the former notions of basis, rank, and stage for $\mathfrak A/\mathfrak M$ agree with the general ones.

For an ideal system $\mathfrak J$, define $\Rg\mathfrak J=r$ as the least integer such that any $r+1$ elements $\alpha_1,
\ldots,\alpha_{r+1}$ of $\mathfrak J$ satisfy a relation
\[
  a_1\alpha_1+\cdots+a_{r+1}\alpha_{r+1}=0
\]
with not all coefficients zero. If no such number exists, $\mathfrak J$ is said to have infinite rank. When $\mathfrak J$ has rank $0$, define $\St\mathfrak J=s$ to be the least number of elements in a basis of $\mathfrak J$; for $\mathfrak J=0$ put $\St\mathfrak J=0$. If no finite basis exists, the system is said to have infinite basis.

\paragraph{55. Order of an element.}
For $\alpha\in\mathfrak J$, the integers $x$ of $K$ satisfying
\[
  x\alpha=0
\]
form an ideal $\mathfrak a$, called the \emph{order} of $\alpha$. The order is $0$ if $x\alpha=0$ only for $x=0$. The zero element is the only element of order $1$.

If $\alpha$ has order $0$ and $c\ne0$, then $c\alpha$ also has order $0$. If the order of $\alpha$ is a non-zero ideal $\mathfrak a$, then the order of $c\alpha$ is
\[
  \frac{\mathfrak a}{(c,\mathfrak a)},
\]
therefore a divisor of $\mathfrak a$. If the non-zero orders of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are $\mathfrak a$ and $\mathfrak b$, and $\mathfrak c$ is their least common multiple, then the order of $\alpha+\beta$ divides $\mathfrak c$. If $\mathfrak a$ and $\mathfrak b$ are relatively prime, the order of $\alpha+\beta$ is $\mathfrak a\mathfrak b$.

Let $\mathfrak a$ be the order of $\alpha$, let $\mathfrak p$ be a prime ideal, and let $\mathfrak p^e$ be the highest power of $\mathfrak p$ contained in $\mathfrak a$, so that $\mathfrak a=\mathfrak p^e\mathfrak b$ and $\mathfrak p^e$ is relatively prime to $\mathfrak b$. Then
\begin{equation}
  x\equiv1\pmod{\mathfrak p^e},
  \qquad
  x\equiv0\pmod{\mathfrak b}
\tag{88}
\end{equation}
has exactly one solution modulo $\mathfrak a$. If $x=c$ is such a solution, then $c\alpha$ is determined by $\alpha$ and $\mathfrak p$; denote it $\alpha_{\mathfrak p}$. Its order is $\mathfrak p^e$. If $\mathfrak p$ does not divide $\mathfrak a$, then $\alpha_{\mathfrak p}=0$.

For
\[
  \mathfrak a=\mathfrak p_1^{e_1}\mathfrak p_2^{e_2}\cdots\mathfrak p_t^{e_t}
\]
one has
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\mathfrak p}\alpha_{\mathfrak p}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{\mathfrak p_i}.
\tag{89}
\end{equation}
Here we may put
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_{\mathfrak p_i}=c_i\alpha,
\tag{90}
\end{equation}
where the $c_i$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
  c_i\equiv1\pmod{\mathfrak p_i^{e_i}},\qquad
  c_i\equiv0\pmod{\mathfrak p_k^{e_k}}
  \quad (i,k=1,\ldots,t;\ i\ne k).
\tag{91}
\end{equation}
Then
\[
  \sum_{\mathfrak p}\alpha_{\mathfrak p}
  =\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t}c_i\right)\alpha,
  \qquad
  \sum_{i=1}^{t}c_i\equiv1\pmod{\mathfrak a},
\]
and hence $\sum_{\mathfrak p}\alpha_{\mathfrak p}=\alpha$.

An element is called \emph{primary} if its order is a prime-power ideal. The elements $\alpha_{\mathfrak p}$ are the primary components of $\alpha$. Every element of non-zero order is the sum of its primary components, and the order of $\alpha_{\mathfrak p}$ is the highest power of $\mathfrak p$ occurring in the order of $\alpha$.

\paragraph{56. Two distinguished species.}
Two classes of ideal systems are singled out: systems of rank $0$, in which no element has order $0$, and systems in which every non-zero element has order $0$. The zero system belongs to both classes.

The elements of an ideal system $\mathfrak J$ whose order is not $0$ form an ideal subsystem, denoted $\mathfrak J'$. In $\mathfrak J/\mathfrak J'$, every non-zero element has order $0$. An ideal system is called \emph{primary} if the orders of all its elements are powers of one and the same prime ideal. The elements of $\mathfrak J'$ whose orders are powers of a given prime ideal $\mathfrak p$ form an ideal system $\mathfrak J_{\mathfrak p}$, called a primary component of $\mathfrak J'$. Every element of $\mathfrak J'$ has a unique representation as a sum of elements from the components $\mathfrak J_{\mathfrak p}$.

More explicitly, if $\eta_i$ are elements from pairwise distinct components and
\begin{equation}
  \eta_1+\cdots+\eta_t=\alpha,
\tag{92}
\end{equation}
then only the corresponding prime ideals divide the order of $\alpha$, so
\begin{equation}
  \alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{\mathfrak p_i}.
\tag{93}
\end{equation}
Equations (92) and (93) give the uniqueness of the primary decomposition.

\paragraph{57. Addition of types of ideal systems.}
When only the type of an ideal system matters, write $[\mathfrak J]$. Thus $[\mathfrak J]=[\mathfrak J_1]$ means that $\mathfrak J$ and $\mathfrak J_1$ are isomorphic.

Let $\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B,\mathfrak C$ be ideal subsystems of an ideal system $\mathfrak J$, and suppose every element $\omega$ of $\mathfrak J$ has a unique representation
\begin{equation}
  \omega=\alpha+\beta+\gamma,
\tag{94}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha\in\mathfrak A$, $\beta\in\mathfrak B$, $\gamma\in\mathfrak C$. Replacing the three subsystems by isomorphic ones yields an isomorphic ideal system. Hence $[\mathfrak J]$ is determined by $[\mathfrak A]$, $[\mathfrak B]$, and $[\mathfrak C]$, and Steinitz writes
\[
  [\mathfrak J]=[\mathfrak A]+[\mathfrak B]+[\mathfrak C].
\]
Conversely, this addition may be defined for arbitrary types by forming rows $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$ with components from representatives of the three types. The operation is associative and commutative, and $[0]$ is its identity element.

\paragraph{58. Ideal systems with finite basis.}
Only ideal systems with finite basis are considered from now on. Every module has such a basis; so does every system $\mathfrak A/\mathfrak M$, where $\mathfrak A$ and $\mathfrak M$ are modules of the same degree. Conversely, every ideal system with finite basis is isomorphic to such a residue-class system.

If $\mathfrak J$ has basis
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n,
\tag{95}
\end{equation}
then each element of $\mathfrak J$ has the form $x_1\alpha_1+\cdots+x_n\alpha_n$. Assign to a row $\sigma=(x_1,
\ldots,x_n)$ the element
\[
  \psi(\sigma)=x_1\alpha_1+\cdots+x_n\alpha_n.
\]
The rows with $\psi(\sigma)=0$ form a module $\mathfrak M$ of degree $n$, and $\psi(\sigma)=\psi(\tau)$ exactly when $\sigma\equiv\tau\pmod{\mathfrak M}$. Thus $\mathfrak J$ is isomorphic to $\mathfrak E_n/\mathfrak M$.

The ideal subsystems $\mathfrak T$ of $\mathfrak J$ correspond one-to-one to the divisors $\mathfrak A$ of $\mathfrak M$, with $\mathfrak T$ isomorphic to $\mathfrak A/\mathfrak M$. Likewise $\mathfrak J/\mathfrak T$ is isomorphic to $\mathfrak E_n/\mathfrak A$. In particular, such subsystems and quotient systems also possess finite bases.

\paragraph{59. Finite systems and systems of module type.}
Let $\varphi(\mathfrak A)$ be the subsystem corresponding to a divisor $\mathfrak A$ of $\mathfrak M$. Then
\begin{equation}
  \varphi(\mathfrak M)=\mathfrak J,
  \qquad
  \varphi(\overline{\mathfrak M})=0.
\tag{96}
\end{equation}
With $\overline{\mathfrak M}$ denoting the principal module associated with $\mathfrak M$, one gets
\begin{equation}
  \varphi(\mathfrak M)=\mathfrak J',
\tag{97}
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation}
  [\mathfrak J']=[\mathfrak M/\overline{\mathfrak M}],
  \qquad
  [\mathfrak J/\mathfrak J']=[\mathfrak E_n/\overline{\mathfrak M}].
\tag{98}
\end{equation}
The systems with finite basis and rank $0$ are exactly the finite systems. Indeed, when $\mathfrak J$ has rank $0$, the corresponding $\mathfrak M$ has equal degree and rank; conversely a finite system cannot contain an element of order $0$.

Assume now that $\mathfrak J\ne\mathfrak J'$ and set $\Rg\mathfrak M=r<n$. Choose a principal module $\mathfrak N$ of degree $n$ and rank $n-r$ such that
\begin{equation}
  (\mathfrak M,\mathfrak N)=\mathfrak E_n.
\tag{99}
\end{equation}
Each row $\sigma$ of degree $n$ is uniquely expressible as
\begin{equation}
  \sigma=u+v,
\tag{100}
\end{equation}
with $u\in\mathfrak M$ and $v\in\mathfrak N$. Therefore
\begin{equation}
  [\mathfrak N]=[\mathfrak E_n/\mathfrak M]=[\mathfrak J/\mathfrak J']
\tag{101}
\end{equation}
and some subsystem $\mathfrak T$ of $\mathfrak J$ is isomorphic to $\mathfrak N$:
\begin{equation}
  [\mathfrak T]=[\mathfrak J/\mathfrak J'].
\tag{102}
\end{equation}
The decomposition
\begin{equation}
  \psi(\sigma)=\psi(u)+\psi(v)
\tag{103}
\end{equation}
is unique, so
\begin{equation}
  [\mathfrak J]=[\mathfrak J']+[\mathfrak J/\mathfrak J'].
\tag{104}
\end{equation}
The first term is finite and the second is of module type. This decomposition into a finite type and a module type is unique. Indeed, if
\begin{equation}
  \gamma=\alpha+\beta
\tag{105}
\end{equation}
uniquely decomposes elements with $\alpha$ in a finite part and $\beta$ in a module-type part, the finite part must be $\mathfrak J'$.

\paragraph{60. Decomposition of finite types into primary types.}
For a finite type $[\mathfrak J]$ one has a unique decomposition into primary components:
\[
  [\mathfrak J]=\sum_{\mathfrak p}[\mathfrak J_{\mathfrak p}],
\]
with only finitely many non-zero summands. Adding primary types belonging to one prime ideal again gives a primary type; the components belonging to distinct prime ideals are fixed independently.

\paragraph{61. Decomposition of finite types into one-stage types.}
If $\mathfrak A$ is finite, $\St\mathfrak A=1$, $\alpha$ is a basis, and $\mathfrak a$ is the order of $\alpha$, then $\mathfrak A$ is isomorphic to the residue-class system modulo $\mathfrak a$. Denote this one-stage type by $T_{\mathfrak a}$; call $\mathfrak a$ its order. If
\[
  \mathfrak a=\mathfrak p_1^{e_1}\cdots\mathfrak p_t^{e_t},
\]
then
\[
  T_{\mathfrak a}=T_{\mathfrak p_1^{e_1}}+\cdots+T_{\mathfrak p_t^{e_t}}.
\]

Let $\mathfrak J$ be finite with basis $\alpha_1,
\ldots,
\alpha_n$. Then $\mathfrak J$ is isomorphic to $\mathfrak E_n/\mathfrak M$ for a module $\mathfrak M$ of degree and rank $n$. If
\begin{equation}
  \mathfrak M=(\mathfrak a_1\tau_1,
  \ldots,
  \mathfrak a_n\tau_n)
\tag{106}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
  \mathfrak E_n=(\tau_1,
  \ldots,
  \tau_n),
\tag{107}
\end{equation}
then each row is uniquely
\begin{equation}
  x=x_1+\cdots+x_n,
\tag{108}
\end{equation}
with $x_i\in\tau_i$. Consequently each element $\alpha\in\mathfrak J$ has a unique decomposition
\begin{equation}
  \alpha=\alpha^{(1)}+\cdots+\alpha^{(n)},
\tag{109}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  [\mathfrak J]=[\mathfrak A_1]+\cdots+[\mathfrak A_n].
\tag{110}
\end{equation}
Choosing in each $\tau_i$ a row whose greatest factor is relatively prime to $\mathfrak a_i$ gives a basis element of order $\mathfrak a_i$; hence
\begin{equation}
  [\mathfrak J]=T_{\mathfrak a_1}+\cdots+T_{\mathfrak a_s},
\tag{111}
\end{equation}
where the non-unit ideals $\mathfrak a_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak a_s$ are ordered so that each divides the next. In a representation
\begin{equation}
  \alpha=x_1\alpha_1+\cdots+x_s\alpha_s,
\tag{112}
\end{equation}
each coefficient $x_i$ runs through a complete residue system modulo $\mathfrak a_i$.

The number of elements whose order divides an ideal $\mathfrak m$ is
\[
  \Nm((\mathfrak m,\mathfrak a_1)\cdots(\mathfrak m,\mathfrak a_s)).
\]
If there were another decomposition
\[
  [\mathfrak J]=T_{\mathfrak b_1}+\cdots+T_{\mathfrak b_{s'}},
\]
then, for every ideal $\mathfrak m$,
\begin{equation}
  \Nm((\mathfrak m,\mathfrak a_1)\cdots(\mathfrak m,\mathfrak a_s))
  =
  \Nm((\mathfrak m,\mathfrak b_1)\cdots(\mathfrak m,\mathfrak b_{s'})).
\tag{113}
\end{equation}
Extending both sequences by unit ideals and taking $n\ge s,s'$, this becomes
\begin{equation}
  \Nm((\mathfrak m,\mathfrak a_1)\cdots(\mathfrak m,\mathfrak a_n))
  =
  \Nm((\mathfrak m,\mathfrak b_1)\cdots(\mathfrak m,\mathfrak b_n)).
\tag{114}
\end{equation}
Putting for $\mathfrak m$ an ideal divisible by $\mathfrak a_1$ and $\mathfrak b_1$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \Nm(\mathfrak a_1\cdots\mathfrak a_n)
  =
  \Nm(\mathfrak b_1\cdots\mathfrak b_n).
\tag{115}
\end{equation}
Putting $\mathfrak m=\mathfrak a_1$ then shows that $\mathfrak b_1$ divides $\mathfrak a_1$; by symmetry $\mathfrak a_1=\mathfrak b_1$, and iteration gives equality of all invariants. Thus the ideals $\mathfrak a_1,\mathfrak a_2,
\ldots$ are invariants of $\mathfrak J$, and equality of invariants is necessary and sufficient for isomorphism of finite ideal systems. Also $\St\mathfrak J=s$.

If $\mathfrak M$ is a module of equal degree and rank, then the elementary divisors of $\mathfrak M$ different from $0$ and $1$ are exactly the invariants different from $1$ of $\mathfrak E_n/\mathfrak M$.

\paragraph{62. Invariants of ideal subsystems.}
Let ideals $\mathfrak n_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak n_s$ be given. Restricting in (112) the coefficient of $\alpha_i$ to numbers divisible by $\mathfrak n_i$ yields an ideal subsystem whose invariants are
\[
  \frac{\mathfrak a_i}{(\mathfrak n_i,\mathfrak a_i)},
\]
provided these ideals again occur in divisibility order. Hence any sequence $\mathfrak b_i$ such that $\mathfrak b_i$ divides $\mathfrak b_{i+1}$ and divides $\mathfrak a_i$ occurs as the invariant sequence of at least one ideal subsystem of $\mathfrak J$.

Conversely, the invariants of every ideal subsystem of $\mathfrak J$ divide the corresponding invariants of $\mathfrak J$. In particular, $\mathfrak n\mathfrak J$ has invariants
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathfrak a_i}{(\mathfrak n,\mathfrak a_i)}\quad (i=1,2,
\ldots).
\tag{117}
\end{equation}
Thus $\St(\mathfrak n\mathfrak J)<k$ holds exactly when $\mathfrak n$ is divisible by the $k$-th invariant of $\mathfrak J$. The $k$-th invariant may therefore be characterized as the smallest ideal satisfying this condition.

\paragraph{63. Irreducible types.}
A non-zero type is called irreducible if it is not a sum of two non-zero types. Every finite type can be decomposed into irreducibles. Such an irreducible finite type must be primary and one-stage, and conversely every primary one-stage type is irreducible. The decomposition of a finite type into irreducibles is unique. If $\mathfrak a_i$ are the invariants of $[\mathfrak J]$ and $\mathfrak a_{i,\mathfrak p}$ is the largest power of $\mathfrak p$ contained in $\mathfrak a_i$, then
\[
  [\mathfrak J]=\sum_{i,\mathfrak p}T_{\mathfrak a_{i,\mathfrak p}},
\]
where only non-unit ideals contribute. Among finite types, exactly the primary one-stage types are irreducible.

\paragraph{64. Module types.}
Two modules, of the same or of different degrees, are isomorphic if and only if they have the same rank and the same class.

Let $\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B$ be modules, $\Rg\mathfrak A=r$, $\Kl\mathfrak A=x$. Choose numbers $c,c'$ whose greatest common divisor belongs to the class $x$, and choose a normal basis $\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_r,
\sigma'_r$ of $\mathfrak A$ such that
\[
  c\sigma_r+c'\sigma'_r=0.
\]
This is the only relation among those basis rows. Under an isomorphism the row
\begin{equation}
  x_1\sigma_1+\cdots+x_r\sigma_r+x'_r\sigma'_r
\tag{118}
\end{equation}
corresponds to
\begin{equation}
  x_1\tau_1+\cdots+x_r\tau_r+x'_r\tau'_r,
\tag{119}
\end{equation}
and equality of rank and class follows. Conversely, if rank and class agree, such bases can be chosen and the correspondence (118)--(119) is an isomorphism.

Thus each natural number $r$ and ideal class $x$ determine a module type, denoted $U_{r,x}$, and for $r=1$ also $U_x$. If $\mathfrak a_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak a_r$ are ideals and $\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\sigma_r$ is a basis of $\mathfrak E_r$, then for
\[
  \mathfrak M=(\mathfrak a_1\sigma_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak a_r\sigma_r)
\]
one has
\begin{equation}
  [\mathfrak M]=[\mathfrak a_1\sigma_1]+\cdots+[\mathfrak a_r\sigma_r]
\tag{120}
\end{equation}
and consequently
\begin{equation}
  U_{r,x}=U_{x_1}+\cdots+U_{x_r}
  \quad (x_1\cdots x_r=x).
\tag{121}
\end{equation}
Among module types, those of rank $1$ and only those are irreducible. Every module type of rank $r$ is the sum of $r$ irreducible module types; $r-1$ of these may be chosen arbitrarily, and the last is then determined. In general,
\[
  U_{r,x}+U_{s,y}=U_{r+s,xy}.
\]

\paragraph{65. Arbitrary types with finite basis.}
Let $\mathfrak J$ be any ideal system with finite basis, $\mathfrak J'$ the subsystem of elements of non-zero order, $\Rg(\mathfrak J/\mathfrak J')=r$, and $\St\mathfrak J'=s$. If $\mathfrak J$ is isomorphic to $\mathfrak E_n/\mathfrak M$, then the first $n-r$ elementary divisors of $\mathfrak M$ are non-zero. Put
\[
  \mathfrak a_i=\mathfrak e_i\quad (i=1,
\ldots,n-r),
  \qquad
  \mathfrak a_i=1\quad (i>n-r).
\]
These give the invariants of $\mathfrak J'$, and therefore
\begin{equation}
  n\ge r+s.
\tag{122}
\end{equation}
Let $x=\Kl(\mathfrak J/\mathfrak J')$ be the class of the module type and put $\mathfrak d=\mathfrak a_1\cdots\mathfrak a_s$. Then the type of $\mathfrak J$ is determined by
\begin{equation}
  r,
  \quad x,
  \quad \mathfrak a_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak a_s,
\tag{123}
\end{equation}
where the $\mathfrak a_i$ are the invariants of its finite part. Applied to residue-class systems of modules, this says: two such systems are isomorphic if and only if the elementary divisors agree up to units, the difference between degree and rank is the same, and the module classes agree. For modules of the same degree this reduces to equality of elementary divisors and class.

Each basis of $\mathfrak J$ gives a module whose residue-class system is isomorphic to $\mathfrak J$. From (122), a basis has at least $r+s$ elements. For $s>0$ there is an irreducible basis with exactly $r+s$ elements. Choose a basis $\alpha_1,
\ldots,
\alpha_s$ of $\mathfrak J'$ with orders $\mathfrak a_1,
\ldots,
\mathfrak a_s$, and a basis
\[
  \rho_1,
\ldots,
\rho_r,
\rho'_r
\]
of a subsystem $\mathfrak T$ isomorphic to $\mathfrak J/\mathfrak J'$ with relation $c\rho_r+c'\rho'_r=0$. Then
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_1,
\ldots,
\alpha_s,
\rho_1,
\ldots,
\rho_r,
\rho'_r
\tag{124}
\end{equation}
is a basis. For an element
\begin{equation}
  a\rho_r+a'\rho'_r+m\alpha_1,
\tag{125}
\end{equation}
if $\ell$ solves $c\ell=a-m\pmod{\mathfrak a_1}$, the same element can be written
\[
  a(\rho_r+\alpha_1)+a'\rho'_r.
\]
Thus in (124) the two elements $\rho_r$ and $\alpha_1$ may be replaced by the single element $\rho_r+\alpha_1$, giving a basis of $r+s$ elements.

\paragraph{66. Unimodular systems and equivalence.}
A unimodular system is a square system of integers of $K$ whose determinant is a unit. Unimodular systems of the same degree reproduce under multiplication, and the reciprocal system is again unimodular.

If $A$ is an $m$-rowed rectangle of degree $n$, $E$ a unimodular system of degree $m$, and $EA=B$, then $A$ and $B$ are left-equivalent. Conversely:

\emph{If $A$ and $B$ are left-equivalent rectangles with the same number of rows, then $B=EA$ with $E$ unimodular.}

More generally: if $A$ and $B$ are left-equivalent $m$-rowed rectangles of rank $r$, if $m>r$, and if $g$ is any integer of $K$, then there is a relation $B=GA$ in which $\det G=g$. Start with
\begin{equation}
  B=SA,
\tag{126}
\end{equation}
and, writing the rows of $A,B,S$ as $\alpha_i,\beta_i,\sigma_i$, put
\begin{equation}
  \beta_i=\sigma_iA\quad (i=1,
\ldots,m).
\tag{127}
\end{equation}
For $m=r+1$, the rows $z$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
  zA=0
\tag{128}
\end{equation}
form a principal module $\mathfrak T$ of degree $m$ and rank $1$. From basis rows of $\Md S$ together with $\mathfrak T$ one obtains a rectangle $T$ such that
\begin{equation}
  \Md T=\mathfrak E_m.
\tag{129}
\end{equation}
If $z,z'$ are a basis of $\mathfrak T$, if $\eta_{ik}$ are the cofactors of the entries $c_{ik}$ of $S$, and $d=\det S$, set
\begin{equation}
  u_i=\sum_k\eta_{ki}z_k,
  \qquad
  u'_i=\sum_k\eta_{ki}z'_k
  \quad (i=1,
\ldots,m).
\tag{130}
\end{equation}
Equation (129) gives
\begin{equation}
  (u_1,
\ldots,u_m,u'_1,
\ldots,u'_m)=1.
\tag{131}
\end{equation}
Hence numbers $q_i,q'_i$ may be chosen so that $\sum_i(q_i u_i+q'_i u'_i)=g$. Replacing the $i$-th row of $S$ by
\[
  \sigma_i-q_i z+q'_i z'
\]
yields a system $G$ with determinant $g$ and $GA=B$.

If $m>r+1$, use a normal basis of the solution module of $zA=0$ and unimodular systems $E,F$ to reduce to
\begin{equation}
  EA=C,
  \qquad
  FB=D,
\tag{132}
\end{equation}
where the last $m-r-1$ rows vanish. For the rectangles $C',D'$ formed from the first $r+1$ rows, the already proved case gives a system $G'$ with
\begin{equation}
  G'C'=D'.
\tag{133}
\end{equation}
Extending $G'$ by ones on the diagonal gives $(F^{-1}GE)A=B$, with determinant $g$. This proves the theorem. The right-equivalence statements are analogous. If $A$ and $B$ are equivalent rectangles with the same numbers of rows and columns, then $B=PAQ$ with square unimodular $P,Q$.

\paragraph{67. Extensions.}
The restriction to finite algebraic number fields may be removed. If $K$ is any field generated by algebraic numbers, the criteria for equivalence and divisibility of rectangular systems remain the same. Only the definition of module must explicitly require a finite basis. Accordingly, an ideal is any ideal subsystem of the integers of $K$ with finite basis. The division into ideal classes follows the same principle as for finite fields, although the number of classes need not be finite. If $K$ is the field of all algebraic numbers, the number of ideal classes is $1$. In such cases, divisibility of a rectangle $B$ by a rectangle $A$ is necessary and sufficient exactly when every elementary divisor of $B$ is divisible by the corresponding elementary divisor of $A$.

\paragraph{68. Earlier literature.}
The oldest investigations related to divisibility of rectangular systems go back to Stephen Smith. In \emph{On Systems of Linear Indeterminate Equations and Congruences}, London Philosophical Transactions 151 (1861), pp. 293--326, the concepts later called determinant-divisors and elementary divisors first appear for rectangular systems with integral entries in the rational number field; the proof that each elementary divisor divides the next is already present there. In 1873 Smith proved that equality of elementary divisors is not only necessary but also sufficient for equivalence of rectangular systems. The divisibility conditions were later completely settled by Frobenius.

The investigations in the section on ideal systems are, in the rational case, only formally different from the theory of groups with commuting elements, developed by Kronecker and by Frobenius--Stickelberger. The connection with module theory is already present there. In algebraic fields, the present paper appears to be the first to carry out the corresponding investigation. The importance of this extension is also shown by I. Schur's recent work on linear substitution groups, which uses results from the first part of the present paper.
