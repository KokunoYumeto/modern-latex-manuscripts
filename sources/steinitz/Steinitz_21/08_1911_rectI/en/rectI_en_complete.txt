only numbers and ideals of K are considered, and they are integral numbers and integral ideals.

3. The first question treated here is the criterion for equivalence of two systems A, B; in a
second article the more general question of divisibility of B by A is to be treated. Over the
rational field the answer is known. Over an arbitrary algebraic number field the final results
remain the same for square systems of nonzero determinant, although the proof requires other
tools. Once one allows nonsquare systems, or square systems of determinant 0, the results
themselves change.
4. Left and right divisibility are special cases. B is left-divisible by A if B = P A, and
right-divisible by A if B = AQ. Correspondingly, two systems are left- or right-equivalent if
each is left- or right-divisible by the other.

§ 4. Modules
22. From now on the degree of a rectangle means its number of columns. This applies in
particular when the rectangle consists of only one row; its degree is then the number of its
elements. We use small Greek letters for rows. The equation σ = 0 means that all elements of σ
are zero. A factor of a row σ is any ideal which divides every element of σ; the greatest common
divisor of the elements is called the greatest factor of σ. By aσ we mean the row obtained from
σ by multiplying its entries by the number a. If σ and τ have the same degree, then σ + τ and
σ − τ denote the rows obtained by componentwise addition and subtraction. If integral rows
σ, σ1 , σ2 , . . . , σm satisfy a relation
σ = a1 σ1 + · · · + am σm
with integral coefficients ai , then σ is said to be composable from σ1 , . . . , σm .
23. Modules. A system S of integral rows of degree n is called a module of degree n if it has
the following two properties:
1) if σ ∈ S, then aσ ∈ S for every integral
number a of K;
2) if σ, τ ∈ S, then σ + τ ∈ S.
Modules are denoted by capital German letters; a lower index gives the degree. The row σ = 0
by itself forms a module, the zero module.
Two rows σ, τ are congruent modulo a module An , written
σ≡τ

(mod An ),

if σ − τ belongs to the module. The module An is said to be divisible by the module Bn if every
row of An is also contained in Bn ; then Bn is a divisor of An , and An is a multiple of Bn . A
congruence which holds modulo An also holds modulo every divisor of An .
24. Rank, determinant divisors, and elementary divisors of a module. Let a module An be
given. Consider all rectangles A formed from rows of An . If among them there are rectangles of
rank r, but none of rank r + 1, then r is called the rank of the module, Rg An . For each natural
number k, the k-th determinant divisors of all such rectangles A have a greatest common divisor
bk ; this is the k-th determinant divisor of An . Clearly bk divides bk+1 , and if Rg An = r, then
the first r determinant divisors are nonzero and all following ones are zero. The elementary
divisors of a module are then defined as before for rectangular systems.
25. Basis of a module. If σ1 , σ2 , . . . , σm are rows of degree n, the collection of all rows
composable from them is plainly a module of degree n. Conversely, every module An is generated
by finitely many of its rows. Indeed, let Rg An = r, and let br be its r-th determinant divisor.
