\section*{Rectangular Systems and Modules in Algebraic Number Fields. I}
\begin{center}
By \emph{Ernst Steinitz} in Breslau.
\end{center}


\textbf{21.} The system of equations
\[
 \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik}x_k=c_i\qquad(i=1,\ldots,m) \tag{22}
\]
is solvable in integral numbers if and only if the coefficient system \(A=(a_{ik})\) and the system \(A'\), obtained from \(A\) by adjoining \(c_1,\ldots,c_m\) as last column, have the same rank \(r\) and the same \(r\)-th determinant divisor \(\mathfrak b\).

Proof. If \(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n\) are an integral solution of (22), then elementary determinant theory shows that every \(r\)-minor of \(A'\) which contains the last column is a linear form in the \(r\)-minors of \(A\) as coefficients; hence it is divisible by \(\mathfrak b\). Thus, in passing from \(A\) to \(A'\), the \(r\)-th determinant divisor, and likewise every other determinant divisor, remains unchanged. Similarly, the rank is not increased. The stated conditions are therefore necessary.

Conversely assume that the conditions are fulfilled, and let \(\delta\) be a nonzero \(r\)-minor of \(A\). Order the equations and unknowns so that
\[
 \left|\begin{array}{ccc}
 a_{11}&\cdots&a_{1r}\\
 \vdots&&\vdots\\
 a_{r1}&\cdots&a_{rr}
 \end{array}\right|=\delta .
\]
Then the equations
\[
 \sum_{k=1}^{r}a_{ik}x_k=\delta c_i\qquad(i=1,\ldots,r)
\]
have an integral solution \(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_r\). Since \(r\) is the rank of the whole coefficient system, the equations
\[
 \sum_{k=1}^{r}a_{ik}\xi_k=\delta c_i
\]
hold; or, putting \(\xi_{r+1}=\xi_{r+2}=\cdots=\xi_n=0\), we have
\[
 \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{ik}\xi_k=\delta c_i\qquad(i=1,\ldots,m). \tag{23}
\]

Let \(\mathfrak p_1,\mathfrak p_2,\ldots,\mathfrak p_l\) be the prime factors occurring in \(\delta\), and fix one of them, \(\mathfrak p_q\). Choose an \(r\)-minor \(\delta_q\) of \(A\) in which \(\mathfrak p_q\) occurs as often as in \(\mathfrak b\). Let \(\delta_q=r_q\delta\) be the principal ideal represented by \(\delta_q\); then \(r_q\) is not divisible by \(\mathfrak p_q\). Finally choose a number \(\mu_q\) divisible by \(r_q\) but not by \(\mathfrak p_q\). Again ordering equations and unknowns suitably, suppose that
\[
 \left|\begin{array}{ccc}
 a_{11}&\cdots&a_{1r}\\
 \vdots&&\vdots\\
 a_{r1}&\cdots&a_{rr}
 \end{array}\right|=\delta_q .
\]
Then the solutions of
\[
 \sum_{k=1}^{r}a_{ik}x_k=\mu_q c_i\qquad(i=1,\ldots,r)
\]
are integral; setting all other unknowns equal to \(0\), we obtain equations of the form
\[
 \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{ik}\xi_{qk}=\mu_q c_i\qquad(i=1,\ldots,m). \tag{24}
\]
This can be done for every prime factor \(\mathfrak p_1,\ldots,\mathfrak p_l\). Then \(\delta,\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_l\) are coprime. Choose \(\alpha,\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_l\) so that
\[
 \alpha\delta+\beta_1\mu_1+\cdots+\beta_l\mu_l=1, \tag{25}
\]
and set
\[
 \alpha\xi_k+\beta_1\xi_{1k}+\cdots+\beta_l\xi_{lk}=\bar\xi_k
 \qquad(k=1,\ldots,n). \tag{26}
\]
Equations (23), (24), (25), and (26) give
\[
 \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{ik}\bar\xi_k=c_i\qquad(i=1,\ldots,m), \tag{27}
\]
which proves the theorem.\footnote{This last argument assumes \(n>1\). For \(n=1\) the assertion follows immediately, since then \(x=1\) satisfies the conditions of the problem.}

\subsection*{§ 4. Modules}

\textbf{22.} From now on the degree of a rectangle means its number of columns. This applies in particular when the rectangle consists of only one row; its degree is then the number of its elements. We use small Greek letters for rows. The equation \(\sigma=0\) means that all elements of \(\sigma\) are zero. A factor of a row \(\sigma\) is any ideal which divides every element of \(\sigma\); the greatest common divisor of the elements is called the greatest factor of \(\sigma\). By \(a\sigma\) we mean the row obtained from \(\sigma\) by multiplying its entries by the number \(a\). If \(\sigma\) and \(\tau\) have the same degree, then \(\sigma+\tau\) and \(\sigma-\tau\) denote the rows obtained by componentwise addition and subtraction. If integral rows \(\sigma,\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\ldots,\sigma_m\) satisfy a relation
\[
 \sigma=a_1\sigma_1+\cdots+a_m\sigma_m
\]
with integral coefficients \(a_i\), then \(\sigma\) is said to be composable from \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_m\).

\textbf{23.} Modules. A system \(\mathfrak S\) of integral rows of degree \(n\) is called a module of degree \(n\) if it has the following two properties:
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
1)&\text{if \(\sigma\in\mathfrak S\), then \(a\sigma\in\mathfrak S\) for every integral}\\
&\text{number \(a\) of \(\K\);}\\[2mm]
2)&\text{if \(\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak S\), then \(\sigma+\tau\in\mathfrak S\).}
\end{array}
\]
Modules are denoted by capital German letters; a lower index gives the degree. The row \(\sigma=0\) by itself forms a module, the zero module.

Two rows \(\sigma,\tau\) are congruent modulo a module \(\mathfrak A_n\), written
\[
 \sigma\equiv\tau\pmod{\mathfrak A_n},
\]
if \(\sigma-\tau\) belongs to the module. The module \(\mathfrak A_n\) is said to be divisible by the module \(\mathfrak B_n\) if every row of \(\mathfrak A_n\) is also contained in \(\mathfrak B_n\); then \(\mathfrak B_n\) is a divisor of \(\mathfrak A_n\), and \(\mathfrak A_n\) is a multiple of \(\mathfrak B_n\). A congruence which holds modulo \(\mathfrak A_n\) also holds modulo every divisor of \(\mathfrak A_n\).

\textbf{24.} Rank, determinant divisors, and elementary divisors of a module. Let a module \(\mathfrak A_n\) be given. Consider all rectangles \(A\) formed from rows of \(\mathfrak A_n\). If among them there are rectangles of rank \(r\), but none of rank \(r+1\), then \(r\) is called the rank of the module, \(\operatorname{Rg}\mathfrak A_n\). For each natural number \(k\), the \(k\)-th determinant divisors of all such rectangles \(A\) have a greatest common divisor \(\mathfrak b_k\); this is the \(k\)-th determinant divisor of \(\mathfrak A_n\). Clearly \(\mathfrak b_k\) divides \(\mathfrak b_{k+1}\), and if \(\operatorname{Rg}\mathfrak A_n=r\), then the first \(r\) determinant divisors are nonzero and all following ones are zero. The elementary divisors of a module are then defined as before for rectangular systems.

\textbf{25.} Basis of a module. If \(\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\ldots,\sigma_m\) are rows of degree \(n\), the collection of all rows composable from them is plainly a module of degree \(n\). Conversely, every module \(\mathfrak A_n\) is generated by finitely many of its rows. Indeed, let \(\operatorname{Rg}\mathfrak A_n=r\), and let \(\mathfrak b_r\) be its \(r\)-th determinant divisor. From the rectangles made of rows of \(\mathfrak A_n\) choose an \(r\)-rowed rectangle \(A_0\) whose \(r\)-th determinant divisor \(\mathfrak b_{0r}\) is nonzero. Let \(\mathfrak p_1,\ldots,\mathfrak p_l\) be the distinct prime factors of \(\mathfrak b_{0r}\). By the definition of determinant divisors for modules, we can choose rectangles \(A_1,\ldots,A_l\), each made of \(r\) rows from \(\mathfrak A_n\), so that the \(r\)-th determinant divisor \(\mathfrak b_{qr}\) of \(A_q\) contains \(\mathfrak p_q\) exactly as often as it occurs in \(\mathfrak b_r\). Then
\[
 (\mathfrak b_{0r},\mathfrak b_{1r},\ldots,\mathfrak b_{lr})=\mathfrak b_r,
\]
and the rectangle made from the \((l+1)r\) rows of \(A_0,A_1,\ldots,A_l\) has \(\mathfrak b_r\) as its \(r\)-th determinant divisor.

Now let
\[
 A=(a_{ik})\qquad(i=1,\ldots,m;\ k=1,\ldots,n)
\]
be any rectangle whose rows, also denoted \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_m\), belong to \(\mathfrak A_n\), and whose \(r\)-th determinant divisor is \(\mathfrak b_r\). If \(A\) is enlarged by adjoining any row \(\sigma=(c_1,\ldots,c_n)\) from \(\mathfrak A_n\), neither the rank nor the \(r\)-th determinant divisor changes. Hence, by No. 21, the equations
\[
 \sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{ik}a_i=c_k\qquad(k=1,\ldots,n)
\]
are solvable in integral numbers \(a_1,\ldots,a_m\), and therefore
\[
 \sigma=a_1\sigma_1+\cdots+a_m\sigma_m.
\]
Thus every row of the module is composable from \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_m\).

A system of rows \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_m\) of a module \(\mathfrak A_n\), from which all rows of the module can be composed, is called a basis of \(\mathfrak A_n\); the same name is used for the rectangle \(A\) formed from these rows. We write
\[
 \mathfrak A_n=\operatorname{Md}(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_m)=\operatorname{Md} A.
\]
The basis is irreducible if there is no basis with fewer rows.

\textbf{26.} Let \(\mathfrak A_n=\operatorname{Md}A\) and \(\mathfrak B_n=\operatorname{Md}B\) be two modules of degree \(n\). For \(\mathfrak B_n\) to be divisible by \(\mathfrak A_n\), it is necessary and sufficient that the rows of \(B\) can be composed from those of \(A\), or equivalently that
\[
 B=PA
\]
for some integral matrix \(P\); that is, \(B\) is left-divisible by \(A\). Consequently,
\[
 \operatorname{Md}B=\operatorname{Md}A
\]
holds if and only if \(A\) and \(B\) are left-equivalent.

If \(C\) is a rectangle made of \(k\) rows of \(\mathfrak A_n\), then \(C=PA\), and the \(k\)-th determinant divisor of \(A\) divides that of \(C\). From this and from the definition of determinant divisors for modules it follows that a basis \(A\) has the same determinant divisors, hence the same elementary divisors, as the module \(\mathfrak A_n\).

All bases of a module are equivalent; hence they have the same row class and the same column class. We therefore transfer these notions to the module. Of the two classes we shall prefer the latter, and call it simply the class of the module:
\[
 \Kkl\mathfrak A_n=\Kl A.
\]
Thus the modules fall into as many classes as the ideals of the field \(\K\).

\textbf{27.} Let \(A=(a_{ik})\) be a rectangle made of \(m\) rows \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_m\) of degree \(n\), let \(\operatorname{Rg}A=r\), \(\operatorname{Md}A=\mathfrak A\), and let \(a_1,\ldots,a_m\) be coprime numbers. Put
\[
 a_1\sigma_1+\cdots+a_m\sigma_m=\sigma. \tag{28}
\]
Then there is a square system \(P\) of degree \(m\), determinant \(1\), whose first row is \(a_1,\ldots,a_m\). The inverse system \(P^{-1}\) is also integral; thus \(B=PA\) and \(A=P^{-1}B\) are left-equivalent. Consequently \(B=PA\), like \(A\), is an \(m\)-rowed basis of \(\mathfrak A\), and \(\sigma\) is the first row of this basis. If \(a_1,\ldots,a_m\) can be chosen so that \(\sigma=0\), then deleting this zero row gives an \((m-1)\)-rowed basis of \(\mathfrak A\); the basis \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_m\) is then reducible.

No. 17 says that, when \(r\le m-2\), the equations
\[
 \sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{ki}x_i=0\qquad(k=1,\ldots,n)
\]
or equivalently
\[
 x_1\sigma_1+\cdots+x_m\sigma_m=0
\]
can be satisfied by coprime numbers. Hence an irreducible basis of a module of rank \(r\) cannot contain more than \(r+1\) rows. It plainly cannot contain fewer than \(r\) rows, so the remaining question is when it has \(r\) rows and when it has \(r+1\).

First consider a nonzero module \(\mathfrak A\) of rank \(r\) with an \(r\)-rowed basis \(A\). Form, as in No. 11, the rectangle \(A'\) of \(r\)-minors of \(A\). This rectangle consists of a single row; each column has one element. Thus the gcd of the elements of a column is a principal ideal, and the column class of \(\mathfrak A\) is the principal class. Conversely, suppose that a rank \(r\) module \(\mathfrak A\) belongs to the principal class, and let \(A=(a_{ik})\) be a basis of \(\mathfrak A\) consisting of rows \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r,\sigma_{r+1}\). If \(c_1,\ldots,c_{r+1}\) is a nonzero column of the rectangle \(A'\) of \(r\)-minors of \(A\), then
\[
 c_1\sigma_1+\cdots+c_{r+1}\sigma_{r+1}=0 \tag{29}
\]
and
\[
 (c_1,c_2,\ldots,c_{r+1}) \tag{30}
\]
is a principal ideal, represented by an integral number \(a\). The numbers \(c_k'=c_k/a\) are integral and coprime, and
\[
 c_1'\sigma_1+\cdots+c_{r+1}'\sigma_{r+1}=0.
\]
Therefore the basis \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_{r+1}\) is reducible, and \(\mathfrak A\) has an \(r\)-rowed basis.

Thus: an irreducible basis of a rank \(r\) module \(\mathfrak A\) consists of \(r\) rows when \(\mathfrak A\) belongs to the principal class, and otherwise of \(r+1\) rows.

\subsection*{§ 5. The Equivalence Conditions}

\textbf{28.} In § 2 we gave two necessary conditions for the equivalence of rectangular systems. We now show that together they are also sufficient. The proof rests on a normal form for a module basis. This normal form is not uniquely fixed, but it is characterized by simple properties.

We first record the required consequences of the preceding work in a slightly altered form. Let
\[
 A=(a_{ik})\qquad(i=1,\ldots,m;\ k=1,\ldots,n)
\]
be a rectangular system with rows \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_m\) and \(n\) columns. Let \(\operatorname{Md}A=\mathfrak A\), \(\operatorname{Rg}A=r\ge1\), let \(\mathfrak b_r\) be the \(r\)-th determinant divisor and \(\mathfrak e_r\) the \(r\)-th elementary divisor of \(A\). Let \(\sigma'\) denote any row of the form
\[
 \sigma'=c_1\sigma_1+\cdots+c_m\sigma_m,
\]
where the coefficients satisfy
\[
 (c_1,\ldots,c_m)=1 \tag{31}
\]
and are otherwise arbitrary. Then:
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\text{a)}&\text{\(\mathfrak A\) has a basis consisting of any prescribed row of the form}\\
&\text{\(\sigma'\) and \(m-1\) further rows;}\\
\text{b)}&\text{if \(m\ge r+1\) and \(\mathfrak m\ne0\), there are rows \(\sigma'\)}\\
&\text{having \(\mathfrak m\) as a factor;}\\
\text{c)}&\text{if \(m=r\), the greatest factor of a row \(\sigma'\) divides \(\mathfrak e_r\),}\\
&\text{and there are rows \(\sigma'\) for which it equals \(\mathfrak e_r\);}\\
\text{d)}&\text{a row \(\sigma\) of degree \(n\) belongs to \(\mathfrak A\) exactly when adjoining}\\
&\text{it to \(A\) gives a rectangle of rank \(r\) and \(r\)-th determinant divisor}\\
&\text{\(\mathfrak b_r\);}\\
\text{e)}&\text{a rectangle \(B\) made of rows of \(\mathfrak A\) is a basis of \(\mathfrak A\)}\\
&\text{exactly when its \(r\)-th determinant divisor is \(\mathfrak b_r\).}
\end{array}
\]

\textbf{29.} Modules of the principal class. Let \(\mathfrak A=\mathfrak M^{(r)}\) be a module of rank \(r\ge1\) belonging to the principal class. It has a basis
\[
 \sigma_1,\sigma_2,\ldots,\sigma_r \tag{32}
\]
of \(r\) rows. Let \(\mathfrak e_r\) be the \(r\)-th elementary divisor of \(\mathfrak M^{(r)}\). From a) and c) of the preceding number, the basis may be chosen so that the greatest factor of \(\sigma_r\) is \(\mathfrak e_r\). Assume this and set
\[
 \operatorname{Md}(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_{r-1})=\mathfrak M^{(r-1)}.
\]
Replacing the first \(r-1\) rows of (32) by any basis of \(\mathfrak M^{(r-1)}\) again gives a basis of \(\mathfrak A\). If \(\mathfrak e_{r-1}\) is the \((r-1)\)-st elementary divisor of \(\mathfrak M^{(r-1)}\), the same argument shows that \(\mathfrak M^{(r-1)}\) has a basis of \(r-1\) rows whose last row has greatest factor \(\mathfrak e_{r-1}\). Continuing, we obtain a basis \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r\) such that the greatest factor \(\mathfrak e_k\) of \(\sigma_k\) is the \(k\)-th elementary divisor of
\[
 \mathfrak M^{(k)}=\operatorname{Md}(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_k).
\]

Assume such a basis. If \(c_1,\ldots,c_k\) are coprime, then by No. 28 c) the greatest factor of
\[
 \sigma'=c_1\sigma_1+\cdots+c_k\sigma_k \tag{33}
\]
divides \(\mathfrak e_k\). Taking \(k>1\), \(c_{k-1}=1\), and all other coefficients zero gives \(\sigma'=\sigma_{k-1}\), whose greatest factor is \(\mathfrak e_{k-1}\). Thus \(\mathfrak e_{k-1}\) divides \(\mathfrak e_k\).

Let \(A_k\) be the rectangle made from \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_k\). Let \(\mathfrak b_k\) be its \(k\)-th determinant divisor and, for \(k>1\), let \(\mathfrak b'_{k-1}\) be its \((k-1)\)-st determinant divisor. Then
\[
 \mathfrak b_1=\mathfrak e_1,\qquad \mathfrak b_k=\mathfrak b'_{k-1}\mathfrak e_k. \tag{34}
\]
Since the last row of \(A_k\) has factor \(\mathfrak e_k\), and the \((k-1)\)-minors made from the first \(k-1\) rows have factor \(\mathfrak b_{k-1}\), it follows that \(\mathfrak b_k\) is divisible by \(\mathfrak b_{k-1}\mathfrak e_k\). Together with (34), this gives \(\mathfrak b'_{k-1}=\mathfrak b_{k-1}\), and hence
\[
 \mathfrak b_k=\mathfrak e_1\mathfrak e_2\cdots\mathfrak e_k
 \qquad(k=1,\ldots,r). \tag{35}
\]
Because \(\mathfrak e_{k-1}\) divides \(\mathfrak e_k\), every product of \(k\) factors chosen from \(\mathfrak e_1,\ldots,\mathfrak e_r\) is divisible by \(\mathfrak b_k\); therefore every \(k\)-minor of \(A=A_r\) has factor \(\mathfrak b_k\). Conversely, \(\mathfrak b_k\) already appears as the gcd of the \(k\)-minors of \(A_k\). Hence \(\mathfrak b_k\) and \(\mathfrak e_k\) are the \(k\)-th determinant and elementary divisors of \(A\) and of \(\mathfrak A\).

Thus a principal-class module of rank \(r\) has a basis \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r\) whose greatest factors are precisely the elementary divisors \(\mathfrak e_1,\ldots,\mathfrak e_r\) of the module; each of these divides the next.

\textbf{30.} Normal basis of an arbitrary module. Let \(\mathfrak A\) be any module of rank \(r\ge1\), with determinant divisors \(\mathfrak b_k\) and elementary divisors \(\mathfrak e_k\). It has a basis of \(r+1\) rows \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r,\sigma_{r+1}\). By No. 28 a), b), this basis may be chosen so that the entries of \(\sigma_{r+1}\) are divisible by any prescribed nonzero ideal \(\mathfrak m\). We take \(\sigma_{r+1}\) to have the factor \(2\mathfrak b_r^2\).

Let \(A_k\) be the rectangle made from \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_k\), and let \(\mathfrak b'_k\) be the \(k\)-th determinant divisor of \(A_k\). Then \(\mathfrak b'_k\) is divisible by \(\mathfrak b_k\), and \(\mathfrak b_k\) is the gcd of \(\mathfrak b'_k\) and the \(k\)-minors of \(A_{k+1}\) in which entries of \(\sigma_{r+1}\) occur. The latter are all divisible by \(2\mathfrak b_r^2\); hence \(\mathfrak b'_k\) must contain each prime factor of \(2\mathfrak b_k\) exactly as often as \(\mathfrak b_k\). Therefore
\[
 \mathfrak b'_k=\mathfrak b_k\mathfrak q_k
\]
with \(\mathfrak q_k\) relatively prime to \(2\mathfrak b_k\). For \(k<r\), inspection of the \((k+1)\)-minors gives \(\mathfrak q_k=1\). Thus \(A_r\) has determinant divisors
\[
 \mathfrak b_1,\ldots,\mathfrak b_{r-1},\mathfrak b_r\mathfrak q_r
\]
and elementary divisors
\[
 \mathfrak e_1,\ldots,\mathfrak e_{r-1},\mathfrak e_r\mathfrak q_r. \tag{36}
\]
Since \(\operatorname{Md}A_r\) is a principal-class module, No. 29 implies that each ideal in (36) divides the next; because \(\mathfrak q_r\) is prime to \(\mathfrak e_{r-1}\), it follows that \(\mathfrak e_{r-1}\) divides \(\mathfrak e_r\). Thus, in general, the elementary divisors
\[
 \mathfrak e_1,\mathfrak e_2,\ldots
\]
of a rectangle or module form a system of ideals in which each divides the next.

\textbf{31.} Continuation. We now transform the basis \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r,\sigma_{r+1}\) of the preceding module. First replace \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r\) by another \(r\)-rowed basis of \(\operatorname{Md}(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r)\). This module belongs to the principal class and has elementary divisors
\[
 \mathfrak e_1,\ldots,\mathfrak e_{r-1},\mathfrak e_r\mathfrak q
\]
where \(\mathfrak q=\mathfrak q_r\). Hence, by No. 29, we may choose the new rows so that their greatest factors are precisely these elementary divisors. We again denote the rows by \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r\), the rectangle they form by \(A_r\), and the rectangle obtained by adjoining \(\sigma_{r+1}\) by \(A_{r+1}\), so that \(\mathfrak A=\operatorname{Md}A_{r+1}\).

Let \(\mathfrak q'\) be an ideal of the same class as \(\mathfrak q\), relatively prime to \(\mathfrak q\). Then there is a row \(\sigma'_r\), proportional to \(\sigma_r\), with greatest factor \(\mathfrak e_r\mathfrak q'\). Adjoining this row to \(A_{r+1}\) gives a rectangle \(A_{r+2}\) of the same rank \(r\). Its \(r+2\) rows have factors
\[
 \mathfrak e_1,\ldots,\mathfrak e_{r-1},\mathfrak e_r\mathfrak q,
 \mathfrak b_r^2,\mathfrak e_r\mathfrak q',
\]
and every product of \(r\) of these factors is divisible by \(\mathfrak e_1\cdots\mathfrak e_r=\mathfrak b_r\). Therefore \(A_{r+2}\), like \(A_{r+1}\), has \(r\)-th determinant divisor \(\mathfrak b_r\), and by No. 28 d), \(\sigma'_r\) belongs to \(\mathfrak A\).

Now consider the rectangle \(A'_{r+1}\) whose rows are
\[
 \sigma_1,\sigma_2,\ldots,\sigma_r,\sigma'_r .
\]
Its \(r\)-minors either vanish, or have gcd \(\mathfrak b_r\mathfrak q\), or have gcd \(\mathfrak b_r\mathfrak q'\). Since \((\mathfrak q,\mathfrak q')=1\), the \(r\)-th determinant divisor is \(\mathfrak b_r\). By No. 28 e), these rows form a basis of \(\mathfrak A\). Hence every module \(\mathfrak A\) of rank \(r\) with elementary divisors \(\mathfrak e_1,\ldots,\mathfrak e_r\) has a basis of \(r+1\) rows
\[
 \sigma_1,\sigma_2,\ldots,\sigma_r,\sigma'_r
\]
such that \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_{r-1}\) have greatest factors \(\mathfrak e_1,\ldots,\mathfrak e_{r-1}\), while \(\sigma_r,\sigma'_r\) are proportional and the gcd of their \(2n\) entries is \(\mathfrak e_r\). Such a basis will be called normal.

\textbf{32.} Let \(\mathfrak A\) be a module of rank \(r\ge1\), and let \(A\) be a basis of \(\mathfrak A\) consisting of rows \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r,\sigma_{r+1}\). There exist \(r+1\) numbers \(c_1,\ldots,c_{r+1}\), not all zero, such that
\[
 c_1\sigma_1+\cdots+c_{r+1}\sigma_{r+1}=0. \tag{37}
\]
These numbers are determined up to a common factor. If one forms the rectangle of \(r\)-minors of \(A\), its nonzero columns are proportional to \(c_1,\ldots,c_{r+1}\). Thus the class \(\varkappa\) of the gcd
\[
 \mathfrak t=(c_1,\ldots,c_{r+1})
\]
is also the column class of \(\mathfrak A\).

If \(A\) is normal, the last two rows, again denoted \(\sigma_r,\sigma'_r\), are proportional, and (37) takes the simple form
\[
 c_r\sigma_r+c'_r\sigma'_r=0, \tag{38}
\]
so that
\[
 \Kl\mathfrak A=\Kl(c_r,c'_r). \tag{39}
\]
Let \(\mathfrak e_r\) be the \(r\)-th determinant divisor of \(\mathfrak A\), the gcd of the \(2n\) entries of \(\sigma_r,\sigma'_r\). If \(\tau,\tau'\) are two rows proportional to \(\sigma_r\) and having the same gcd \(\mathfrak e_r\) among their \(2n\) entries, then
\[
 \operatorname{Md}(\sigma_r,\sigma'_r)=\operatorname{Md}(\tau,\tau'). \tag{40}
\]
Hence replacing \(\sigma_r,\sigma'_r\) by \(\tau,\tau'\) again gives a normal basis of \(\mathfrak A\).

Let \(\mathfrak e_r\mathfrak q\) and \(\mathfrak e_r\mathfrak q'\) be the greatest factors of \(\sigma_r\) and \(\sigma'_r\). Then
\[
 (\mathfrak q,\mathfrak q')=1, \tag{41}
\]
and \(\mathfrak q,\mathfrak q'\) lie in the same class \(\lambda\). If \(c_r,c'_r\) denote also the principal ideals represented by the corresponding numbers, then (38) gives
\[
 \mathfrak q\,c_r=\mathfrak q'\,c'_r.
\]
Together with (41), this yields
\[
 c_r=c_r(\mathfrak q,\mathfrak q')=(\mathfrak q c_r,\mathfrak q'c_r)
       =(\mathfrak q'c'_r,\mathfrak q'c_r)=\mathfrak q'(c_r,c'_r),
\]
so that \(\Kl\mathfrak q=\lambda=1/\varkappa\), or
\[
 \Kl\mathfrak A=\Kl\frac{1}{\mathfrak q}. \tag{42}
\]

Now let \(c,c'\) be any two numbers whose gcd \(\mathfrak u\) belongs to the class \(\varkappa\), and let \(c,c'\) also denote the principal ideals they represent. Put
\[
 \mathfrak t=\frac{c'}{\mathfrak u},\qquad
 \mathfrak t'=\frac{c}{\mathfrak u}.
\]
Then \(\mathfrak t,\mathfrak t'\) are integral ideals in the class \(1/\varkappa\), and \((\mathfrak t,\mathfrak t')=1\). Hence one can choose two rows \(\tau,\tau'\), proportional to \(\sigma_r\), whose greatest factors are \(\mathfrak e_r\mathfrak t\) and \(\mathfrak e_r\mathfrak t'\). The rows \(c\tau\) and \(c'\tau'\) then have the same greatest factor \(\mathfrak e_r cc'/\mathfrak u\); they therefore differ only by a unit, and may be chosen so that
\[
 c\tau+c'\tau'=0.
\]
Thus: if \(\mathfrak A\) is a module of rank \(r\), \(\Kl\mathfrak A=\varkappa\), and \(c,c'\) are any two numbers whose gcd belongs to \(\varkappa\), then a normal basis
\[
 \sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r,\sigma'_r
\]
of \(\mathfrak A\) can be chosen so that
\[
 c\sigma_r+c'\sigma'_r=0.
\]

\textbf{33.} The equivalence theorem. The preceding results give the theorem: two rectangular systems \(A\) and \(B\) are equivalent if and only if they have the same elementary divisors, the same row class, and the same column class.\footnote{Rectangles of rank \(0\) belong to no row or column class. It is immediate that all such rectangles, whose entries, elementary divisors, and determinant divisors all vanish, are mutually equivalent. They may therefore be disregarded below.}

Proof. The necessity of these conditions was already proved in Nos. 10 and 14. Moreover the conditions are not independent: by No. 12, if two rectangles have the same elementary divisors and column class, then they must also have the same row class, and conversely with rows and columns interchanged.

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be two rectangles, of equal or unequal degree, of rank \(r\ge1\), with elementary divisors \(\mathfrak e_1,\mathfrak e_2,\ldots\), determinant divisors \(\mathfrak b_1,\mathfrak b_2,\ldots\), and common column class \(\varkappa\). Choose nonzero numbers \(c,c'\) whose gcd belongs to the class \(\varkappa\). Choose a normal basis
\[
 \sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r,\sigma'_r
\]
of \(\mathfrak A=\operatorname{Md}A\), and a normal basis
\[
 \tau_1,\ldots,\tau_r,\tau'_r
\]
of \(\mathfrak B=\operatorname{Md}B\), so that
\[
 c\sigma_r+c'\sigma'_r=0,\qquad c\tau_r+c'\tau'_r=0. \tag{42}
\]
Let
\[
 A'=(a_{ik})\quad(i=1,\ldots,r+1;\ k=1,\ldots,n),
\]
\[
 B'=(b_{ik})\quad(i=1,\ldots,r+1;\ k=1,\ldots,n')
\]
be the rectangles formed from these rows. Then \(A'\) is left-equivalent to \(A\), and \(B'\) to \(B\). It remains only to prove that \(A'\) and \(B'\) are equivalent.

Adjoin to \(A'\) a column \(b_{1q},\ldots,b_{r+1,q}\) of \(B'\), forming \(A'_q\). By (42), the last two rows of \(A'_q\) are still proportional, so \(\operatorname{Rg}A'_q=r\). Its rows have factors
\[
 \mathfrak e_1,\mathfrak e_2,\ldots,\mathfrak e_r,\mathfrak e_r,
\]
and all \(r\)-minors of \(A'_q\) are divisible by \(\mathfrak b_r=\mathfrak e_1\cdots\mathfrak e_r\). Hence the \(r\)-th determinant divisor is unchanged in passing from \(A'\) to \(A'_q\). By No. 21 the equations
\[
 \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{ik}x_k=b_{iq}\qquad(i=1,\ldots,r+1)
\]
are solvable in integral numbers \(p_{1q},\ldots,p_{nq}\). Doing this for all \(n'\) columns of \(B'\) gives
\[
 \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{ik}p_{kq}=b_{iq}\qquad(i=1,\ldots,r+1;\ q=1,\ldots,n'). \tag{43}
\]
With \(P=(p_{ik})\), this is
\[
 A'P=B'.
\]
The same argument proves the existence of \(B'Q=A'\). Thus \(A'\) and \(B'\) are right-equivalent, and the theorem is proved.

\textbf{34.} The elementary divisors
\[
 \mathfrak e_1,\mathfrak e_2,\ldots
\]
of a rectangle or module form, as shown above, a sequence of ideals each dividing the next, with only finitely many nonzero terms. Conversely every such sequence is the elementary-divisor system of modules and rectangles, indeed of modules of every class; it will therefore be called simply an elementary-divisor system.

Let \(\mathfrak e_1,\ldots,\mathfrak e_r\) be nonzero ideals, each dividing the next, and let \(\varkappa\) be an arbitrary ideal class. Let \(\mathfrak q,\mathfrak q'\) be two ideals in the class reciprocal to \(\varkappa\), with \((\mathfrak q,\mathfrak q')=1\), and put
\[
 \mathfrak e_1\mathfrak e_2\cdots\mathfrak e_k=\mathfrak b_k\qquad(k=1,\ldots,r).
\]
If \(n>r\), then by No. 16 we can construct a rectangle \(A_r\) of degree \(n\), with \(r\) rows \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r\), such that these rows have factors
\[
 \mathfrak e_1,\ldots,\mathfrak e_{r-1},\mathfrak e_r\mathfrak q \tag{44}
\]
and the rectangle \(A_k\) made from the first \(k\) rows, for \(k<r\), has \(k\)-th determinant divisor \(\mathfrak b_k\), while the \(r\)-th determinant divisor of \(A_r\) is \(\mathfrak b_r\mathfrak q\). It is then immediate that the ideals in (44) are the elementary divisors of \(A_r\) and also the greatest factors of its rows. Since \(\mathfrak q,\mathfrak q'\) lie in the same class, there is a row \(\sigma'_r\), proportional to \(\sigma_r\), whose greatest factor is \(\mathfrak e_r\mathfrak q'\). The rectangle \(A_{r+1}\) made from \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r,\sigma'_r\) then has elementary divisors
\[
 \mathfrak e_1,\ldots,\mathfrak e_r,
\]
and so does the module \(\mathfrak A=\operatorname{Md}A_{r+1}\). Since \(\mathfrak q,\mathfrak q'\) are in the class \(1/\varkappa\), No. 32 gives
\[
 \Kl\mathfrak A=\varkappa.
\]

If one wants a module of degree and rank \(r\) with elementary divisors \(\mathfrak e_1,\ldots,\mathfrak e_r\), then its class cannot be prescribed arbitrarily: the row class of such a module is plainly the principal class, so its column class must be \(\Kl\mathfrak b_r\). Modules of this class can also be constructed. By No. 16, form a rectangle \(A'\) of \(r\) rows and degree \(r+1\) whose rows have factors \(\mathfrak e_1,\ldots,\mathfrak e_r\) and whose \(r\)-th determinant divisor is \(\mathfrak b_r\). Then \(A'\) has elementary divisors \(\mathfrak e_1,\ldots,\mathfrak e_r\); the same is true of the transposed rectangle \(A\) and of the degree \(r\) module \(\mathfrak A=\operatorname{Md}A\).
