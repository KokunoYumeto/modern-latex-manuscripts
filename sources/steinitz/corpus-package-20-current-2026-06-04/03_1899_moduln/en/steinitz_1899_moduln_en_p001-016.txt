On the Theory of Modules
                                   By Ernst Steinitz in Charlottenburg.

    The following investigations concern those modules whose introduction into number theory is
due to Dedekind. In the eleventh supplement to Dirichlet’s lectures edited by him, the theory of
modules is presented in its basic features. In addition, two further papers must be considered
here: Frobenius, Theory of linear forms with integral coefficients; Frobenius and Stickelberger,
On groups of interchangeable elements (Journal für die reine und angewandte Mathematik, vols.
86, 88). In the latter, the connection between modules and groups of interchangeable elements
is made clear, and after this it is easy to transfer results obtained in one theory to the other.
I shall therefore confine myself to the treatment of modules, although it would be natural to
include group-theoretic questions.
   I first state the most important relevant theorems of Dedekind and Frobenius, in the form
and with the notation which I shall use throughout what follows.


§ 1. Rectangular systems; their division into classes.
1. Rectangular systems.

In what follows, capital Latin letters denote systems of rational integers arranged in rows and
columns.1 On such systems the operations addition, subtraction, and multiplication are to be
applied in the usual way. Addition and subtraction are possible when the rectangular systems
contain the same number of rows and the same number of columns; multiplication is possible
when the number of columns of each factor is equal to the number of rows of the following factor,
and the product then has as many rows as its first factor and as many columns as its last factor.
    The number of columns of a rectangular system A is called its degree. If this degree is n and
0 < k ≤ n, then the greatest common divisor of the determinants of degree k in A is called “the
kth determinant-divisor of A”. It is also to be noted that the greatest common divisor of given
numbers is taken positive and is to be put equal to 0 only when all the numbers are zero. If the
number r of rows of A is < n, then nevertheless n determinant-divisors are to be counted, by
stipulating that for k > r the kth determinant-divisor is to be put equal to 0. The number of
non-zero determinant-divisors of a rectangle is called its “rank”.
    If the system A is square, it is called a diagonal system when all elements outside the diagonal
vanish. If a1 , a2 , . . . , an are the diagonal elements of such a system, it will also be denoted by
Dg(a1 | a2 | · · · | an ). This diagonal system is called a “principal system” when none of the
elements a is negative and each is a divisor of the following one. A square system A is called
unimodular when its “norm” (Nm A), that is, the absolute value of its determinant, is equal to
1. The letter E will always denote only unimodular systems; the symbol E+ will denote those
of determinant +1. If E 0 is the diagonal system of degree n all of whose diagonal elements are
equal to 1, then for every square system A of degree n one has AE 0 = A, E 0 A = A, and to every
unimodular system E there belongs a second one E −1 for which
                                            EE −1 = E −1 E = E 0
holds.

We shall first restrict ourselves to considering square systems.

   Definition 1. If A and B are square systems of the same degree n, and if A can be represented
as a product in which B occurs as a factor, then we say: “The class of the system A (Cl A) is
divisible by the class of the system B.”
    If Cl A is divisible by Cl B and Cl B is divisible by Cl C, then, as is immediately seen, Cl A is
also divisible by Cl C.
   Definition 2. Two classes are called equal when each is divisible by the other.
    The admissibility of these definitions is easily verified. The square systems are thereby divided
into classes; each class is completely determined by a single system contained in it.
   The problems now arise: given two square systems A and B, decide whether Cl A is divisible
by Cl B, or whether the systems A and B belong to the same class. These questions are decided
by two theorems, probably first developed by Frobenius, which in what follows will be called
Fundamental Theorems I and II.

it is necessary and sufficient that they possess the same determinant-divisors. If this requirement
is fulfilled, A can always be represented in the form

3. Classes of positive norm.

Let A = Cl(a1 | a2 | · · · | an ) be a class of positive norm, and let Nm A = a be the product of
the relatively prime numbers a′ and a′′ . Then A has only one divisor A′ of norm a′ and only one
divisor A′′ of norm a′′ . These classes
                                    A′ = Cl((a1 , a′ ) | (a2 , a′ ) | · · · | (an , a′ )),
                                   A′′ = Cl((a1 , a′′ ) | (a2 , a′′ ) | · · · | (an , a′′ ))
are called “components” of A; A is “decomposable” into the components A′ and A′′ . The
characteristic relations for this decomposability are
                                         (A′ , A′′ ) = E,            [A′ , A′′ ] = A.
The decomposition of A can be continued until one reaches primary classes, i.e. classes whose
norm is a power of a prime. In general, if m is a positive integer and p a prime, let mp denote
the highest power of p dividing m; correspondingly put
                                       Ap = Cl((a1 )p | (a2 )p | · · · | (an )p ).
Then Ap is the primary component of A corresponding to the prime p. For primes p which do
not divide Nm A, one has Ap = E. Every class A of positive norm can be decomposed into
primary components in one way only, and is the least common multiple of these components. If
A is divisible by B, then for every prime p, Ap is divisible by Bp ; and if for every prime p, Ap
is divisible by Bp , then A is divisible by B. If A1 , A2 , . . . , As , ∆, Γ are classes of positive norm,
then the relations
                          (A1 , A2 , . . . , As ) = ∆, [A1 , A2 , . . . , As ] = Γ
are respectively equivalent to the systems of relations
                 ((A1 )p , (A2 )p , . . . , (As )p ) = ∆p ,          [(A1 )p , (A2 )p , . . . , (As )p ] = Γp .

4. Division of rectangular systems into classes.

In order now to include all rectangular systems among the classes, stipulate that every rectangular
system A belongs to the class which possesses the same determinant-divisors as A. If the invariants
of the class Cl A are also called the invariants of the system A, then the following theorem holds:
   If A and B are rectangular systems, then in order that an equation of the form

                                             A=P ·B·Q

be possible, it is necessary and sufficient that Rg A ≤ Rg B and that each positive invariant of A
be divisible by the corresponding invariant of B.
    If A and B have the same degree, this condition amounts to Cl A being divisible by Cl B. If
the rectangles A and B have the same degree (n) and the same number of rows (r), and if Cl A
is divisible by Cl B, then in an equation of the form A = P · B · Q the systems P and Q are
square systems; and if Cl A = Cl B, it can always be arranged that P and Q are unimodular.


§ 2. Modules.
1. Modules.

According to Dedekind, a system of numbers is called a “module” if the difference of any two
numbers of the system again belongs to the system. The module a is said to be divisible by the
module b (a multiple of b, and b a divisor of a) when every number of a is also contained in b. If
the numbers
                                          α1 , α2 , . . . , αr
belong to the module a, then the same is true of every number of the form

                                    α = c1 α1 + c2 α2 + · · · + cr αr ,                            (1)

where the coefficients c are rational integers. The module a is called finite when one can take
from it a finite number of numbers α1 , α2 , . . . , αr such that every number of a can be represented
in the form (1). The numbers α1 , α2 , . . . , αr are then said to form a basis of a, and this will be
indicated by writing
                                     a = Md(α1 , α2 , . . . , αr ).
The basis is called reduced if the expression (1) can vanish only by all coefficients vanishing.
Then every number α of a can be brought into the form (1) in only one way; every basis of a
consists of at least r numbers; and the module a is called r-component. Every multiple of an
r-component module is at most r-component.
   Suppose one has an n-component module

                                        e = Md(η1 , η2 , . . . , ηn ).

If one considers only numbers of this module and performs no operations on them other than
addition and subtraction, the basis elements η1 , η2 , . . . , ηn may be treated as indeterminate
quantities. This amounts to considering, in place of the numbers contained in e

                                        η = c1 η1 + · · · + cn ηn ,

the systems of the n coefficients
                                              c1 , c2 , . . . , cn

by which the numbers η are assembled from the numbers η1 , η2 , . . . , ηn . Such a system of
coefficients will always be thought of as arranged in a horizontal row. Doing this, while otherwise
retaining the notation used so far, we shall understand by a module a totality of systems or
“rows” σ which reproduce themselves under subtraction.
   Now let
                                           a = Md(α1 , α2 , . . . , αr )
be any module. If one forms the rectangle A whose rows are α1 , α2 , . . . , αr , then the module a is
completely determined by it. We call the rectangle A a basis of the module a and write

                                                     a = Md A.

The necessary and sufficient condition that the module b = Md B be divisible by the module
a = Md A is evidently the existence of an equation of the form

                                                     B = P · A.

Taking account of the definitions and theorems given in § 1, it follows that:
   If the rectangles A1 , A2 , . . . are bases of one and the same module a, then they all belong to
the same class. It is convenient to call this class the class of the module a, so that

                                        Cl A1 = Cl A2 = · · · = Cl a.

By transferring to the modules belonging to a class the concepts previously introduced for the
class itself, it is then clear what is to be understood by the degree, rank, norm of a module a
(Gd a, Rg a, Nm a), and by its determinant-divisors and invariants. One sees that the rank of a
module is the same thing as what was earlier called its number of components, so that a module
of rank r is r-component. Divisibility can of course occur only between modules of the same
degree.
    I now state several important theorems from the theory of modules, which will be used
frequently later.
   I. If Rg a = r, the module a possesses a basis of k systems if and only if k ≥ r. Since
Gd a ≥ Rg a, every module of degree n also possesses a basis of n systems, a square basis.
    II. Every module a possesses a square basis of the form AE, where A is the principal system
of Cl a. The class E (n) contains only one module; this module is a divisor of every module of
degree n, and will be denoted by e(n) (or e). Every unimodular system of degree n is a basis of
e(n) . Theorem II can therefore also be formulated as follows:
   IIa. If Cl a = A = Cl(a1 | a2 | · · · | an ), one can give n rows α1 , α2 , . . . , αn such that

                    Md(α1 , α2 , . . . , αn ) = e,       Md(a1 α1 , a2 α2 , . . . , an αn ) = a.

If Rg a = r, then also a = Md(a1 α1 , . . . , ar αr ).
   III. If the module a is a multiple of b, then Cl a is a multiple of Cl b; and if the class A is a
multiple of B, then every module of A has at least one divisor within B, and every module of B
has at least one multiple within A.
   IV. If a is a multiple of b and Cl a is a divisor of Cl b, then a = b.
    Let a1 = Md A1 , a2 = Md A2 , . . . , as = Md As be modules of the same degree n. They
possess a greatest common divisor, that is, there exists a definite module b with the property
that every divisor common to the modules a1 , a2 , . . . , as is a divisor of b, and conversely. The
rectangle whose rows are all the rows of the rectangles A1 , A2 , . . . , As is a basis of this module.
The modules a1 , a2 , . . . , as also possess a least common multiple, that is, there exists a definite

of c, and conversely. Simple considerations show how to find a basis of this module c by a finite

                                          Nm a Nm b = Nm(a, b) Nm[a, b].

the class A. In order that the class A be divisible by B, it is necessary and sufficient that the
principal module of A be divisible by the principal module of B. The greatest common divisor
(respectively, the least common multiple) of the principal modules of the classes A1 , A2 , . . . , As is
the principal module of the class (A1 , A2 , . . . , As ) (respectively, [A1 , A2 , . . . , As ]). Two modules
a, b are called relatively prime when (a, b) = e.


2. Unimodular transformations.

Let A and B be square systems of degree n, let E be a unimodular system of the same degree,
and suppose there is an equation
                                        A = CB.                                         (2)
Then it follows that
                                                       AE = CBE.                                               (3)
Conversely, (2) also follows from (3). This result can be stated as follows:
    If Md A is divisible by Md B, then Md AE is divisible by Md BE, and conversely. Of the
relations
                             Md A = Md B,     Md AE = Md BE,                            (4)
each is therefore a consequence of the other. Thus, for every square basis A of a definite module
a, AE represents the basis of a definite second module a′ . We therefore say:
       “The module a is carried by the unimodular transformation [E] into the module a′ ”
       and write
                                                          a′ = aE.

                                   a′ = Md E1 AE2 = Md AE2 = aE2 .

    VIII. A module a can be carried by unimodular transformations into every module of its
class, but into no further module.
    This is the basis for the fact that all essential properties of a module are determined by its
class.
   One of the most important problems to be solved will consist in determining the number of
modules which belong to the same class A, given by its system of invariants. We denote this
number, which is a “function of A”, by
                                          ψ(A).
It is easy to see that for classes whose norm vanishes, ψ(A) = ∞, except in the case in which all
invariants of A vanish, where evidently one must put ψ(A) = 1. We shall therefore be able to
restrict ourselves to determining the function ψ for classes of positive norm.


3. Multiplication of modules by a number.

Let α1 , α2 , . . . , αr be rows of n integers each, and let m be any integer. Then the mod-
ule Md(mα1 , mα2 , . . . , mαr ) consists of all rows which arise from the rows of the module
Md(α1 , α2 , . . . , αr ) by multiplication by m. We denote it by “m · a” and suppose m > 0.
If a is divisible by b, then ma is divisible by mb, and conversely. If the module a has invariants
a1 , a2 , . . . , an , then ma has invariants ma1 , ma2 , . . . , man ; conversely, if a module c has invariants
ma1 , ma2 , . . . , man , one may write c = ma′ , and then a1 , a2 , . . . , an are the invariants of a′ .
Hence, if A = Cl(a1 | a2 | · · · | an ) is any class and if Cl(ma1 | ma2 | · · · | man ) = mA is put, the
classes A and mA contain equally many modules, i.e.

                                          IX.        ψ(mA) = ψ(A).

The class E contains only the module e, and hence mE contains only the single module me, so
that for every unimodular system E one has meE = me.

§ 3. Congruences; the functions φr and φ.
Two rows σ, σ ′ each of n elements are called congruent modulo the module a of degree n,

                                                   σ ≡ σ′       (mod a),

when σ − σ ′ belongs to the module a. If R, R′ are rectangles of degree n with r rows each, then

                                                   R ≡ R′           (mod a)

means that each row of R is congruent to the corresponding row of R′ . Each row of n elements
represents a definite residue of the module a, in the sense that congruent rows are regarded as
representatives of the same residue. Likewise, a rectangle of degree n with r rows represents a
definite r-rowed residue of a. From the congruence

                                                   R ≡ R′           (mod a)

it follows that
                                              RE ≡ R′ E             (mod aE),

where E denotes any unimodular system. Thus the unimodular transformation [E], which carries
a into aE, at the same time carries every residue of a into a definite residue of aE. Since the
transformation is uniquely reversible, it establishes a one-to-one correspondence between the
residues of a and those of aE. From this and from § 2, VIII it follows that for all modules of
a class, the number of residues of a specified row-number is the same. Consideration of the
principal module of a class A shows that, if Nm A ̸= 0, the number of one-row residues of a
module of A is equal to Nm A, and consequently the number of r-rowed residues is equal to
(Nm A)r . If, on the other hand, Nm A = 0, then the number of residues is evidently infinite.
