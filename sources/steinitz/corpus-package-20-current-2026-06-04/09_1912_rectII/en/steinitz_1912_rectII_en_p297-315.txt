§6. Fundamental modules
35. Least common multiple and greatest common divisor. Let S denote a system
of finitely or infinitely many modules of the same degree s. The rows that occur in every
one of these modules–and there is at least one such row, namely the row 0–form a module
A, as follows immediately from the definition of modules. This module A is a common
multiple of all modules in S, and indeed the least common multiple; for every common
multiple A′ of the modules in S may contain only rows that occur in all modules of S, and
hence also in A, so that it is a multiple of A.
    The modules of S have at least one common divisor, namely the unit module Es , which
comprises all rows of degree s and therefore divides every module of degree s. Let S ′ be the
system of all common divisors of the modules in S, and let T be the least common multiple
of the modules in S ′ . Then T is not only divisible by every common divisor of the modules
in S; it is itself a common divisor. For if U is any module from S, then U is a common
multiple of all modules in S ′ , and is therefore divisible by their least common multiple T.
Thus T will be called the greatest common divisor of the modules in the system S.
    If S consists of finitely many modules A(1) , A(2) , . . . , A(k) , we denote their greatest
common divisor by
                                     (A(1) , A(2) , . . . , A(k) ).
If A(i) = Md. Ai , then a basis of the greatest common divisor is obtained by adjoining the
rows of the rectangles Ai into one rectangle. Every row of this module can be written in
the form α1 + α2 + · · · + αk , where αi is taken from A(i) .
    For two modules A and B, the equation

                                           (A, B) = A

is plainly the assertion that A divides B. If A is a basis of A, B a basis of B, and if r is
the rank and dr the r-th determinant-divisor of A, then by Nos. 21 and 28d) B is divisible
by A if and only if the rectangle formed from all rows of A and B has rank r and r-th
determinant-divisor dr . If these conditions are fulfilled, and if also Rg. B = r and the r-th
determinant-divisor of B is dr , then A is likewise divisible by B, hence A = B. Therefore:
    If the modules A, B have the same rank r and the same r-th determinant-divisor, and
if B is divisible by A, then A = B.
    If further rows σ1 , σ2 , . . . , σk are adjoined to a basis of a module M, one obtains a basis
of the module M′ that is the greatest common divisor of M and Md.(σ1 , σ2 , . . . , σk ). We
write
                                         M′ = (M, σ1 , σ2 , . . . , σk ).

This module is unchanged if the rows σi are replaced by rows congruent to them modulo
M.

36. Fundamental modules. Let C be a rectangle of degree n and rank r, consisting of
rows σ1 , . . . , σm whose entries are integral or fractional numbers of K. Let Z be the system
of all rows σ of the form
                                  σ = c1 σ1 + c2 σ2 + · · · + cm σm ,
where arbitrary integral or fractional numbers of K are admitted as coefficients. A row
belongs to Z if and only if it can be adjoined to C without increasing the rank. From the
rows of Z infinitely many rectangles A of rank r can be formed, and every such A, just
like C, determines the whole system Z. If A is integral, it is a basis of an integral module
A of rank r. Thus one obtains a system S of infinitely many modules of rank r. Every
module A in S determines Z and hence S. A module B of rank r belongs to the system
determined by A if and only if

                                    Rg.(A, B) = r = Rg. A.

Among all these modules there is one that contains every integral row in Z and therefore
divides every module in S. This module is called the fundamental module belonging to
S, or to any module of S. Conversely, each module in S is said to belong to this same
fundamental module. If A is any module, then A will henceforth denote the corresponding
fundamental module. Thus
                                        A=B
is equivalent to
                                 Rg. A = Rg. B = Rg.(A, B).
The fundamental module belonging to A can be characterized as follows:

  a) A consists of all integral rows σ satisfying Rg.(A, σ) = Rg. A;

  b) A consists of all integral rows that can be composed from a basis of A with integral
     or fractional coefficients;

  c) A consists of 0 and all rows proportional to some row of A.

If A is itself a fundamental module, then A = A. Hence a fundamental module A can be
characterized by any of the following properties:

  1) every row satisfying Rg.(A, σ) = Rg. A belongs to A;

  2) every integral row that can be composed from a basis of A with fractional numbers
     can also be composed with integral numbers;

  3) every row proportional to a row of A belongs to A.

If er is the r-th elementary divisor of a module A of rank r and σ1 , . . . , σr , σr′ is a normal
basis of A, then every row of A proportional to er has the form cr σr + cσr′ and therefore
has the factor er . Thus a fundamental module must have er = 1. Conversely, if er = 1,
and therefore the r-th determinant-divisor is 1, then every row σ with Rg.(A, σ) = Rg. A
belongs to A, since adjoining σ to a basis of A cannot change the r-th determinant-divisor.
Hence:

  4) A module is a fundamental module if and only if it has no elementary divisor different
     from 0 and 1.

   Let A and B be modules of the same rank, with A divisible by B, so that (A, B) = B.
Then Rg.(A, B) = Rg. A = Rg. B, hence

                                                A = B.

If B is assumed to be a fundamental module, then B = A: A is the only fundamental
module of rank r divided by A. Conversely, if A is assumed to be a fundamental module,
the same equality follows. Therefore:

  5) A fundamental module is characterized by having no divisor of the same rank except
     itself.

   If A is a fundamental module and m an ideal, mA denotes the module consisting of all
rows of A that have m as a factor. If m = 0, then mA means the zero module; otherwise
mA is a module belonging to the fundamental module A.

37. Fundamental modules of rank 1. A fundamental module of rank 1 consists of all
rows proportional to a fixed nonzero row σ, together with the zero row. It is denoted by σ.
If A is a module of rank 1, e1 its first elementary divisor, and σ ̸= 0 any row in A, then
A = σ. Every row of A belongs to σ and has the factor e1 ; conversely every row of σ with
factor e1 belongs to A. Thus
                                           A = e1 σ.
This gives a one-to-one correspondence between all nonzero ideals e and all modules eσ
belonging to the same fundamental module. The relation

                                         (aσ, bσ) = (a, b)σ                                      (46)

is immediate.

38. Let σ1 , . . . , σr be r linearly independent rows of degree n. Let qi be the greatest factor
of σi , let σi′ be a row proportional to σi , let q′i be the greatest factor of σi′ , and put

                                            (qi , q′i ) = gi .                                   (47)

Then
                                        Md.(σi , σi′ ) = gi σi .                                 (48)
If the r-th determinant-divisor of Md.(σ1 , . . . , σr ) is written as q1 · · · qr b, then for each of
the 2r rectangles obtained by choosing one row from each pair σi , σi′ , the r-th determinant-
divisor equals b times the product of the greatest factors of the chosen rows. The gcd of
these products is
                                  q1 · · · qr b · (g1 , . . . , gr ).                             (49)
It is also the r-th determinant-divisor of

                                  M = Md.(σ1 , σ1′ , . . . , σr , σr′ ).                        (49’)

By (48), this module is the greatest common divisor of the modules gi σi :

                                      M = (g1 σ1 , . . . , gr σr ).                              (50)

40. The only fundamental module of degree and rank n is the unit module En containing
all rows of degree n. If it is represented as

                                              En = (σ1 , . . . , σn ),

and if σi is a nonzero row of σi with greatest factor qi , then q1 · · · qn is the n-th determinant-
divisor of the square system formed by σ1 , . . . , σn ; equivalently, it is the principal ideal
represented by its determinant.
    Conversely, given nonzero ideals q1 , . . . , qn whose product is a principal ideal, one may
choose rows σ1 , . . . , σn with greatest factors q1 , . . . , qn such that

                                              (σ1 , . . . , σn ) = En .

Indeed, after choosing the first n − 1 rows so that the (n − 1)-st determinant-divisor is
q1 · · · qn−1 , the determinant obtained after adjoining a variable row σn = (x1 , . . . , xn ) has
the form
                                       c1 x1 + · · · + cn xn ,                                 (53)

                                                             4
with (c1 , . . . , cn ) = q1 · · · qn−1 . Restricting the xi to numbers divisible by qn represents
exactly the numbers divisible by q1 · · · qn , so the determinant can be made to represent the
given principal ideal.
    If A = (σ1 , . . . , σr ) is a fundamental module of degree n and rank r < n, then one can
choose a complementary fundamental module B = (σr+1 , . . . , σn ) of rank n − r with

                                           (A, B) = En .

Moreover the classes of A and B are reciprocal.

41. Every module A of degree n with elementary divisors ei can be written

                                      A = (e1 σ1 , . . . , en σn )                           (54)

in such a way that (σ1 , . . . , σn ) = En . If Rg. A = r, one first writes

                                      A = (e1 σ1 , . . . , er σr ),                          (55)

where (σ1 , . . . , σr ) = A, and then complements this fundamental module to En as in No.
40; for i > r the elementary divisors are 0.
    If m is a nonzero ideal, then a module whose first s factors are mσi and whose remaining
factors are σi contains exactly the rows with factor m; hence

                                     (mσ1 , . . . , mσn ) = mEn .

Consequently, if A has elementary divisors ei , then (A, mEn ) has elementary divisors (ei , m).

42. Relative basis. For modules A and M of the same degree, rows

                                            α1 , . . . , αm                                  (56)

belonging to A are called a relative basis of A | M if every row α of A satisfies a congruence

                              α ≡ c1 α1 + · · · + cm αm        (mod M).

Equivalently, A is divisible by

                                      A′ = (α1 , . . . , αm , M).                            (57)

This is exactly the condition

                                   (M, α1 , . . . , αm ) = (A, M).                           (58)

The same rows then also form a basis of (A, M) | M. Conversely, from a basis α1′ , . . . , αm

                                 ′
of (A, M) | M one may write αi = αi + µi , with αi ∈ A and µi ∈ M; then the αi form a
basis of A | M.
    The least number of rows of A that must be adjoined to M in order to obtain (A, M)
is 0 if A is divisible by M; otherwise it is the least possible number of rows in a basis of
A | M. This number is unchanged if M is replaced by (A, M).

43. Relative rank. Define
                                        Rg. A | M = r
to mean that r is the least number such that between any r + 1 rows α1 , . . . , αr+1 of A
there is a congruence
                          c1 α1 + · · · + cr+1 αr+1 ≡ 0 (mod M)                       (60)
with not all coefficients zero. Then

                                Rg. A | M = Rg.(A, M) | M.                                (62)

If A divides M, Rg. A | M = r, and Rg. M = s, then

                                        Rg. A = r + s.

The proof chooses rows α1 , . . . , αr in A without a linear congruence modulo M and rows
µ1 , . . . , µs in M without a linear equation. There is no linear equation among the combined
r + s rows; conversely every row α of A can be expressed fractionally from them by using a
congruence
                                 cα ≡ c1 α1 + · · · + cr αr (mod M)                        (65)
and then an ordinary linear equation in M. For arbitrary modules of the same degree this
yields
                            Rg. A | M = Rg.(A, M) − Rg. M.                          (69)
Replacing each module by another belonging to the same fundamental module leaves the
relative rank unchanged:
                              Rg. A | M = Rg. A | M.                            (70)
Thus Rg. A | M = 0 is precisely the condition that A be a multiple of M; and if it holds,
then A is divisible by M.


§7. The divisibility conditions
44. A module of rank r has a basis of r + 1 rows. If it belongs to the principal class, an
irreducible basis may consist of only r rows. In general, when forming a basis of r + 1 rows
for a module A of rank r, one row within A may be chosen arbitrarily; if A is not in the
principal class, that row must be chosen nonzero.
    Let σ1 , . . . , σr+1 be a basis of A and let α ̸= 0 be a row of A. Then

                                 α = a1 σ1 + · · · + ar+1 σr+1 ,                          (71)
                                 0 = b1 σ1 + · · · + br+1 σr+1 ,                          (72)

with not all bi zero. The bi may be normalized so that their gcd is relatively prime to the
gcd of the ai . The linear forms ai x + bi y can then be made coprime by a suitable choice
x = x0 , y = y0 :
                                      βi = ai x0 + bi y0 .                             (73)
Hence A has a basis consisting of the prescribed row α and r further rows of A.

45. Level of a module relative to another. The notation St. A | M is used only when
Rg. A | M = 0, that is, when A is divisible by M. It denotes the least number of rows that
must be adjoined to M to obtain (A, M). It satisfies

                                    St. A | M = St.(A, M) | M,                              (74)

and
                                          St. A | M ≤ Rg. A.                                (75)
The latter follows by choosing a nonzero row α of A, multiplying it so that cα ∈ M, and
using No. 44 to form a basis of A from cα and at most r = Rg. A further rows.

46. Let Rg. A | M = 0 and Rg. B | M = 0. If A divides B, then

                                       St. B | M ≤ St. A | M.                               (76)

After replacing A and B by (A, M) and (B, M), choose bases α1 , . . . , αs of A′ | M and
β1 , . . . , βt of B′ | M. Since the βi also belong to A′ , they are congruent modulo M to linear
combinations of the αk . These combinations span a module B′′ of rank at most s, but by
(75) its level over M is t. Hence t ≤ s.

47. Let A be a module of degree n and let m ̸= 0 be an ideal. Then Rg. A | mEn = 0. If
e1 , . . . , en are the elementary divisors of A, then

                        St. A | mEn = #{k : ek is not divisible by m}.

Writing A in the normal form

                          A = (e1 σ1 , . . . , en σn ),   (σ1 , . . . , σn ) = En ,

reduces the assertion to counting those elementary divisors of (A, mEn ) that are proper
divisors of m.

48. Let A and B be modules of degree n, and suppose that A divides B. If ak and bk are
the corresponding elementary divisors of A and B, then every bk is divisible by ak .
    Indeed, if ak ̸= 0, then by No. 46

                                   St. B | ak En ≤ St. A | ak En .                          (81)

The right-hand side is at most k − 1, so at most k − 1 elementary divisors of B can fail to
be divisible by ak . Thus bk is divisible by ak .

49. The divisibility conditions. For the rectangle B to be divisible by the rectangle A, it
is necessary that every elementary divisor of B be divisible by the corresponding elementary
divisor of A. This condition is also sufficient if Rg. A > Rg. B. If Rg. A = Rg. B = r, one
further condition is necessary and sufficient: if dr and br are the r-th determinant-divisors
of A and B, then the integral ideal br : dr must have a divisor belonging to the class
CCl. B : CCl. A.
    Necessity follows by assuming
                                         B = P AQ.                                       (82)

      In the second case r = r′ , take a divisor t of b′r : dr in the class x′ : x. Choose ideals

                       A = (a1 σ1 , . . . , ar σr ),       B = (b1 τ1 , . . . , br τr ),

                       C = (c1 σ1 , . . . , cr σr ),       D = (c1 τ1 , . . . , cr τr ).

equivalent. Since B is divisible by D∗ and C by A, B is divisible by A.


§8. Ideal systems
50. Ideal systems. In No. 22 Steinitz defined the addition of two rows of degree n and
the multiplication of a row by a number. Thus all rows of degree n constitute a system E
with two laws of composition. First, two elements α, β determine a sum α + β; addition is
associative and commutative, and for every pair α, β there is a unique γ with β + γ = α,
written α − β. Second, an element α and an integer a of K determine aα, with

          1α = α,    a(bα) = (ab)α,         a(α + β) = aα + aβ,              (a + b)α = aα + bα.

Steinitz henceforth calls an ideal system belonging to the field K any system whose elements
possess these two operations–addition and multiplication by an integer of K–with the
properties just listed.
