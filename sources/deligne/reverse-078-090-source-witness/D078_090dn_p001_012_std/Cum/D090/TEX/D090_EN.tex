\begin{abstract}
Let \(G\) be a reductive group over a field \(k\) which is algebraically closed of characteristic
\(p\ne 0\). We prove a structure theorem for a class of subgroup schemes of \(G\) when \(p\)
is bounded below by the Coxeter number of \(G\). As applications, we derive semisimplicity
results generalizing earlier results of Serre proven in 1998 and also obtain an analogue of
Luna's étale slice theorem for suitable bounds on \(p\).
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords.} Group schemes, saturation, reductive, étale slice, Dynkin height, Coxeter number.

\noindent\textbf{2010 Mathematics Subject Classification.} 14L15, 14L24.

\section{Introduction}

We work over an algebraically closed field \(k\) of characteristic \(p>0\).

In \([{\rm S1}]\), Serre showed that if semisimple representations \(V_i\) of a group
\(\Gamma\) are such that
\[
  \sum_i(\dim V_i-1)<p,
\]
then their tensor product is semisimple. In \([{\rm S2}]\), he more generally considers the
case where \(\Gamma\) is a subgroup of \(G(k)\), for \(G\) a reductive group, and where
\(\Gamma\) is \(G\)-cr, meaning that whenever \(\Gamma\) is contained in a parabolic subgroup
\(P\), it is already contained in a Levi subgroup of \(P\). For
\[
  G=\prod_i \GL(V_i),
\]
this is equivalent to the semisimplicity of the representations \(V_i\) of \(\Gamma\).
For a representation \(V\) of \(G\), one can then ask under what conditions \(V\) becomes
semisimple when considered as a representation of \(\Gamma\). In \([{\rm S2},\)
Theorem 6, page 25], Serre shows that this is the case when the Dynkin height
\(\htG(V)\) is less than \(p\). For
\[
  G=\prod_i \GL(V_i),\qquad V=\bigotimes_i V_i,
\]
one has
\[
  \htG(V)=\sum_i(\dim V_i-1).
\]

In \([{\rm D}]\), the results of \([{\rm S1}]\) were generalized to the case when the
\(V_i\) are semisimple representations of a group scheme \(\mathcal G\). In this paper,
we consider the case when \(\mathcal G\) is a subgroup scheme of a reductive group
\(G\), and generalize \([{\rm S2}]\), see 4.11, and \([{\rm D}]\), see 2.5. As in
\([{\rm D}]\), we first have to prove a structure theorem, 2.5, on doubly saturated,
see 2.4, subgroup schemes \(\mathcal G\) of reductive groups \(G\). The proof makes
crucial use of a result of Zhiwei Yun on root systems. The appendix contains the result.

In Section 5, we consider a reductive group acting on an affine variety \(X\) and a point
\(x\) of \(X\) whose orbit \(G\cdot x\) is closed in \(X\). We prove a schematic analogue
of \([{\rm BR},\) Propositions 7.4, 7.6] under some conditions on the characteristic of
\(k\). More precisely, if \(X\) embeds in a \(G\)-module \(V\) of low height, then we obtain,
as a consequence of 2.5(2), an analogue of Luna's étale slice theorem, 5.1 and 5.6.
In \([{\rm BR}]\) the language of schemes was not used, and as a consequence the orbit
\(G\cdot x\) had to be assumed ``separable''. An orbit \(G\cdot x\) is separable if and only if
the stabilizer \(G_x\) is reduced.

\section{Saturation and Infinitesimal saturation}

\subsection*{2.1}

Let \(G\) be a reductive algebraic group over \(k\). Our terminology is that of
\([{\rm SGA3}]\): reductive implies smooth and connected. By an algebraic group we
will mean an affine group scheme of finite type over \(k\). Fix a maximal torus
\(T_G\) and a Borel subgroup containing \(T_G\), which determines a root system \(R\)
and a set of positive roots \(R^+\). Let \(\langle\, ,\,\rangle\) be the natural pairing
between the characters and cocharacters and, for each root \(\alpha\), let
\(\alpha^\vee\) be the corresponding coroot.

If the root system \(R\) associated to \(G\) is irreducible, the Coxeter number
\(h_G\) of \(R\) and of \(G\) admits the following equivalent descriptions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=2em]
\item It is the order of the Coxeter elements of the Weyl group \(W\). This shows
that \(R\) and the dual root system \(R^\vee\) have the same Coxeter number.
\item Let \(\alpha_0\) be the highest root and \(\sum n_i\alpha_i\) its expression as
a linear combination of the simple roots. One has
\[
  h_G=1+\sum_i n_i. \tag{2.1.1}
\]
\item Applying this to the dual root system, one gets
\[
  h_G=\langle \rho,\beta^\vee\rangle+1,
\]
where \(\rho\) is half the sum of positive roots and \(\beta^\vee\) is the highest
coroot. Indeed, \(\rho\) is also the sum of the fundamental weights \(\omega_i\), and
\[
  \langle \omega_i,\alpha_j^\vee\rangle=\delta_{ij}.
\]
\end{enumerate}

For a general reductive group \(G\), define the Coxeter number \(h_G\) to be the largest
among the ones for the irreducible components of \(R\).

It follows from (2) above that if \(G\) is a reductive group, \(U\) the unipotent radical
of a Borel subgroup, and \(\mathfrak u:=\Lie(U)\), the descending central series of
\(\mathfrak u\), defined by \(Z^1\mathfrak u=\mathfrak u\) and
\[
  Z^i\mathfrak u=[\mathfrak u,Z^{i-1}\mathfrak u],
\]
satisfies
\[
  Z^{h_G}(\mathfrak u)=0. \tag{2.1.2}
\]
For the group \(U\), similarly \(Z^{h_G}(U)=(1)\).

The Lie algebra \(\mathfrak g\) of \(G\) is a \(p\)-Lie algebra. Let
\(X\mapsto X^{[p]}\) denote the \(p\)-power operation on \(\mathfrak g\). If
\(\mathfrak n\in\mathfrak g\) is nilpotent and if \(p\ge h_G\), then
\(\mathfrak n^{[p]}=0\). To check this, we may assume that \(\mathfrak n\) is in the
Lie algebra \(\mathfrak u\) of the unipotent radical \(U\) of a Borel subgroup. For each
positive root \(\alpha\), let \(X_\alpha\) be a basis for the root subspace
\(\mathfrak g_\alpha\). Express
\[
  \mathfrak n=\sum_{\alpha\in R^+} a_\alpha X_\alpha .
\]
Each \(X_\alpha\) is the infinitesimal generator of an additive group. It follows that
\(X_\alpha^{[p]}=0\) for each \(\alpha\). Observe that
\[
  \mathfrak n^{[p]}
  =\sum_{\alpha\in R^+} a_\alpha^{p}X_\alpha^{[p]}
    \pmod{Z^p\mathfrak u}, \tag{2.1.3}
\]
and \(Z^p\mathfrak u=0\), as \(p\ge h_G\), see \([{\rm Mc1},\) page 10].

\subsection*{2.2}

Let \(\mathfrak g_{\nilp}\), respectively \(G^{\unip}\), be the reduced subscheme of
\(\Lie(G)\), respectively \(G\), with points the nilpotent, respectively unipotent,
elements. Let \(U\) be the unipotent radical of a Borel subgroup. For \(p\ge h_G\),
the Campbell--Hausdorff group law \(\circ\) makes sense in characteristic \(p\) and
turns \(\mathfrak u:=\Lie(U)\) into an algebraic group over \(k\). This is so since
\(Z^p\mathfrak u=0\). Further, there is a unique isomorphism
\[
  \exp:(\Lie(U),\circ)\xrightarrow{\sim} U \tag{2.2.1}
\]
equivariant for the action of \(B\) and whose differential at the origin is the identity.
If, in addition, the simply connected covering of the derived group of \(G\) is an étale
covering, which is the case for \(p>h_G\) and could fail when \(p=h_G\) because of the
presence of \(\mathrm{SL}(p)\) as factors in the covering, then there is a unique
\(G\)-equivariant isomorphism, see \([{\rm S2},\) Theorem 3, page 21] and Section 6
for details,
\[
  \exp:\mathfrak g_{\nilp}\longrightarrow G^{\unip}, \tag{2.2.2}
\]
which induces (2.2.1) on each unipotent radical of a Borel subgroup. Let
\[
  \log:G^{\unip}\longrightarrow \mathfrak g_{\nilp}
\]
denote its inverse.

For \(u\) a unipotent element of \(G(k)\), one defines the \(t\)-power map
\(t\mapsto u^t\), from \(\Ga\) to \(G\), by
\[
  t\longmapsto \exp(t\log u). \tag{2.2.3}
\]
For \(G=\GL(V)\) such a map \(t\mapsto u^t\) is more generally defined for any
\(u\) in \(G\) such that \(u^p=1\). It is given by the truncated binomial expression
\([{\rm S1},\) 4.1.1, page 524]:
\[
  t\longmapsto u^t:=\sum_{i<p}\binom{t}{i}(u-1)^i. \tag{2.2.4}
\]
Similarly, \(X\in\operatorname{End}(V)\) such that \(X^{[p]}=0\) defines a morphism
\(t\mapsto \exp(tX)\) from \(\Ga\) to \(\GL(V)\) given by the truncated exponential
series
\[
  t\longmapsto I+tX+\frac{(tX)^2}{2!}+\cdots+
  \frac{(tX)^{p-1}}{(p-1)!}. \tag{2.2.5}
\]

Until the end of Section 3, we make the following assumptions on the reductive group
\(G\).

\begin{assumption23}
Let \(\widetilde G\) be the simply connected covering of the derived group \(G'\).
We assume that \(p\ge h_G\) and that the map \(\widetilde G\to G'\) is étale.
\end{assumption23}

In particular, by 2.1 and 2.2, the exponential map (2.2.2) is defined, every unipotent
element in \(G(k)\) is of order \(p\), and every nilpotent in \(\Lie(G)\) is
\(p\)-nilpotent. One can then define the notions of saturation and infinitesimal
saturation of subgroup schemes \(\mathcal G\subset G\) as follows; see Remark 2.19
for the case when \(G=\operatorname{PGL}(p)\).

\begin{definition24}[\({\rm S1},\S4\), \({\rm D}\), Definition 1.5]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=2em]
\item A subgroup scheme \(\mathcal G\subset G\) is called saturated if, for every
\(u\in \mathcal G(k)\) which is unipotent, the homomorphism \(t\mapsto u^t\),
(2.2.3), from \(\Ga\) to \(G\) factors through \(\mathcal G\).
\item A subgroup scheme \(\mathcal G\subset G\) is called infinitesimally saturated if,
for every nilpotent \(X\in\Lie(\mathcal G)\), the morphism
\(t\mapsto \exp(tX)\), (2.2.2), from \(\Ga\) to \(G\) factors through
\(\mathcal G\).
\item \(\mathcal G\) is doubly saturated if it is saturated and infinitesimally
saturated.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition24}

An element of \(\Lie(\mathcal G)\) is nilpotent if and only if it is nilpotent as an
element of \(\Lie(G)\). The reference to the exponential map (2.2.2) in (2) therefore
makes sense. One way to see that the notions of nilpotence for elements of
\(\Lie(\mathcal G)\) and \(\Lie(G)\) coincide is to observe that the inclusion of
\(\Lie(\mathcal G)\) in \(\Lie(G)\) is a morphism of \(p\)-Lie algebras and that \(X\)
is nilpotent if and only if it is killed by an iterated \(p\)-power map, i.e.
\[
  X^{[p^\ell]}=0.
\]

Let \(\mathcal G^0\) be the identity component of \(\mathcal G\) and
\(\mathcal G^0_{\red}\) the reduced subscheme of \(\mathcal G^0\).

\begin{theorem25}
Let \(\mathcal G\subset G\) be a \(k\)-subgroup scheme which is infinitesimally
saturated. Assume that, if \(p=h_G\), \(\mathcal G^0_{\red}\) is reductive. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=2em]
\item The group \(\mathcal G^0_{\red}\) and its unipotent radical
\(\Ru(\mathcal G^0_{\red})\) are normal subgroup schemes of \(\mathcal G\), and the
quotient group scheme
\[
  \mathcal G^0/\mathcal G^0_{\red}
\]
is of multiplicative type.
\item If \(\mathcal G^0_{\red}\) is reductive, there exists a central, connected subgroup
scheme of multiplicative type \(M\subset \mathcal G^0\) such that the morphism
\[
  M\times \mathcal G^0_{\red}\longrightarrow \mathcal G^0
\]
realizes \(\mathcal G^0\) as a quotient of \(M\times \mathcal G^0_{\red}\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem25}

We note that since \(k\) is assumed to be algebraically closed, a group scheme of
multiplicative type is simply a diagonalizable group scheme.

The proof of part (2) of 2.5 will occupy most of Section 2, until 2.16. Part (1) will
be proven in Section 3.

By \([{\rm D},\) Lemma 2.3], the conclusions of 2.5 hold for \(\mathcal G\) if and
only if they hold for the identity component \(\mathcal G^0\). Until the end of
Section 3, we will assume that \(\mathcal G\) is connected.

\begin{lemma26}
If \(\mathcal G\) is an infinitesimally saturated subgroup scheme of \(G\), every
nilpotent element \(\mathfrak n\) of \(\Lie(\mathcal G)\) is in
\(\Lie(\mathcal G_{\red})\).
\end{lemma26}

\begin{proof}
As \(\Ga\) is reduced, the morphism \(t\mapsto \exp(t\mathfrak n)\) maps \(\Ga\) to
\(\mathcal G_{\red}\subset \mathcal G\). Identifying \(\Lie(\Ga)\) with a copy of the
ground field, the image of \(1\) in \(\Lie(\Ga)\) is \(\mathfrak n\).
\end{proof}



Part (2) of 2.5 is a corollary of 2.6 and of the following theorem, which does not refer to
\(G\) anymore.

\begin{theorem27}
Let \(\mathcal G\) be a connected algebraic group such that
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2em]
\item \(\mathcal G_{\red}\) is reductive,
\item any nilpotent element of \(\Lie(\mathcal G)\) is in
\(\Lie(\mathcal G_{\red})\).
\end{enumerate}
Then the conclusion of 2.5(2) holds. As a consequence,
\(\mathcal G_{\red}\) is a normal subgroup scheme of \(\mathcal G\) and
\(\mathcal G/\mathcal G_{\red}\) is of multiplicative type.
\end{theorem27}

Let \(T\) be a maximal torus of \(\mathcal G_{\red}\), and let \(H\) be the centralizer
of \(T\) in \(\mathcal G\). One has \(H\cap \mathcal G_{\red}=T\). It follows from (b)
that any nilpotent element \(\mathfrak n\) of \(\Lie(H)\) is in \(\Lie(T)\), hence
vanishes. By the following lemma, \(H\) is of multiplicative type, and in particular
commutative.

\begin{lemma28}
Let \(H\) be a connected algebraic group over \(k\). If all the elements of \(\Lie(H)\)
are semisimple, then \(H\) is of multiplicative type.
\end{lemma28}

\begin{proof}
See also \([\mathrm{DG},\,\mathrm{IV},\S3,\text{ Lemma }3.7]\). The Lie algebra
\(\Lie(H)\) is commutative. Fix \(x\in \Lie(H)\), and let us show that it is central in
\(\Lie(H)\). Since \(\operatorname{ad}x\) is semisimple, it suffices to show that, if
\(y\) is in an eigenspace of \(\operatorname{ad}x\), i.e. \([x,y]=\lambda y\), then \(x\) and
\(y\) commute, i.e. \(\lambda=0\). Let \(W\) be the vector subspace of \(\Lie(H)\)
generated by the elements \(y^{[p^\ell]}\), \(\ell\ge0\). The elements
\(y^{[p^\ell]}\) commute. The map \(z\mapsto z^{[p]}\) therefore induces a \(p\)-linear
map from \(W\) to itself, injective by assumption. This implies that \(W\) has a basis
\(e_i\), \(1\le i\le N\), consisting of elements such that
\[
  e_i^{[p]}=e_i,
  \qquad
  \Big(\sum a_i e_i\Big)^{[p]}=\sum a_i^p e_i.
\]
To see this, we view \(W\) as an algebraic group. The morphism \(W\to W\) given by
\(x\mapsto x^{[p]}-x\) is étale. Let \(K\) be its kernel. In any basis it is defined
by \(d=\dim(W)\) equations of degree \(p\), namely
\[
  (x^{[p]})_i-x_i=0,\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,d,
\]
with an étale set of solutions and no solution at infinity: the system of homogeneous
equations \((x^{[p]})_i=0\) has no nonzero solutions by assumption. By Bézout, the
group of solutions is hence a \((\mathbb Z/p)^d\). Let \(e_i\) be a basis for it. If the
\(e_i\) were not linearly independent in \(W\), there would be a linear dependence
\(\sum_{i\in J} a_i e_i=0\), with the \(a_i\in k\) nonzero, involving a minimal number
of \(e_i\). We also have \(\sum a_i^p e_i=0\). Hence, by the minimality of the \(e_i\),
for some \(\lambda\) and all \(i\), one has \(a_i^p=\lambda a_i\). Rescaling by
\(\lambda^{1/(p-1)}\), we get \(a_i\in\mathbb F_p\), which is a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma29}
For \(b=(b_i)\in k^N\), define \(b^{[p]}:=(b_i^p)\). Then any \(b\in k^N\) is a
linear combination of the \(b^{[p^\ell]}\) for \(\ell\ge1\).
\end{lemma29}

\begin{proof}
The \(b^{[p^a]}\), \(a\ge0\), are linearly dependent. A linear dependence relation can
be written
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j\ge m} c_j b^{[p^j]}=0
  \tag{2.9.1}
\end{equation}
with \(c_m\ne0\). Extracting \(p^m\)-roots, we get
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j\ge0} d_j b^{[p^j]}=0,
  \tag{2.9.2}
\end{equation}
where \(d_j=c_{m+j}^{1/p^m}\). In particular, \(d_0\ne0\), proving the lemma.
\end{proof}

\noindent\emph{End of proof of commutativity.}
From the lemma above, \(y\) is a linear combination of the \(y^{[p^\ell]}\),
\(\ell>0\). The bracket \([y^{[p^\ell]},x]\) vanishes for \(\ell>0\). Indeed, it is
\((\operatorname{ad}y)^{[p^\ell]}(x)\), which vanishes because
\([y,[y,x]]=[y,-\lambda y]=0\). It follows that \([y,x]\) vanishes too.

The \(p\)-Lie algebra \(\Lie(H)\) is commutative and hence its \(p\)-power operation
\(\Lie(H)\to\Lie(H)\) is injective. It therefore has a basis \(e_i\) such that
\(e_i^{[p]}=e_i\). The dual of its restricted enveloping algebra is the affine bigebra
of the kernel \(K\) of the Frobenius morphism \(F:H\to H^{(p)}\), where \(H^{(p)}\)
is obtained from \(H\) by extension of scalars \(\lambda\mapsto\lambda^{[p]}\),
\(k\to k\). It follows that \(K\) is a product of \(\mu_p\)'s.

The same holds for \(H^{(p)}\), which is obtained from \(H\) using an automorphism of
\(k\). The same holds for each \(H^{(p^\ell)}\) as well.

For any \(n\), the kernel \(K_n\) of the iterated Frobenius map
\(F^n:H\to H^{(p^n)}\) is an iterated extension of subgroups of the kernel of the
Frobenius of the \(H^{(p^i)}\), \(i<n\). It is hence of multiplicative type, being an
iterated extension of connected groups of multiplicative type
\([\mathrm{SGA3},\mathrm{XVII},7.1.1]\).

The \(K_n\) form an increasing sequence. By the proof of \([\mathrm D,\) Proposition
1.1], there exists a subgroup of multiplicative type \(M\) of \(H\) containing all
\(K_n\). Since \(H\) is connected and since \(M\) contains all infinitesimal
neighborhoods of the identity element, one has \(M=H\).

Whenever a group \(M\) of multiplicative type acts on a group \(K\), its action on
\(\Lie(K)\) defines a weight decomposition
\begin{equation}
  \Lie(K)=\bigoplus_{\beta\in X(M)} \Lie(K)_\beta .
  \tag{2.9.3}
\end{equation}
If \(v\in \Lie(K)_\beta\), then \(v^{[p]}\) is in \(\Lie(K)_{p\beta}\). Indeed, after
any extension of scalars \(R/k\), if \(m\in M(R)\),
\begin{equation}
  m(v^{[p]})=(m(v))^{[p]}=(\beta(m)v)^{[p]}=\beta(m)^{[p]}v^{[p]}.
  \tag{2.9.4}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma210}
Let \(M\) be a multiplicative group acting on a group \(K\), and let (2.9.3) be the
weight-space decomposition for the corresponding action on \(\Lie(K)\). If
\(\beta\in X(M)\) is not torsion, then \(\Lie(K)_\beta\) consists of nilpotent elements.
\end{lemma210}

\begin{proof}
Indeed, if \(\beta\) is not torsion, the \(p^\ell\beta\) are all distinct and
\(\Lie(K)_{p^\ell\beta}\) must vanish for \(\ell\gg0\). It follows that the elements of
\(\Lie(K)_\beta\) are nilpotent.
\end{proof}

Let us apply this to the action of \(T\) on \(\mathcal G\) by inner automorphisms.

\begin{lemma211}
If \(\beta\in X(T)\) is nonzero, the weight space \(\Lie(\mathcal G)_\beta\) equals
\(\Lie(\mathcal G_{\red})_\beta\).
\end{lemma211}

\begin{proof}
Indeed, \(\Lie(\mathcal G)_\beta\) consists of nilpotent elements. By our assumption
2.7(b), it is contained in \(\Lie(\mathcal G_{\red})\).
\end{proof}

Let \(B\) be a Borel subgroup of \(\mathcal G_{\red}\) containing \(T\), and let \(U\)
be its unipotent radical.

\begin{lemma212}
Under the assumption of 2.7, \(H\) normalizes \(U\).
\end{lemma212}

\begin{proof}
Let \(C\) in \(X(T)\otimes\mathbb R\) be the cone generated by the positive roots
relative to \(B\), and define
\begin{equation}
  C^*:=C\setminus\{0\}.\tag{2.12.1}
\end{equation}
As in 2.11, we let \(T\) act on \(\mathcal G\) by conjugation, so that \(t\) acts by
\(g\mapsto tgt^{-1}\). This action induces actions on
\(\mathfrak g:=\Lie(\mathcal G)\), on the affine algebra \(A\) of \(\mathcal G\), on
its augmentation ideal \(\mathfrak m\), and on the dual
\(\mathfrak g^\vee=\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2\) of \(\mathfrak g\). Similarly, \(T\)
acts on \(U\), on its Lie algebra \(\mathfrak u\), on its affine algebra \(A_U\), and
on its augmentation ideal \(\mathfrak m_U\). For the action on affine algebras,
\(t\in T\) transforms \(f(g)\) into \(f(t^{-1}gt)\). From these actions we get
\(X(T)\)-gradings. By 2.7(b),
\begin{equation}
  \mathfrak u=\bigoplus_{\beta\in C^*} \mathfrak g_\beta .
  \tag{2.12.2}
\end{equation}
It follows that \(\mathfrak u^\vee=\mathfrak m_U/\mathfrak m_U^2\) is the sum of the
\((\mathfrak g^\vee)_\beta\), for \(\beta\) in the negative \(-C^*\) of \(C^*\). For
\(n>0\), the weights by which \(T\) acts on
\(\mathfrak m_U^n/\mathfrak m_U^{n+1}\) are in \(-C^*\). Since \(U\) is connected,
the intersection of the \(\mathfrak m_U^n\) is reduced to \(0\), and the weights by
which \(T\) acts on \(\mathfrak m_U\) are also in \(-C^*\).

Let \(I\) be the ideal of \(A\) generated by the graded components \(\mathfrak m_\beta\)
of \(\mathfrak m\) for \(\beta\notin -C^*\), and put \(A_1:=A/I\). The image in
\(\mathfrak m_U\) of an \(\mathfrak m_\beta\) as above is contained in
\((\mathfrak m_U)_\beta\), hence vanishes. It follows that \(U\) is contained in the
closed subscheme \(U_1=\operatorname{Spec} A_1\) of \(\mathcal G\) defined by \(I\).
As the graded component \(\mathfrak m^0\) of \(\mathfrak m\) is contained in \(I\),
the graded component \(A_1^0\) of \(A_1\), the \(T\)-invariants, is reduced to the
constants. Since \(T\) is connected, it follows that \(U_1\) is connected. Since the
image of \((\mathfrak m_U)_\beta\) in \(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2\) is
\((\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2)_\beta\), the image of \(I\) in
\(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2=\mathfrak g^\vee\) is the orthogonal of
\(\mathfrak u\subset \mathfrak g\), and the tangent space at the origin of \(U_1\) is
\(\mathfrak u\).

\emph{Claim.} The subscheme \(U_1\) of \(\mathcal G\) is a subgroup scheme; that is,
the coproduct \(\Delta:A\to A\otimes A\), \(f(g)\mapsto f(gh)\), maps \(I\) to
\(I\otimes A+A\otimes I\). Indeed, \(\Delta\) respects the gradings. For \(k\subset A\)
the constants, one has \(A=k\oplus\mathfrak m\), and \(\Delta\) maps
\(\mathfrak m\) to
\((k\otimes\mathfrak m)\oplus(\mathfrak m\otimes k)\oplus
(\mathfrak m\otimes\mathfrak m)\), and hence \(\mathfrak m_\beta\) maps to
\begin{equation}
(k\otimes\mathfrak m_\beta)\oplus(\mathfrak m_\beta\otimes k)
\oplus
\sum_{\beta=\beta'+\beta''} \mathfrak m_{\beta'}\otimes\mathfrak m_{\beta''}.
\tag{2.12.3}
\end{equation}
Since \(-C^*\) is stable under addition, if \(\beta=\beta'+\beta''\) and \(\beta\) is
not in \(-C^*\), one of \(\beta'\) or \(\beta''\) is not in \(-C^*\), and the
corresponding \(\mathfrak m_{\beta'}\) or \(\mathfrak m_{\beta''}\) is contained in
\(I\). The claim follows.

To summarize, \(U_1\) is connected, and the inclusion \(U\subset U_1\) induces an
isomorphism \(\Lie(U)\xrightarrow{\sim}\Lie(U_1)\). Since \(U\) is smooth, this
implies that \(U=U_1\).

Since \(H\) centralizes \(T\), the ideal \(I\) is stable under \(H\), meaning that \(H\)
normalizes \(U\).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary213}
Under the assumption of 2.7, \(H\) normalizes \(\mathcal G_{\red}\).
\end{corollary213}

\begin{proof}
Let \(B^-\) be the Borel subgroup of \(\mathcal G_{\red}\) containing \(T\) and
opposite to \(B\), and let \(U^-\) be its unipotent radical. As \(U^-\), \(T\), and
\(U\) are normalized by \(H\), the big cell \(U^-TU\subset \mathcal G_{\red}\) is
stable under the conjugation action of \(H\), and so is its schematic closure
\(\mathcal G_{\red}\).
\end{proof}

In what follows, we identify schemes with the corresponding fppf sheaves. A quotient
such as \(\mathcal G/H\) represents the quotient of the sheaf of groups \(\mathcal G\)
by the subsheaf \(H\): \(\mathcal G\) is an \(H\)-torsor over \(\mathcal G/H\).

\begin{lemma214}
The morphism of schemes
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal G_{\red}/T\longrightarrow \mathcal G/H
  \tag{2.14.1}
\end{equation}
is an isomorphism.
\end{lemma214}

\begin{proof}
As \(T\) is the intersection of \(\mathcal G_{\red}\) and \(H\), the morphism (2.14.1),
as a morphism of fppf sheaves, is injective. Testing on \(\operatorname{Spec}(k)\) and
\(\operatorname{Spec}(k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2))\), one sees that it is bijective
on points and injective on tangent spaces at each point. It is hence radicial and
unramified. Therefore, on some open set of \(\mathcal G_{\red}/T\), this morphism is
an immersion. The \(\mathcal G_{\red}\)-homogeneity then shows that it is a closed
embedding. Since \(\mathcal G_{\red}/T\) is smooth and (2.14.1) is bijective on
\(k\)-points, to prove that the closed embedding (2.14.1) is an isomorphism, it
suffices to prove that at each point it induces an isomorphism of tangent spaces. By
homogeneity, it suffices to check this at the origin. The tangent space at the origin of
\(\mathcal G_{\red}/T\) is \(\Lie(\mathcal G_{\red})/\Lie(T)\).

For the tangent space of \(\mathcal G/H\) we proceed as follows. Let \(J\) be the ideal
defining \(H\) in \(\mathcal G\), and let \(\eta\) be the ideal defining the origin in
\(\mathcal G/H\). Since \(\mathcal G\) is an \(H\)-torsor over \(\mathcal G/H\), the
pull-back of \(\eta/\eta^2\) to \(\mathcal G\) is \(J/J^2\), and \(\eta/\eta^2\) is the
fiber of \(J/J^2\) at the origin \(e\). By \([\mathrm D,2.15]\) applied to \(T\) acting
on \(\mathcal G\) by conjugation, we have an isomorphism
\[
  (J/J^2)_e \xrightarrow{\sim}
  \bigoplus_{\beta\ne0}(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2)_\beta
  =\bigl(\Lie(\mathcal G_{\red})/\Lie(T)\bigr)^\vee .
\]
Taking duals, we see that (2.14.1) is an isomorphism near the origin, hence everywhere.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary215}
Under the assumptions of 2.7, \(\mathcal G_{\red}\) is normal in \(\mathcal G\), and
there exists in \(\mathcal G\) a central connected subgroup scheme of multiplicative
type \(M\) such that the morphism
\[
  M\times \mathcal G_{\red}\longrightarrow \mathcal G
\]
realizes \(\mathcal G\) as a quotient of \(M\times\mathcal G_{\red}\).
\end{corollary215}

\begin{proof}
By 2.14, the product map \(\mathcal G_{\red}\times H\to \mathcal G\) is onto as a
morphism of sheaves. Since both \(\mathcal G_{\red}\) and \(H\) normalize
\(\mathcal G_{\red}\subset \mathcal G\), so does \(\mathcal G\).

To complete the proof of 2.15, and thereby of 2.7, we follow \([\mathrm D,\S2.25]\).
Let \(M\) be the subgroup of \(H\) which centralizes \(\mathcal G_{\red}\), as always in
the scheme-theoretic sense. By \([\mathrm{DG},\) Corollary 2.4(a), page 476], \(M\) is
also multiplicative. Since \(\mathcal G_{\red}\) is reductive, the group scheme
\(\Aut_T(\mathcal G_{\red})\) of automorphisms preserving \(T\) is precisely
\(T^{\mathrm{ad}}\), the image of \(T\) in the adjoint group. Hence the conjugation
action of \(H\) on \(\mathcal G_{\red}\) gives the exact sequence
\begin{equation}
  1\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow H\longrightarrow T^{\mathrm{ad}}\longrightarrow 1,
  \tag{2.15.1}
\end{equation}
and \(T\) surjects onto \(T^{\mathrm{ad}}\), implying that \(M\) and \(T\) generate
\(H\). Since \(M\) is generated by \(M_{\red}\subset \mathcal G_{\red}\) and \(M^0\),
and since \(H\) and \(\mathcal G_{\red}\) generate \(\mathcal G\), we see that \(M^0\)
and \(\mathcal G_{\red}\) generate \(\mathcal G\). Moreover \(M^0\) is central. Thus
\begin{equation}
  M^0\times \mathcal G_{\red}\longrightarrow \mathcal G
  \tag{2.15.2}
\end{equation}
is an epimorphism. This concludes the proof of 2.7 and in particular of 2.5(2).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma216}
Suppose that \(H\) is a maximal connected subgroup scheme of multiplicative type of an
algebraic group \(\mathcal G\). Let \(Z^0_{\mathcal G}(H)\) be the identity component
of the centralizer of \(H\) in \(\mathcal G\), and define
\(U:=Z^0_{\mathcal G}(H)/H\). Then the sequence
\begin{equation}
0\longrightarrow \Lie(H)\longrightarrow \Lie(Z^0_{\mathcal G}(H))
\longrightarrow \Lie(U)\longrightarrow 0
\tag{2.16.1}
\end{equation}
associated with the central extension
\begin{equation}
1\longrightarrow H\longrightarrow Z^0_{\mathcal G}(H)\longrightarrow U
\longrightarrow 1
\tag{2.16.2}
\end{equation}
is exact.
\end{lemma216}

\begin{proof}
Left exactness of (2.16.1) is clear. The maximality of \(H\) implies that \(U\) is
unipotent; see \([\mathrm D,\S2.5,\text{ page }590]\).

Embed \(H\) in \(\Gm^r\) as a subgroup scheme. The quotient \(H''=\Gm^r/H\) is a
torus, being a quotient of one. The central extension (2.16.2), by a push-forward,
gives a central extension \([\mathrm{SGA3},\) Exposé XVII, Lemma 6.2.4]
\begin{equation}
1\longrightarrow \Gm^r\longrightarrow E\longrightarrow U\longrightarrow 1
\tag{2.16.3}
\end{equation}
and a diagram of groups
\begin{equation}
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=1.5em,row sep=1.2em]
 & 1 \arrow[d] & 1 \arrow[d] & 1 \arrow[d] & \\
1 \arrow[r] & H \arrow[r] \arrow[d] & Z^0_{\mathcal G}(H) \arrow[r] \arrow[d] & U \arrow[r] \arrow[d,equal] & 1 \\
1 \arrow[r] & \Gm^r \arrow[r] \arrow[d] & E \arrow[r] \arrow[d] & U \arrow[r] \arrow[d] & 1 \\
1 \arrow[r] & H'' \arrow[r,equal] \arrow[d] & H'' \arrow[r] \arrow[d] & 1 \arrow[d] & \\
 & 1 & 1 & 1 &
\end{tikzcd}
\tag{2.16.4}
\end{equation}

Since \(H''\) is multiplicative, every nilpotent in \(\Lie(E)\) maps to \(0\) in
\(\Lie(H'')\), and hence comes from a nilpotent in \(\Lie(Z^0_{\mathcal G}(H))\).
Since \(\Gm^r\) is smooth, \([\mathrm{SGA3},\) Exposé VII, Proposition 8.2] gives from
(2.16.3) an exact sequence
\begin{equation}
0\longrightarrow \Lie(\Gm^r)\longrightarrow \Lie(E)\longrightarrow \Lie(U)
\longrightarrow 0.
\tag{2.16.5}
\end{equation}
Since \(U\) is unipotent, any element \(z\in\Lie(U)\) is nilpotent. Let \(z'\in\Lie(E)\)
be a lift of \(z\). The Jordan decomposition makes sense for any \(p\)-Lie algebra over
a perfect field \(k\) and uses only the \(p\)-power map; see, for example,
\([\mathrm{W2},\) Corollary 4.5.9, page 135]. Thus, by using the Jordan decomposition
of the lift \(z'\) in \(\Lie(E)\), and noting that the semisimple part gets mapped to
zero in \(\Lie(U)\), we can assume that \(z'\) can also be chosen to be nilpotent.

Since every nilpotent in \(\Lie(E)\) comes from a nilpotent in
\(\Lie(Z^0_{\mathcal G}(H))\), we conclude that \(z\) is lifted to a nilpotent in
\(\Lie(Z^0_{\mathcal G}(H))\). This implies that (2.16.1) is also right exact.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma217}
Suppose that \(\mathcal G\) is a subgroup scheme of \(G\) which is infinitesimally
saturated in \(G\). Then the subgroup scheme \(Z^0_{\mathcal G}(H)\) is infinitesimally
saturated in \(G\). In particular, every nonzero nilpotent in
\(\Lie(Z^0_{\mathcal G}(H))\) lies in \(\Lie(Z^0_{\mathcal G}(H)_{\red})\).
\end{lemma217}

\begin{proof}
If \(\mathfrak n\in\Lie(Z^0_{\mathcal G}(H))\) is nilpotent, the map
\(\rho:t\mapsto \exp(t\mathfrak n):\Ga\to G\) factors through \(\mathcal G\). Since
\(H\) is central in \(Z^0_{\mathcal G}(H)\), the action of \(H\) by inner automorphisms
fixes \(\mathfrak n\). Since the map \(\exp\) is compatible with conjugation, the entire
curve \(\rho\) is fixed by \(H\). Therefore \(\rho\) factors through \(Z_G(H)\), and
hence through \(Z^0_{\mathcal G}(H)_{\red}\), since \(\Ga\) is reduced and connected.
Thus \(\mathfrak n\in\Lie(Z^0_{\mathcal G}(H)_{\red})\).
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Remark 2.19.}
Recall that in 2.2, for the existence of an exponential map (2.2.2), we assumed that
the covering morphism \(\widetilde G\to G'\) is étale, which therefore became a part of
the standing assumption (2.3). The case which gets excluded is when the simply connected
cover of the derived group has factors of \(\SL(p)\). For instance, when \(G'\) is simple
with \(p=h_G\), the only case excluded is \(G'=\PGL(p)\).

Let \(G\) be a reductive group for which \(p=h_G\). The tables of Coxeter numbers of
simple groups show that except for type \(A\), where \(h_{\SL(n)}=n\), Coxeter numbers
are even greater than \(2\). It follows that \(\widetilde G\) is a product of
\(\SL(p)\)'s and other simple factors with \(p\) larger than their Coxeter numbers. Even
when the morphism \(\widetilde G\to G'\) is not étale, we can still define the notions
of saturation, respectively infinitesimal saturation, of subgroup schemes
\(\mathcal G\subset G\) as follows.

Say that \(\mathcal G\subset G\) is saturated, respectively infinitesimally saturated, if
the inverse image of \(\mathcal G\) in \(\widetilde G\) is saturated, respectively
infinitesimally saturated. With this definition, Theorem 2.5 remains true for
\(p\ge h_G\).

This notion of saturation, respectively infinitesimal saturation, can also be seen in
terms of suitably defined \(t\)-power maps and exponential maps. We restrict ourselves to
the case when \(G'=\PGL(p)\). At the level of Lie algebras, the induced morphism
\begin{equation}
  \mathfrak{sl}(p)\longrightarrow \mathfrak{pgl}(p)
  \tag{2.19.1}
\end{equation}
is a radicial map on the locus of nilpotent elements. If \(A\in\mathfrak{gl}(p)\) is a
matrix representing an element of \(\mathfrak{pgl}(p)\), it is nilpotent if all but the
constant coefficient of the characteristic polynomial vanish, i.e.
\(\Tr(\wedge^i A)=0\) for \(1\le i<p\), and the characteristic polynomial reduces to
\(T^p-\det(A)\). This condition is stable under \(A\mapsto A+\lambda I\), as
\[
  (T-\lambda)^p-\det(A)=T^p-(\det(A)+\lambda^p)=T^p-\det(A+\lambda I).
\]
We get the unique lift \(\widetilde A\in\mathfrak{sl}(p)\) by taking
\(\widetilde A:=A-\det(A)^{1/p}I\). Now one has \(\Tr(\wedge^i\widetilde A)=0\) for
\(1\le i\le p\), and hence we can define the exponential morphism
\(\Ga\to\PGL(p)\) as
\begin{equation}
  t\longmapsto \exp(tA):=\exp(t\widetilde A).
  \tag{2.19.2}
\end{equation}
Likewise, in the case of unipotents in the group \(\PGL(p)\), the restriction of this
map to the locus of unipotent elements
\begin{equation}
  \SL(p)_u\longrightarrow \PGL(p)_u
  \tag{2.19.3}
\end{equation}
is radicial. Thus any unipotent \(u\in\PGL(p)\) has a unique unipotent lift
\(\widetilde u\in\SL(p)\), and one can define the \(t\)-power map \(\Ga\to\PGL(p)\) as
\begin{equation}
  t\longmapsto u^t:=\operatorname{image}(\widetilde u^{\,t}).
  \tag{2.19.4}
\end{equation}
With the notions of \(t\)-power map and exponential morphisms in place, we can define
the notions of saturation, respectively infinitesimal saturation, of subgroup schemes
\(\mathcal G\subset G'\) exactly as in Definition 2.4 using (2.19.4), respectively
(2.19.2), and these coincide with the definitions made above.

We note however that these punctual maps, i.e. maps defined for each nilpotent \(A\),
respectively each unipotent \(u\), are not induced by a morphism from
\(\mathfrak{pgl}(p)_{\nilp}\) to \(\PGL(p)_u\), respectively
\(\mathbb A^1\times\PGL(p)_u\to\PGL(p)_u\), \((t,u)\mapsto u^t\), is not a morphism.

\begin{proposition31}

\paragraph{3.2.}
Assumption 2.3 on the reductive group \(G\) continues to be in force. Let \(\mathcal G\)
be a subgroup scheme of \(G\), and let \(H\) be a maximal connected subgroup scheme of
multiplicative type of \(\mathcal G\). For the existence of \(H\), see
\([\mathrm D,\) Proposition 2.1].

Let \(X(H)\) be the group of characters of \(H\). The action of \(H\) on \(\Lie(G)\) by
conjugation gives an \(X(H)\)-gradation
\[
  \Lie(G)=\Lie(Z^0_G(H))\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\ne0}\Lie(G)_\alpha.
\]
Recall that if \(M\) is a connected group scheme of multiplicative type over \(k\), the
group of characters \(X(M)\) is finitely generated and does not have torsion prime to
\(p\). As \(k\) is algebraically closed, this means that \(M\) is a product of subgroups
isomorphic to \(\Gm\) or \(\mu_{p^j}\) with \(j>0\).

\begin{corollary33}
Cf. \([\mathrm D,\) Lemma 2.12]. Let \(M\) be a connected subgroup scheme of
multiplicative type of \(G\), and suppose that \(p>h_G\). The action of \(M\) on
\(\Lie(G)\) by conjugation gives an \(X(M)\)-gradation
\[
  \Lie(G)=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in X(M)}\Lie(G)_\alpha.
\]
If \(\alpha\ne0\), then for \(\gamma\in\Lie(G)_\alpha\) one has \(\gamma^{[p]}=0\). In
particular, if \(M=H\) and \(\gamma\in\Lie(G)_\alpha\), we have \(\gamma^{[p]}=0\).
\end{corollary33}

\begin{proof}
If \(\alpha\) is of infinite order, it suffices to apply 2.10. If \(\alpha\) is of finite
order, there is a subgroup \(N\) of \(M\) isomorphic to \(\mu_{p^j}\) to which
\(\alpha\) restricts nontrivially, and it suffices to prove 3.3 for \(N\) and the
restriction of \(\alpha\) to \(N\). Choose an isomorphism of \(X(N)\) with
\(X(\mu_{p^j})\) such that the image of \(\alpha\) in \(\mathbb Z/p^j\) is of the form
\(p^b\), with \(0\le b<j\).

In a smooth algebraic group, the maximal connected subgroups of multiplicative type are
maximal tori. Indeed, if \(T\) is such a maximal subgroup, its centralizer \(Z_G(T)\) is
smooth, being the fixed locus of a linearly reductive group acting on a smooth variety;
see for example \([\mathrm{DG},\) Theorem 2.8, Chapter II, \S5]. Define
\(U:=Z_G(T)^0/T\). The group scheme \(U\) is smooth, as a quotient of \(Z_G(T)^0\),
which is smooth, and is unipotent by maximality of \(T\). By \([\mathrm{SGA3},\)
Exposé XVII, Theorem 6.1.1], we have a splitting
\(Z_G(T)^0\simeq T\times U\), and hence \(T\) is smooth.

In our case, \(N\) is hence contained in a maximal torus \(T\) of \(G\). Let
\begin{equation}
  \varphi:X(T)\longrightarrow \mathbb R/\mathbb Z
  \tag{3.3.1}
\end{equation}
be the composite of \(X(T)\to X(N)=\mathbb Z/p^j\) and of the inclusion
\(x\mapsto x/p^j\) of \(\mathbb Z/p^j\) in \(\mathbb R/\mathbb Z\). The \(N\)-weight
space \(\Lie(G)_\alpha\) is the sum of \(T\)-weight spaces \(\Lie(G)_\beta\), for
\(\beta\) a root such that \(\varphi(\beta)=1/p^{j-b}\). As
\[
  \frac1{p^{j-b}}\le \frac1p < \frac1h,
\]
we may apply 3.1, to conclude that \(\Lie(G)_\alpha\) is contained in the Lie algebra
of the unipotent radical of a Borel subgroup of \(G\). In particular, it consists of
nilpotent elements, proving 3.3.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Example 3.6 (Brian Conrad).}
Here is an example, in any characteristic \(p>0\), of a connected group of multiplicative
type \(M\) acting on a reductive group \(G\), and of a nontrivial character \(\alpha\) of
\(M\), such that the weight space \(\Lie(G)_\alpha\) contains elements which are not
nilpotent. We take \(G=\SL_p\), so that \(h_G=p\), and \(M=\mu_{p^2}\), and for
\(\alpha\) the character \(\zeta\mapsto\zeta^p\).

We embed \(M\) in the maximal torus of diagonal matrices of \(\SL_p\) by
\[
  \zeta\longmapsto \diag(\zeta^0,\zeta^{-p},\zeta^{-2p},\ldots,\zeta^{-(p-1)p}).
\]
The restriction to \(M\) of each simple root and of the lowest root is the character
\(\alpha:\zeta\mapsto\zeta^p\), and \(\Lie(G)_\alpha\) is the sum of the corresponding
root spaces. In the standard visualization of \(\SL_p\), this weight space inside
\(\Lie(G)=\mathfrak{sl}_p\) is the span of the superdiagonal entries and the lower-left
entry.

A sum of nonzero elements in those root lines contributing to \(\Lie(G)_\alpha\) is a
\(p\times p\)-matrix \(X\in\mathfrak{sl}_p\) which satisfies
\begin{equation}
  X(e_1)=t_p e_p,\qquad X(e_2)=t_1 e_1,
  \qquad\ldots,\qquad X(e_p)=t_{p-1}e_{p-1}.
  \tag{3.6.1}
\end{equation}
Iterating \(p\) times gives \(X^p=\diag(t,\ldots,t)\), with \(t:=\prod t_j\ne0\).
Hence \(X\in\Lie(G)_\alpha\) is not nilpotent.

\paragraph{Example 3.7.}
A variant of the above example leads to an example of an infinitesimally saturated group
scheme \(\mathcal G\subset\SL(V)\), with \(\dim(V)=p\), such that \(V\) is an
irreducible representation and \(\mathcal G_{\red}\) is a unipotent group. This in
particular implies that \(\mathcal G_{\red}\) is not normal in \(\mathcal G\).

Let \(V\) be the affine algebra of \(\mu_p\), that is
\begin{equation}
  V=\mathcal O(\mu_p):=\frac{k[u]}{(u^p-1)}.
  \tag{3.7.1}
\end{equation}
The vector space \(V\) admits the basis \(\{u^i\mid i\in\mathbb Z/p\}\). The
multiplicative group \(\mathcal O(\mu_p)^*\) acts by multiplication on \(V\). For
\(f\in\mathcal O(\mu_p)^*\), \(f^p\) is constant. Define \(N_f\) to be the constant
value of \(f^p\). It is in fact the norm of \(f\). The action of
\(\mathcal O(\mu_p)^*\) on \(V\) induces an action of the subgroup
\(N\subset\mathcal O(\mu_p)^*\) for which \(N_f=1\).

On \(V\) we also have the action of \(\mu_p\) by translations, and this action normalizes
the group \(\mathcal O(\mu_p)^*\) and its subgroup \(N\). Consider the group scheme
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal G:=\mu_p\ltimes N.
  \tag{3.7.2}
\end{equation}
We make a few observations on \(\mathcal G\):
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=2em]
\item The group \(\mathcal G\) is infinitesimally saturated.
\item In \(\mathcal G\), consider the first factor \(\mu_p\) and
\(\{u^i\mid i\in\mathbb Z/p\}\simeq\mathbb Z/p\). These subgroups generate a subgroup
\(H\), which is a central extension of \(\mu_p\ltimes\mathbb Z/p\) by \(\mu_p\).
\item The representation \(V\) is irreducible as an \(H\)-module. Indeed, a
\(\mu_p\)-submodule is generated by some of the \(u^i\), and if nonzero and
\(\mathbb Z/p\)-stable, contains all of them. A fortiori, \(V\) is an irreducible
\(\mathcal G\)-module.
\item The reduced group \(\mathcal G_{\red}\) can be identified with the unipotent group
\(\{f\in\mathcal O(\mu_p)^*\mid f(1)=1\}\).
\item The subgroup \(\mathcal G_{\red}\) is not normal, as the point \(1\) of \(\mu_p\)
is not invariant under translations.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Semi-simplicity statements}

Let \(G\) be a reductive group. Let \(C\) be an algebraic group and
\(\rho:C\to G\) a morphism.

\begin{definition41}[{[S2, page 20]}]
One says that \(\rho\) is \(\mathrm{cr}\) if, whenever \(\rho\) factors through a
parabolic \(P\) of \(G\), it factors through a Levi subgroup of \(P\).
\end{definition41}

When \(G=\GL(V)\), \(\rho\) is \(\mathrm{cr}\) if and only if the representation \(V\)
of \(C\) is completely reducible, or equivalently semisimple, and hence the terminology.

The property of \(\rho\) being \(\mathrm{cr}\) depends only on the subgroup scheme of
\(G\) which is the schematic image of \(C\). It in fact only depends on the image of
\(C\) in the adjoint group \(G^{\mathrm{ad}}\). Indeed, the parabolic subgroups of \(G\)
are the inverse images of the parabolic subgroups of \(G^{\mathrm{ad}}\), and similarly
for the Levi subgroups. A subgroup scheme \(\mathcal G\) of \(G\) will be called
\(\mathrm{cr}\) if its inclusion in \(G\) is so.

For an irreducible root system \(R\), let \(\alpha_0\) be the highest root and let
\(\sum n_i\alpha_i\) be its expression as a linear combination of the simple roots. The
characteristic \(p\) of \(k\) is called good for \(R\) if \(p\) is larger than each
\(n_i\). For a general root system \(R\), \(p\) is good if it is so for each irreducible
component of \(R\).

\begin{proposition42}
Suppose \(C\) is an extension
\begin{equation}
  1\longrightarrow B\longrightarrow C\longrightarrow A\longrightarrow 1
  \tag{4.2.1}
\end{equation}
with \(A^0\) of multiplicative type and \(A/A^0\) a finite group of order prime to
\(p\), and suppose that \(p\) is good. Let \(\rho:C\to G\) be a morphism. If the
restriction of \(\rho\) to \(B\) is \(\mathrm{cr}\), then \(\rho\) is \(\mathrm{cr}\).
\end{proposition42}

We do not know whether the proposition holds without the assumption that \(p\) is good.

\begin{proof}
Let \(P\) be a parabolic subgroup, \(U\) its unipotent radical, and
\(\mathfrak u\) the Lie algebra of \(U\). The parabolic \(P\) is said to be
\emph{restricted} if the nilpotence class of \(U\) is less than \(p\). If
\(P\) is a maximal parabolic corresponding to a simple root \(\alpha\), the
nilpotence class of \(U\) is the coefficient of \(\alpha\) in the highest root.
It follows that \(p\) is good if and only if all the maximal parabolic subgroups
are restricted. By \([\mathrm{Sei},\) Proposition 5.3], credited by the author
to Serre, see 6.6, if \(P\) is restricted, one obtains by specialization from
characteristic zero a \(P\)-equivariant isomorphism
\begin{equation}
  \exp:(\mathfrak u,\circ)\xrightarrow{\sim} U
  \tag{4.2.2}
\end{equation}
from \(\mathfrak u\), endowed with the Campbell--Hausdorff group law, to \(U\).

We will first show that whenever \(\rho\) factors through a restricted parabolic
subgroup \(P\) as above, if the restriction of \(\rho\) to \(B\) factors through
some Levi subgroup of \(P\), then \(\rho\) itself factors through some Levi
subgroup of \(P\).

The group \(U(k)\) acts on the right on the set \(\mathcal L(k)\) of Levi
subgroups of \(P\) by
\[
  u\in U(k) \quad\text{acts by}\quad L\longmapsto u^{-1}Lu.
\]
This action turns \(\mathcal L(k)\) into a \(U(k)\)-torsor. This expresses the
fact that two Levi subgroups are conjugate by a unique element of \(U(k)\). The
group \(P(k)\) acts on \(\mathcal L(k)\) and on \(U(k)\) by conjugation. This
turns \(\mathcal L(k)\) into an equivariant \(U(k)\)-torsor.

We will need a scheme-theoretic version of the above. Fix a Levi subgroup
\(L_0\). Let \(\mathcal L\) be the trivial \(U\)-torsor, that is, \(U\) with the
right action of \(U\) by right translations. We have the family of Levi subgroups
\[
  L_u:=u^{-1}L_0u
\]
parametrized by \(\mathcal L=U\). We let \(P\) act on \(U\) by conjugation, and
on \(\mathcal L\) as follows: if \(p=v\ell\) in \(P=U L_0\), then \(p\) acts on
\(\mathcal L=U\) by
\[
  u\longmapsto v^{-1}pup^{-1}.
\]
This turns \(\mathcal L\) into an equivariant \(U\)-torsor. When we pass to
\(k\)-points, and attach to \(u\in\mathcal L\) the Levi subgroup \(u^{-1}L_0u\),
we recover the previously described situation.

The morphism \(\rho:C\to P\) turns \(\mathcal L\) into an equivariant
\(U\)-torsor. A point \(x\) of \(\mathcal L\), corresponding to a Levi subgroup
\(L_x\), is fixed by \(C\), scheme-theoretically, if and only if \(\rho\) factors
through \(L_x\). This expresses the fact that a Levi subgroup is its own normalizer
in \(P\).

We want to prove that if \(B\) has a fixed point in \(\mathcal L\), then so does
\(C\). Let \(U^B\) be the subgroup of \(U\) fixed by \(B\), for the conjugation
action. If \(B\) has a fixed point in \(\mathcal L\), the fixed locus
\(\mathcal L^B\) is a \(U^B\)-torsor. As \(B\) is a normal subgroup of \(C\), the
group \(C\) acts on \(\mathcal L^B\) and on \(U^B\), and the action factors
through \(A\).

The isomorphism \(\exp:(\mathfrak u,\circ)\to U\) is compatible with the action
of \(B\) by conjugation. Hence it induces an isomorphism
\[
  (\mathfrak u^B,\circ)\xrightarrow{\sim}U^B.
\]
Let \(Z^i(\mathfrak u^B)\) be the central series of \(\mathfrak u^B\), and define
\[
  Z^i(U^B):=\exp\bigl(Z^i(\mathfrak u^B)\bigr).
\]
The isomorphism \(\exp\) induces an isomorphism between the vector group
\[
  \operatorname{Gr}_{Z}^{i}(\mathfrak u^B)
\]
and
\[
  Z^i(U^B)/Z^{i+1}(U^B),
\]
compatible with the action of \(A\). On
\(\operatorname{Gr}_{Z}^{i}(\mathfrak u^B)\), this action is linear.

The assumption on \(A\) amounts to saying that \(A\) is linearly reductive, that
is, all its representations are semi-simple. Equivalently, if \(k\) is the trivial
representation, any extension
\begin{equation}
  0\longrightarrow V\stackrel{a}{\longrightarrow}E
  \stackrel{b}{\longrightarrow}k\longrightarrow 0
  \tag{4.2.3}
\end{equation}
splits. Passing from \(E\) to \(b^{-1}(1)\), such extensions correspond to
\(A\)-equivariant \(V\)-torsors, and the extension splits if and only if \(A\) has
a scheme-theoretic fixed point on the corresponding torsor.

Define \(U_i^B\) to be \(U^B/Z^i(U^B)\), and \(\mathcal L_i^B\) to be the
\(U_i^B\)-torsor obtained from \(\mathcal L^B\) by pushing out by
\(U^B\to U_i^B\). We prove by induction on \(i\) that \(A\) has a fixed point on
\(\mathcal L_i^B\).

As \(U_1^B\) is trivial, the case \(i=1\) is trivial. If \(x\) is a fixed point of
\(A\) in \(\mathcal L_i^B\), the inverse image of \(x\) in \(\mathcal L_{i+1}^B\)
is an equivariant \(A\)-torsor on
\[
  \operatorname{Gr}_{Z}^{i}(\mathfrak u^B)
  \simeq Z^i(U^B)/Z^{i+1}(U^B).
\]
By linear reductivity, \(A\) has a fixed point on the inverse image. As the central
descending series of \(\mathfrak u\), and hence of \(\mathfrak u^B\), terminates,
this proves 4.2 for restricted parabolic subgroups.

We now prove 4.2 by induction on the dimension of \(G\). Suppose that \(\rho\)
factors through a proper parabolic subgroup \(P\). As \(p\) is good, there exists
a restricted proper parabolic \(Q\) containing \(P\), and \(P\) is the inverse image,
by the projection \(Q\to Q/R_u(Q)\), of a parabolic \(P'\) of \(Q/R_u(Q)\). Let
\(L\) be a Levi subgroup of \(Q\) through which \(\rho\) factors, and let \(P'_L\) be
the parabolic subgroup of \(L\) obtained as the inverse image of \(P'\) by the
isomorphism
\[
  L\xrightarrow{\sim} Q/R_u(Q).
\]
Levi subgroups of \(P'_L\) are Levi subgroups of \(P\), and it remains to apply the
induction hypothesis to \(L\), for which \(p\) is good too.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Remark 4.3.}
For several results related to 4.2 but in the setting of reduced subgroups, see
\([\mathrm{BMR1},\) Theorem 3.10] and \([\mathrm{BMR2},\) Theorem 1.1 and
Corollary 3.7].

Fix, in the reductive group \(G\), a maximal torus \(T\), and a system of simple
roots corresponding to a Borel subgroup \(B\) containing \(T\). Let \(U\) be the
unipotent radical of \(B\).

\begin{definition44}[{cf. [Dy], [S1], [IMP]}]
The Dynkin height \(\htG(V)\) of a representation \(V\) of \(G\) is the largest
among
\[
  \left\{\sum_{\alpha>0}\langle\lambda,\alpha^\vee\rangle\right\},
\]
where \(\lambda\) runs over the weights for the action of \(T\) on \(V\).
\end{definition44}

This notion and this terminology go back to Dynkin \([\mathrm{Dy},\) pages
331--332], where it is called ``height'', as in \([\mathrm{IMP}]\), while in Serre
\(([\mathrm{S1}]\) and \([\mathrm{S2}])\), it is simply \(n(V)\). If \(V\) is an
irreducible representation, with dominant weight \(\lambda^+\) and smallest weight
\(\lambda^-\), it is the sum of the coefficients of \(\lambda^+-\lambda^-\), expressed
as a linear combination of the simple roots.

It follows that the product in \(\End(V)\) of the action of \(\htG(V)+1\) elements
of \(\Lie(U)\) vanishes and, for \(\mathfrak n\) nilpotent in \(\Lie(G)\), one has
\[
  \mathfrak n^{\htG(V)+1}=0\quad\text{in }\End(V).
\]

\paragraph{4.5.}
The representation \(\rho:G\to\GL(V)\) is said to be of \emph{low height} if
\(p>\htG(V)\). By \([\mathrm{S2},\) Theorem 6, page 25], representations of low
height are semi-simple. One can show that if \(G\) admits a representation \(V\) of
low height which is almost faithful, meaning that its kernel is of multiplicative type,
then \(G\) satisfies Assumption 2.3. That \(p\ge h_G\) results from the more precise
statement that \(\htG(V)\ge h_G-1\), \([\mathrm{S3},\) (5.2.4), page 213]. For the
property that \(\widetilde G/G^0\) is étale, one uses the fact that the non-trivial
irreducible representation of \(\PGL(p)\) of the smallest height is the adjoint
representation, which is of height \(2p-2\).

We now assume that \(V\) is of low height, and that Assumption 2.3 holds for \(G\).
It follows that any nilpotent \(\mathfrak n\) in \(\Lie(G)\) satisfies
\(\mathfrak n^{[p]}=0\), and further, the exponential map (2.2.2) is defined. The image
\(d\rho(\mathfrak n)\) of \(\mathfrak n\) in
\(\Lie(\GL(V))=\End(V)\) also has a vanishing \(p\)-th power; hence
\(\exp(d\rho(\mathfrak n)t)\) is defined. By \([\mathrm{S2},\) Theorem 5, page 24],
one has the following compatibility statement.

\paragraph{4.6. Compatibility.}
If \(\mathfrak n\) in \(\Lie(G)\) is nilpotent, then
\begin{equation}
  \rho\bigl(\exp(t\mathfrak n)\bigr)=\exp\bigl(t\,d\rho(\mathfrak n)\bigr).
  \tag{4.6.1}
\end{equation}
As a consequence, if \(u^{[p]}=1\) in \(G\), then
\begin{equation}
  \rho(u^t)=\rho(u)^t.
  \tag{4.6.2}
\end{equation}

The following theorem is a schematic analogue of \([\mathrm{BT}]\), for \(p\) large enough.

\begin{theorem47}
Suppose that the reductive group \(G\) admits a low-height almost faithful representation
\(\rho:G\to\GL(V)\), and that \(p>h_G\). Then, for any non-trivial unipotent subgroup
\(U\) of \(G\), there exists a proper parabolic subgroup \(P\) of \(G\), containing the
normalizer \(N_G(U)\) of \(U\), and whose unipotent radical contains \(U\).
\end{theorem47}

The condition \(p>h_G\) implies that \(G\) satisfies Assumption 2.3. If \(G\) is simple
simply connected, it implies the existence of an almost faithful low-height representation
except for \(G\) of type \(F_4\), \(E_6\), \(E_7\), or \(E_8\), in which case the lowest
height of a non-trivial representation and the Coxeter number are respectively
\(16>12\), \(16>12\), \(27>18\), and \(58>30\). For these groups, we do not know
whether the conclusion of the theorem is valid assuming only \(p>h_G\).

\begin{proof}
Let \(V^U\) be the invariants of \(U\) acting on \(V\). It is not zero, because \(U\)
is unipotent. It is not \(V\), because the representation \(V\) is almost faithful, hence
faithful on \(U\). It does not have a \(U\)-stable supplement \(V'\) in \(V\), because
\(U\) would have invariants in \(V'\).

Let \(H\) be the subgroup scheme of \(G\) which stabilizes \(V^U\). It contains the
normalizer \(N_G(U)\) of \(U\). It is a doubly saturated subgroup scheme of \(G\).
Indeed, if \(h\in H(k)\) is of order \(p\), by (4.6.2),
\[
  \rho(h^t)=\rho(h)^t=
  \sum_{i<p}\binom{t}{i}(\rho(h)-1)^i,
\]
which stabilizes \(V^U\); and similarly, if \(\mathfrak n\in\Lie(H)\) is nilpotent,
then the \(\exp(t\mathfrak n)\) are in \(H\).

As \(p>h_G\), Theorem 2.5 ensures that \(H^0_{\red}\) is a normal subgroup scheme
of \(H\), and that the quotient \(H/H^0_{\red}\) is an extension of a finite group of
order prime to \(p\) by a group of multiplicative type. It follows that
\(U\subset H^0_{\red}\).

\begin{lemma48}
The group \(H^0_{\red}\) is not reductive.
\end{lemma48}

\begin{proof}
If it were, \(V\) would be a representation of low height of \(H^0_{\red}\),
\([\mathrm{S2},\) Corollary 1, page 25], hence a semi-simple representation of
\(H^0_{\red}\), and \(V^U\) would have in \(V\) an \(H^0_{\red}\)-stable supplement.
As \(V\) does not admit a supplement stable under \(U\subset H^0_{\red}\), this is absurd.
\end{proof}

If \(S\) is a doubly saturated subgroup scheme of \(G\), we will call
\(R_u(S^0_{\red})\) the unipotent radical of \(S\) and denote it simply by \(R_u(S)\).
By 2.5, it is a normal subgroup of \(S\), and \(S/R_u(S^0_{\red})\) does not contain
any normal unipotent subgroup. This justifies the terminology.

Define \(U_1:=R_u(H)\). By 4.8, it is a non-trivial unipotent subgroup of \(G\), and
we can iterate the construction. We define, for \(i\ge1\),
\[
  H_i:=\text{stabilizer of }V^{U_i}\subset V,
  \qquad U_{i+1}:=R_u(H_i).
\]
One has \(U\subset H^0_{\red}\) and
\begin{equation}
  U\subset N_G(U)\subset H\subset N_G(U_1)\subset H_1
  \subset N_G(U_2)\subset H_2\subset\cdots .
  \tag{4.8.1}
\end{equation}
The \((H_i)^0_{\red}\) form an increasing sequence of smooth connected subgroups of
\(G\). It stabilizes, hence so do the sequences of the \(U_i\) and of the \(H_i\). If
\(H_i=H_{i+1}\), one has \(H_i=N_G(U_{i+1})=H_{i+1}\) and
\[
  U_{i+1}=R_u(H_i)=R_u(H_{i+1})=R_u\bigl(N_G(U_{i+1})^0_{\red}\bigr).
\]
By \([\mathrm{BT},\) Proposition 2.3, page 99], or for example
\([\mathrm H,\) Section 30.3, Proposition on page 186], this implies that
\(N_G(U_{i+1})_{\red}=(H_{i+1})_{\red}\) is a proper parabolic subgroup of \(G\).
Call it \(Q\). A parabolic subgroup of \(G\) is its own normalizer scheme, cf.
\([\mathrm{SGA3},\) XII, 7.9], \([\mathrm{CGP},\) page 469]. As
\((H_{i+1})_{\red}=Q\) is normal in \(H_{i+1}\), it follows that \(H_{i+1}=Q\) and
that \(N_G(U)\subset Q\).

A Levi subgroup \(L\) of \(Q\) is a reductive subgroup of \(G\), hence satisfies the
assumptions of 4.7. It is isomorphic to \(Q/R_u(Q)\). If \(U\) is not contained in
\(R_u(Q)\), we can repeat the argument for the image \(\overline U\) of \(U\) in
\(Q/R_u(Q)\), which is isomorphic to \(L\). One obtains a proper parabolic subgroup of
\(Q/R_u(Q)\) which contains the normalizer of \(\overline U\). Its inverse image in \(Q\)
is a parabolic subgroup, properly contained in \(Q\) and containing the normalizer of
\(U\). Iterating, one eventually finds a parabolic \(P\) containing \(N_G(U)\) and such
that \(U\subset R_u(P)\).
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Remark 4.9.}
Let \(\widetilde G\) be the simply connected central extension of the derived group
\(G'\), and let \(\widetilde G=\prod_i\widetilde G_i\) be its simple factors. A
representation of low height \(V\) of \(G\) is almost faithful if and only if its
restriction to each \(\widetilde G_i\) is not trivial. It suffices to check this for each
\(\widetilde G_i\) separately. Thus we may assume that \(G\) is simply connected. The
existence of a non-trivial \(V\) of low height implies that \(p>2\) for \(G\) of type
\(B_n\), \(C_n\) with \(n\ge2\), or \(F_4\), and \(p>3\) for \(G\) of type \(G_2\).
Let \(\overline G\) be the image of \(G\) in \(\GL(V)\). If \(V\) is non-trivial,
\(u:G\to\overline G\) is an isogeny. We want to show that it is a central isogeny. If it
is not, the structure of isogenies, \([\mathrm{SGA3},\) XXII, 4.2.13], shows that
\(\ker(u)\) contains the kernel of the Frobenius. The weights of \(V\) are the
\(p\)-th powers and \(\htG(V)\) is a multiple of \(p\), contradicting the low-height
assumption.

\paragraph{4.10.}
If \(G\) is a reductive group for which Assumption 2.3 holds, and \(\mathcal G\) is a
subgroup scheme of \(G\), the \emph{double saturation} \(\mathcal G^*\) of
\(\mathcal G\) is the smallest doubly saturated subgroup scheme of \(G\) containing
\(\mathcal G\). It is the intersection of the doubly saturated subgroup schemes
containing \(\mathcal G\), and is obtained from \(\mathcal G\) by iterating the
construction of taking the group generated by \(\mathcal G\), the additive groups
\(\exp(t\mathfrak n)\) for \(\mathfrak n\) nilpotent in \(\Lie(\mathcal G)\), and
\(u^t\) for \(u\) of order \(p\) in \(\mathcal G(k)\).

\begin{corollary411}
Let \(V\) be a low-height almost faithful representation of a reductive group \(G\).
Assume that \(p>h_G\). Let \(\mathcal G\) be a subgroup scheme of \(G\), and let
\(\mathcal G^*\) be its double saturation. Then the following conditions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2em]
\item \(V\) is a semi-simple representation of \(\mathcal G\);
\item \(V\) is a semi-simple representation of \(\mathcal G^*\);
\item \(\mathcal G\) is \(\mathrm{cr}\) in \(G\);
\item \(\mathcal G^*\) is \(\mathrm{cr}\) in \(G\);
\item the unipotent radical of \((\mathcal G^*)^0_{\red}\) is trivial.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary411}

\begin{proof}
\((\mathrm i)\Leftrightarrow(\mathrm{ii})\): if \(W\) is a subspace of \(V\), the
stabilizer in \(G\) of \(W\) is doubly saturated, as we saw in the beginning of the
proof of 4.7. If \(\mathcal G\) stabilizes \(W\), it follows that \(\mathcal G^*\)
also stabilizes \(W\): the lattice of sub-representations of \(V\) is the same for
\(\mathcal G\) and \(\mathcal G^*\), hence the claim.

\((\mathrm{iii})\Leftrightarrow(\mathrm{iv})\): similarly, the parabolic subgroups of
\(G\) and their Levi subgroups are doubly saturated, hence contain \(\mathcal G\) if and
only if they contain \(\mathcal G^*\).

Not \((\mathrm v)\Rightarrow\) not \((\mathrm{ii})\): let \(U\) be the unipotent
radical of \((\mathcal G^*)^0_{\red}\). If it is non-trivial, \(V^U\ne V\), because
\(V\) is faithful on \(U\) and does not have a \(U\)-stable supplement. As \(U\) is
normal in \(\mathcal G^*\), by 2.5, \(V^U\) is a sub-representation for the action of
\(\mathcal G^*\) on \(V\). This contradicts the semi-simplicity of \(V\).

\((\mathrm v)\Rightarrow(\mathrm{ii})\): the representation \(V\) of the reductive group
\((\mathcal G^*)^0_{\red}\) is of low height, hence semi-simple. By 2.5,
\((\mathcal G^*)^0_{\red}\) is a normal subgroup of \(\mathcal G^*\), and the quotient
\(A\) is linearly reductive. If \(W\) is a sub-\(\mathcal G^*\)-representation of
\(V\), \(A\) acts on the affine space of \((\mathcal G^*)^0_{\red}\)-invariant
retractions \(V\to W\). It has a fixed point, whose kernel is a supplement to \(W\).

Not \((\mathrm v)\Rightarrow\) not \((\mathrm{iv})\): let \(U\) be the unipotent
radical of \((\mathcal G^*)^0_{\red}\). If it is not trivial, there exists a parabolic
\(P\) containing its normalizer, hence \(\mathcal G^*\), and the unipotent radical of
\(P\) contains \(U\), by 4.7. Thus no Levi subgroup of \(P\) can contain
\(\mathcal G^*\).

\((\mathrm v)\Rightarrow(\mathrm{iv})\): by \([\mathrm{S2},\) Theorem 7, page 26],
\((\mathcal G^*)^0_{\red}\) is \(\mathrm{cr}\) in \(G\), and one applies 4.2.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary412}
Let \(\rho:G\to\GL(V)\) be an almost faithful low-height representation, and let
\(v\in V\) be an element such that the \(G\)-orbit of \(v\) in \(V\) is closed. Then
there exists a connected central subgroup scheme of multiplicative type
\(M\subset G_v^0\) and a surjective homomorphism
\[
  M\times G_{v,\red}^{0}\longrightarrow G_v^0.
\]
\end{corollary412}

\begin{proof}
The orbit being closed in \(V\), and hence affine, the reduced stabilizer
\(G_{v,\red}\) is reductive, \([\mathrm{Bo}]\). Since \(p\ge h_G\), and since
stabilizers are doubly saturated and 2.5(2) holds for \(\mathcal G=G_v\), we get the
required result.
\end{proof}

We now observe that the results of \([\mathrm D,\) Section 6] can be obtained as a
consequence of 4.11. Note that by the remarks in \([\mathrm D,\) page 607] it suffices
to prove the semisimplicity results in the case when \(k\) is algebraically closed.

\begin{theorem413}
Let \(\mathcal G\) be an algebraic group. Let \((V_i)_{i\in I}\) be a finite family of
semi-simple \(\mathcal G\)-modules and let \(m_i\) be integers \(\ge0\). If
\begin{equation}
  \sum_i m_i(\dim V_i-m_i)<p,
  \tag{4.13.1}
\end{equation}
then the \(\mathcal G\)-module
\[
  \bigotimes_j \bigwedge^{m_j}V_j
\]
is semi-simple.
\end{theorem413}

\begin{proof}
Let \(G=\prod_j\GL(V_j)\) and \(V=\bigotimes_j\bigwedge^{m_j}V_j\). Then (4.13.1)
is simply the inequality \(p>\htG(V)\). Replacing \(\mathcal G\) by its image in
\(G\), we may and shall assume that \(\mathcal G\) is a subgroup scheme of \(G\).
Since the \(V_i\) are semi-simple \(\mathcal G\)-modules, it follows that
\(\mathcal G\) is \(G\)-cr, for \(G=\prod_j\GL(V_j)\). By \([\mathrm D,\) \S6.2],
we may also assume that \(p>\dim(V_j)=h_{\GL(V_j)}\) for every \(j\).

Hence, by working with the image of \(G\), and \(\mathcal G\), in \(\GL(V)\), and
applying 4.11, we conclude that \(V\) is semi-simple as a \(\mathcal G\)-module.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Remark 4.14.}
If \((V_i,q_i)\) is a non-degenerate quadratic space with \(\dim V_i=2d_i\) on which
\(\mathcal G\) acts by similitudes, then, by passing to a subgroup of index at most
\(2\) and mapping to the group of similitudes rather than \(\GL(V_i)\), one can replace
the term \(m_i(\dim V_i-m_i)\) by \(m_i(\dim V_i-m_i-1)\), when \(m_i\le 2d_i\).

\paragraph{Complete reducibility in the classical case.}
By 4.1, a subgroup scheme \(\mathcal G\subset G\) is called \(\mathrm{cr}\) if for
every parabolic subgroup \(P\subset G\) containing \(\mathcal G\), there exists an
opposite parabolic subgroup \(P'\) such that \(\mathcal G\subset P\cap P'\). Suppose
that \(\operatorname{char}(k)\ne2\) and let \(G\) be \(\SO(V)\), or \(\Sp(V)\) in any
characteristic, relative to a non-degenerate symmetric or alternating bilinear form
\(B\) on \(V\). In this situation, the notion of \(\mathrm{cr}\) can be interpreted as
follows: \(\mathcal G\) is \(\mathrm{cr}\) in \(G\) if and only if for every
\(\mathcal G\)-submodule \(W\subset V\) which is totally isotropic, there exists a
totally isotropic \(\mathcal G\)-submodule \(W'\) of the same dimension, such that the
restriction of \(B\) to \(W+W'\) is non-degenerate, cf. \([\mathrm{S3},\) Example
3.3.3, page 206].

\begin{lemma415}
Let the subgroup scheme \(\mathcal G\) of \(G\) be \(\mathrm{cr}\). Then the
\(\mathcal G\)-module \(V\) is semi-simple, and conversely.
\end{lemma415}

\begin{proof}
Let \(W\subset V\) be a \(\mathcal G\)-submodule. We need to produce a
\(\mathcal G\)-complement.

Consider \(W_1:=W\cap W^\perp\). If \(W_1=(0)\), then
\(W\oplus W^\perp=V\) and we are done. So let \(W_1\ne(0)\). Then \(W_1\) is a
\(\mathcal G\)-submodule which is totally isotropic; hence, by the \(\mathrm{cr}\)
property, we have a totally isotropic \(\mathcal G\)-submodule \(W'_1\) of the same
dimension as \(W_1\), such that the form \(B\) is non-degenerate on
\(W_1+W'_1\). In particular, \(W_1\cap W'_1=(0)\). Since \(W_1\subset W^\perp\),
we see that \(W\subset W_1^\perp\).

Let \(w\in W\cap W'_1\subset W_1^\perp\cap W'_1\) and suppose \(w\ne0\). Since
\(w\in W'_1\), there exists \(w'\in W_1\) such that \(B(w,w')\ne0\). On the other
hand, since \(w\in W_1^\perp\), \(B(w,v)=0\) for all \(v\in W_1\), and in particular
\(B(w,w')=0\), which contradicts the assumption that \(w\ne0\). Hence it follows that
\(W\cap W'_1=(0)\).

Thus \(W\subsetneq X=W\oplus W'_1\subset V\) is a \(\mathcal G\)-submodule. We
proceed similarly and get \(X_1=X\cap X^\perp\) and a totally isotropic
\(\mathcal G\)-submodule \(X'_1\), of the same dimension, such that
\(X\oplus X'_1\subset V\). If \(X_1=(0)\), then \(V=X\oplus X^\perp\), so we get a
\(\mathcal G\)-decomposition of \(V\) as
\[
  W\oplus W'_1\oplus\cdots,
\]
that is, a \(\mathcal G\)-complement of \(W\) in \(V\).

Conversely, let \(V\) be semi-simple as a \(\mathcal G\)-module. Let \(W\subset V\)
be a totally isotropic \(\mathcal G\)-submodule of dimension \(d\). Note that
\(d<\dim(V)/2\). We therefore have a \(\mathcal G\)-submodule \(Z\subset V\) such
that \(V=W\oplus Z\). The non-degenerate form \(B\) gives a \(\mathcal G\)-equivariant
isomorphism
\[
  \varphi:V\longrightarrow V^*=W^*\oplus Z^*.
\]
Since \(W\) is totally isotropic, \(\varphi(W)\cap W^*=(0)\). Hence
\(\varphi(W)\subset Z^*\).

Again, since \(W\) is totally isotropic, the restriction of \(B\) to \(Z\) is
non-degenerate, and hence we get an isomorphism \(\psi:Z^*\to Z\). Define
\[
  W':=\psi\circ\varphi(W).
\]
Then it is easily seen that \(W'\) is of dimension \(d\) and is also a totally isotropic
\(\mathcal G\)-invariant submodule of \(V\). Finally, \(B\) is non-degenerate on
\(W\oplus W'\). Hence \(\mathcal G\) is \(\mathrm{cr}\) in \(G\).
\end{proof}


\section{Étale slices in positive characteristics}

Let \(G\) act on an affine variety \(X\) and let \(x\) be a point of \(X\) whose orbit
\(G\cdot x\) is closed in \(X\). We prove a schematic analogue of Luna's étale slice
theorem (5.1, 5.6) for suitable bounds on the characteristic \(p\). In order to make
this precise, one needs to extend the notion of a slice in \([\mathrm{BR},\) Definition 7.1]
to a scheme-theoretic setting (see 5.6), and in the process one has to work with
schematic stabilizers of closed orbits. The key issue then becomes the semisimplicity
of the tangent space to \(x\) in \(X\) as a representation of the stabilizer of \(G\) at
\(x\). Here the structure theorem 2.5 becomes crucial, and the bounds on \(p\) are forced
as a consequence. In \([\mathrm{BR}]\) the scheme-theoretic aspects are eschewed, forcing
them to make the assumption that the orbit \(G\cdot x\) is ``separable'', i.e. the
stabilizer \(G_x\) is reduced. The bounds on the characteristic therefore do not show up
in \([\mathrm{BR}]\).

We begin this section with the following linear analogue of Luna's étale slice theorem
in positive characteristics.

\begin{theorem51}
Let \(V\) be a \(G\)-module with low height, i.e. such that \(p>\htG(V)\). Let
\(v\in V\) be an element such that the orbit \(G\cdot v\) is a closed orbit in \(V\).
Then there exists a \(G_v\)-invariant linear subspace \(S\) of \(V\) giving rise to a
commutative diagram
\begin{equation}
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large,row sep=large]
  G\times^{G_v}S \arrow[r,"\phi"] \arrow[d,"f"'] & V \arrow[d,"q"] \\
  (G\times^{G_v}S)\sslash G \arrow[r,"\ell"'] & V\sslash G
\end{tikzcd}
\tag{5.1.1}
\end{equation}
and \(G\)-equivariant open subsets \(U\subset (G\times^{G_v}S)\) containing the closed
orbit \(G\cdot v\), and an open subset \(U'\) of \(V\) containing \(v\), for which
(5.1.1) induces a cartesian diagram
\begin{equation}
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large,row sep=large]
  U \arrow[r,"\phi"] \arrow[d,"f"'] & U' \arrow[d,"q"] \\
  U\sslash G \arrow[r,"\ell|_{U}"'] & U'\sslash G
\end{tikzcd}
\tag{5.1.2}
\end{equation}
such that the morphism \(\ell|_{U}\) is étale.
\end{theorem51}

\paragraph{Remark 5.2.}
The above theorem was stated in the note \([\mathrm{MP}]\), whose proof contained serious
gaps, as was pointed out to the authors by Serre in private correspondence.

We note that \(G/G_v\) is constructed in \([\mathrm{DG},\) III, Proposition 3.5.2]. It
represents the quotient in the category of fppf sheaves. Furthermore, if
\(\pi_v:G\to V\), \(g\mapsto g\cdot v\), the image \(\operatorname{im}(\pi_v)\), as a
locally closed subscheme of \(V\) with its reduced scheme structure, can be identified
with the scheme \(G/G_v\). We call this locally closed subscheme the orbit \(G\cdot v\),
and have the identification \(G/G_v\simeq G\cdot v\), see also \([\mathrm{DG},\)
Proposition and Definition 1.6, III, \S3, page 325].

\begin{proposition53}
Let \(V\) be an arbitrary \(G\)-module. Let \(v\in V\), and suppose that there exists a
\(G_v\)-submodule \(S\) of the tangent space \(T_v(V)\), such that
\[
  T_v(V)=T_v(G\cdot v)\oplus S.
\]
Let \(G\) act on \(G\times S\) by \(h\cdot(g,s):=(h g,s)\). Then the \(G\)-morphism
\(\Phi:G\times S\to V\), given by \((g,s)\mapsto g\cdot v+g\cdot s\), descends to a
\(G\)-morphism
\begin{equation}
  \phi:G\times^{G_v}S\longrightarrow V
  \tag{5.3.1}
\end{equation}
which is étale at \((e,0)\), where \(e\) is the identity of \(G\).
\end{proposition53}

\begin{proof}
To ensure that \(\Phi\) descends to a morphism \(\phi\), we need to check, for every
commutative \(k\)-algebra \(A\), that \(\Phi\) is constant on all \(G_v(A)\)-orbits. For
simplicity of exposition we suppress \(A\).

Let \(\alpha\in G_v\) act on \(G\times S\) by
\[
  \alpha\cdot(g,s)=(g\alpha,\alpha^{-1}s).
\]
Observe that
\[
  \Phi\bigl(\alpha\cdot(g,s)\bigr)=\Phi(g\alpha,\alpha^{-1}s)
  =g\alpha\cdot v+g\alpha\alpha^{-1}\cdot s
  =g\cdot v+g\cdot s,
\]
since \(\alpha\) fixes \(v\). Therefore \(\Phi\) is constant on the \(G_v\)-orbits.
Since the action of \(G_v\) on \(G\times S\) is scheme-theoretically free, \(\Phi\)
descends to a morphism \(\phi:G\times^{G_v}S\to V\). Clearly the actions of \(G\) and
\(G_v\) on \(G\times S\) commute, and hence the descended morphism is also a
\(G\)-morphism.

Observe that the quotient morphism \(G\to G/G_v\) is a torsor for the group scheme
\(G_v\), locally trivial under the fppf topology. Since the action of \(G_v\) on \(S\)
is linear, the associated fibre space
\[
  \pi:G\times^{G_v}S\longrightarrow G/G_v
\]
is a locally free sheaf of rank \(\dim(S)\). In particular, \(G\times^{G_v}S\) is a
smooth \(k\)-scheme of finite type. Observe further that, under the morphism \(\phi\), the
zero section of the vector bundle \(\pi:G\times^{G_v}S\to G/G_v\) canonically maps onto
the orbit \(G\cdot v\subset V\), while the fibre \(\pi^{-1}(eG_v)\) of the identity coset
\(eG_v\in G/G_v\) maps isomorphically to the affine subspace \(S+v\subset V\). Since
\(T_v(V)=T_v(G\cdot v)\oplus S\) by assumption, it follows that the differential
\(d\phi_z\), at \(z=(e,0)\), is an isomorphism.

We now apply \([\mathrm D,\) Lemme 2.9] to conclude that the morphism
\(\phi:G\times^{G_v}S\to V\) is étale at \(z=(e,0)\).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition54}
Let \(V\) be a \(G\)-module such that \(p>\htG(V)\), and let \(v\in V\) be an element
such that the \(G\)-orbit of \(v\) in \(V\) is closed. Then there exists a
\(G_v\)-submodule \(S\subset V\) such that
\[
  V=T_v(G\cdot v)\oplus S
\]
as a \(G_v\)-module. In particular, the consequences of 5.3 hold.
\end{proposition54}

\begin{proof}
One knows that \(G_v^0\) is the kernel of the natural map \(G_v\to\pi_0(G_v)\) to the
group of connected components, and hence is a normal subgroup of finite index. Further,
we note that
\[
  |G_v/G_v^0|=|G_{v,\red}/G_{v,\red}^0|.
\]
Note also that since \(G_{v,\red}\) is a saturated subgroup of \(G\), by
\([\mathrm{S2},\) page 23, Property 3] the index \(|G_{v,\red}/G_{v,\red}^0|\) is prime
to \(p\).

Since \(G\cdot v\) is a closed orbit, by 4.12 and 2.5 we have an exact sequence
\begin{equation}
  1\longrightarrow G_{v,\red}^0\longrightarrow G_v^0\longrightarrow \tau\longrightarrow 1,
  \tag{5.4.1}
\end{equation}
where \(\tau\) is a multiplicative group scheme.

Now \(V\) is semi-simple as a \(G_{v,\red}^0\)-module, \(G_{v,\red}^0\) being a
saturated subgroup of \(G\), and also as a \(\tau\)-module, since \(\tau\) is
multiplicative and hence linearly reductive. Thus, by \([\mathrm D,\) Lemme 4.2],
\(V\) is semi-simple as a \(G_v^0\)-module and therefore as a \(G_v\)-module as well.

In particular, we have a \(G_v\)-supplement \(S\) for the \(G_v\)-invariant subspace
\(T_v(G\cdot v)\subset T_v(V)=V\), i.e. we have a \(G_v\)-decomposition
\(S\oplus T_v(G\cdot v)\) for \(V\).
\end{proof}

We recall the ``Fundamental lemma of Luna'', which holds in positive characteristics as
well and which is essential to complete the proof of Theorem 5.1.

\begin{lemma55}[{\([\mathrm{GIT},\) page 152]}]
Let \(\bar s:X\to Y\) be a \(G\)-morphism of affine \(G\)-schemes. Let \(F\subset X\)
be a closed orbit such that:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item \(\bar s\) is étale at some point of \(F\);
\item \(\bar s(F)\) is closed in \(Y\);
\item \(\bar s\) is injective on \(F\);
\item \(X\) is normal along \(F\).
\end{enumerate}
Then there are affine \(G\)-invariant open subsets \(U\subset X\) and \(U'\subset Y\),
with \(F\subset U\), such that
\[
  \bar s\sslash G:U\sslash G\longrightarrow U'\sslash G
\]
is étale and
\[
  (\bar s,p_U):U\longrightarrow U'\times_{U'\sslash G}U\sslash G
\]
is an isomorphism, where \(p_U:U\to U\sslash G\) is the quotient morphism.
\end{lemma55}

\paragraph{Proof of 5.1.}
As a first step, we need to show that the categorical quotient
\(G\times^{G_v}S\sslash G\) exists as a scheme. As we have seen in 5.3, the action of
\(G\) on \(G\times S\), via \(g\cdot(a,s)=(ga,s)\), and the twisted action of \(G_v\)
on \(G\times S\) commute, and hence
\begin{equation}
  k[G\times^{G_v}S]^G=(k[G\times S]^{G_v})^G=(k[G\times S]^G)^{G_v}=k[S]^{G_v}.
  \tag{5.5.1}
\end{equation}
Thus it is enough to show that \(k[S]^{G_v}\) is finitely generated, and we would have
\begin{equation}
  G\times^{G_v}S\sslash G\simeq S\sslash G_v.
  \tag{5.5.2}
\end{equation}
As we have observed in the proof of 5.4, \(G_v^0\subset G_v\) is a normal subgroup of
finite index. Thus, the finite generation of \(k[S]^{G_v}\) is reduced to checking finite
generation of \(k[S]^{G_v^0}\). We may therefore replace \(G_v\) by \(G_v^0\) in the
proof. Further, since \(G\cdot v\) is a closed orbit, \(G_{v,\red}\) is reductive.
Therefore, by 4.12, we have a surjection
\[
  M\times G_{v,\red}\longrightarrow G_v.
\]
Since \(M\) in \(G_v\) is central,
\[
  k[S]^{G_v}=\bigl(k[S]^{G_{v,\red}}\bigr)^M.
\]
Again, since \(G_{v,\red}\) is reductive, the ring of invariants
\(k[S]^{G_{v,\red}}\) is a finitely generated \(k\)-algebra. Since \(M\) is a group of
multiplicative type over an algebraically closed field, it is a product of \(\Gm\)'s and a
finite group scheme. Therefore, by \([\mathrm{GIT},\) Theorem 1.1] and \([\mathrm{AV},\)
page 113], the ring of invariants \(\bigl(k[S]^{G_{v,\red}}\bigr)^M\) is also finitely
generated. This proves the finite generation of \(k[S]^{G_v}\).

The commutativity of the diagram (5.1.1) now follows by the property of categorical
quotients by the group \(G\).

Now we check the conditions of Lemma 5.5. We consider the given closed \(G\)-orbit
\[
  F=G\times^{G_v}\{v\}\subset G\times^{G_v}S.
\]
Then we need to check that \(\phi(F)\) is a closed orbit in \(V\). In fact, \(\phi(F)\)
is precisely the closed orbit \(G\cdot\phi(v)=G\cdot v\subset V\). Thus we have verified
conditions (2) and (3) of Lemma 5.5. Condition (1) is precisely the content of 5.4, and
(4) holds since \(G\times^{G_v}S\) has been seen to be smooth.

The isomorphism \((\bar s,p_U)\) shows that the diagram (5.1.2) is cartesian. This
completes the proof of 5.1.

\begin{corollary56}
Let \(X\) be an affine \(G\)-scheme embeddable as a closed \(G\)-subscheme in a low
height \(G\)-module. Let \(x\in X\) be such that the orbit \(G\cdot x\subset X\) is closed.
Then there exists a locally closed \(G_x\)-invariant subscheme, a ``slice'' \(X_1\), of
\(X\), with \(x\in X_1\subset X\), such that the conclusions of 5.1 hold for the
\(G\)-morphism \(G\times^{G_x}X_1\to X\).
\end{corollary56}

\begin{proof}
This follows from 5.1, which corresponds to \([\mathrm{BR},\) Proposition 7.4], exactly as
in \([\mathrm{BR},\) Proposition 7.6].
\end{proof}

The following consequence of the slice theorem has many applications, so we state it here
without proof.

\begin{corollary57}[{see \([\mathrm{BR},\) Proposition 8.5, page 312]}]
Let \(F\) be an affine \(G\)-subvariety of \(\mathbb P(V)\), with \(V\) as a
\(G\)-module with low height, and suppose that \(F\) contains a unique closed orbit
\(F^{\mathrm{cl}}\). Then there exists a \(G\)-retract
\[
  F\longrightarrow F^{\mathrm{cl}}.
\]
\end{corollary57}

Let \(V\) be a finite dimensional \(G\)-module and let
\begin{equation}
  \htG\bigl(\bigwedge(V)\bigr):=\max_i\{\htG(\bigwedge^i V)\}.
  \tag{5.7.1}
\end{equation}
We then have the following application to the theory of semistable principal bundles in
positive characteristics.

\begin{theorem58}
Let \(E\) be a stable \(G\)-bundle with \(G\) semi-simple and
\(\rho_V:G\to\SL(V)\) be a representation such that
\(p>\htG(\bigwedge(V))\). Then the associated bundle \(E(V)\) is polystable of degree
\(0\).
\end{theorem58}

\begin{proof}
The proof follows \([\mathrm{BP},\) Theorem 9.11] verbatim, where the notion of
``separable index'' is replaced by the Dynkin height, the key ingredient being Corollary
5.7.
\end{proof}

\section{Added in proof}

In Section 2.2, after making suitable assumptions on \(G\), we refer to Serre in (2.2.2)
for the construction of the exponential map
\[
  \exp:\mathfrak g_{\nilp}\xrightarrow{\sim}G^{\unip}.
\]
The proof that Serre gives in \([\mathrm{S2},\) Theorem 3, page 21] is only a sketch. At
his suggestion, we complete it. We work in principle over an algebraically closed field, but
phrase the argument so that it remains valid over more general bases.

Let \(T\) be a maximal torus, \(B\) a Borel subgroup containing \(T\), and \(U\) its
unipotent radical. We assume \(p\ge h\). By this assumption on the characteristic, the
Campbell-Hausdorff group law \(\circ\) on \(\Lie(U)\) is well-defined. The first step is the
construction of a \(B\)-equivariant isomorphism
\begin{equation}
  \exp:(\Lie(U),\circ)\xrightarrow{\sim}U.
  \tag{6.0.1}
\end{equation}
For this step we give complete proofs. The second step is to glue these isomorphisms into
\begin{equation}
  \exp:\mathfrak g_{\nilp}\xrightarrow{\sim}G^{\unip}.
  \tag{6.0.2}
\end{equation}
This requires the additional assumption that the simply connected covering \(\widetilde G\)
of the derived group \(G'\) is étale over \(G'\). Here we will mainly give references.

\subsection*{6.1}
Let \(R^+\) be the set of positive roots: the set of roots of \(T\) acting on \(\Lie(U)\).
For \(\alpha\in R^+\), let \(U_\alpha\) be the corresponding root subgroup, and let
\(x_\alpha\) be an isomorphism from \(U_\alpha\) to \(\Ga\).

Fix a total order on \(R^+\). The corresponding product map from
\(\prod U_\alpha\) to \(U\) is an isomorphism of schemes: it is a morphism between smooth
connected schemes which is étale at the origin, bijective by \([\mathrm{Bible},\) 13-05],
and one applies Zariski's main theorem as in \([\mathrm{SGA3},\) XXII, 4.1], or see
\([\mathrm{Bo2},\) IV, 14.5].

This isomorphism identifies \(U\) with the affine space with coordinates
\((x_\alpha)_{\alpha\in R^+}\). In these coordinates, the action of \(T\) on \(U\) by
conjugation is given by
\[
  t\in T\quad\text{acts by}\quad (x_\alpha)\longmapsto(\alpha(t)x_\alpha).
\]
This action respects the group law. It follows that the \(\alpha\)-coordinate of
\(x\cdot y\) is a linear combination of the \(x_\beta^i y_\gamma^j\) for
\(i\beta+j\gamma=\alpha\).

We grade the polynomial algebra \(\mathcal O(U)\) by defining the degree of \(x_\alpha\)
to be the height of \(\alpha\). If the expression of \(\alpha\) as a linear combination of
simple roots is \(\sum n_i\alpha_i\), the height of \(\alpha\) is \(\sum n_i\). The
corresponding increasing filtration \(\Fil\) of \(\mathcal O(U)\) is invariant by
\(T\)-conjugation as well as by the action on the right and left by \(U\)-translations.
It is hence invariant by \(B\)-conjugation.

The assumption \(p\ge h\) is equivalent to \(\mathcal O(U)\) being generated by
\(\Fil_{p-1}\). The definition of the exponential (6.0.1) is a special case of the
following construction.


\subsection*{6.2}
Let \(U\) be an algebraic group and let \(\Fil\) be an increasing filtration of its affine algebra. Assume that
\(\OO(U)\) is a polynomial algebra \(k[(x_\alpha)_{\alpha\in A}]\), and that for some integers
\(d(\alpha)>0\), \(\Fil\) is the increasing filtration deduced from the grading for which
\(x_\alpha\) is homogeneous of degree \(d(\alpha)\). Assume that the \(\alpha\)-coordinate of
\(x\cdot y\) is a polynomial of degree \(\le d(\alpha)\) in the weighted coordinates of \(x\) and
\(y\). This is equivalent to the compatibility
\begin{equation}
  \Delta(\Fil_i)\subset \sum_{j+k=i}\Fil_j\otimes\Fil_k
  \tag{6.2.1}
\end{equation}
of the filtration \(\Fil\) with the coproduct.

We will view \(\Lie(U)\) as the Lie algebra of the left invariant vector fields on \(U\). Let
\(D_\alpha\) be the element of \(\Lie(U)\) which is \(\partial_\alpha\) at \(e\). If
\([\varepsilon]_\alpha\) is the \(k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)\)-valued point of \(U\) with
coordinate \(x_\alpha=\varepsilon\), and \(x_\beta=0\) for \(\beta\ne\alpha\), then
\(D_\alpha f\) is the coefficient of \(\varepsilon\) in \(f(x\cdot[\varepsilon]_\alpha)\). It follows that
\(D_\alpha(x_\beta)\) is of degree \(\le d(\beta)-d(\alpha)\), and consequently
\begin{equation}
  D_\alpha\Fil_i\subset \Fil_{i-d(\alpha)}.
  \tag{6.2.2}
\end{equation}
Thus \(D_\alpha\) is of descending filtration \(d(\alpha)\).

We now assume that \(\OO(U)\) is generated by \(\Fil_{p-1}\), i.e. that all \(d(\alpha)\) are
less than \(p\). Then any Lie polynomial of degree \(\ge p\) in the derivations \(D_i\) in
\(\Lie(U)\) vanishes on the \(x_\alpha\), and hence vanishes identically. It follows that the
Campbell--Hausdorff group law \(\circ\) on \(\Lie(U)\) is defined.

\begin{definition63}
The exponential map \(\exp\) from \(\Lie(U)\) to \(U\) is the map having as coordinates the
truncated exponential
\begin{equation}
  \exp(D)_\alpha:=\sum_{n<p}\left(\frac{D^n}{n!}(x_\alpha)\text{ at }e\right).
  \tag{6.3.1}
\end{equation}
\end{definition63}

\begin{lemma64}
For \(f\in\Fil_{p-1}\), one has
\begin{equation}
  f(\exp(D))=\sum_{n<p}\left(\frac{D^n}{n!}(f)\text{ at }e\right).
  \tag{6.4.1}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma64}

\begin{proof}
This holds by the definition of \(\exp\) when \(f\) is a coordinate function \(x_\alpha\). It
therefore suffices to check that, for \(g\in\Fil_a\) and \(h\in\Fil_b\) with \(a+b<p\), one has
\begin{equation}
  \sum\frac{D^n}{n!}(gh)=\sum\frac{D^n}{n!}(g)\sum\frac{D^n}{n!}(h),
  \tag{6.4.2}
\end{equation}
the sums being truncated at \(n<p\). Both sides can be identified with sums
\(\sum \frac{D^n}{n!}(g)\frac{D^m}{m!}(h)\), where on the left we have \(n+m<p\), and on the
right \(n<p\) and \(m<p\). If \(n+m\ge p\), then either \(n>a\) or \(m>b\), in which case
\(D^n(g)=0\), respectively \(D^m(h)=0\). The terms missing on the left therefore vanish.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma65}
The morphism \(\exp\) is a group homomorphism
\begin{equation}
  \exp:(\Lie(U),\circ)\longrightarrow U.
  \tag{6.5.1}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma65}

\begin{proof}
If \(f\in\Fil_{p-1}\), then so are its left-translates. Since the derivations \(D\) in
\(\Lie(U)\) are left invariant, (6.4.1) applied to the left-translate of \(f\) gives
\begin{equation}
  f(x\cdot\exp(D))=\sum_{n<p}\left(\frac{D^n}{n!}(f)\text{ at }x\right),
  \tag{6.5.2}
\end{equation}
hence
\begin{equation}
  f(\exp(D')\cdot\exp(D))=
  \sum_{n,m<p}\left(\frac{(D')^m}{m!}\frac{D^n}{n!}(f)\text{ at }e\right).
  \tag{6.5.3}
\end{equation}

In the completed free associative algebra \(\mathbb Q\langle\!\langle X,Y\rangle\!\rangle\), if
\(H_d(X,Y)\) is the Lie polynomial which is the degree \(d\) component of the
Campbell--Hausdorff group law, one has
\begin{equation}
  \exp\left(\sum H_d(X,Y)\right)=\exp(X)\cdot\exp(Y).
  \tag{6.5.4}
\end{equation}
Modulo terms of degree \(\ge p\), one still has
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{n\le p}\frac{\left(\sum_{d<p}H_d(X,Y)\right)^n}{n!}
  \equiv
  \sum_{n<p}\frac{X^n}{n!}\sum_{n<p}\frac{Y^n}{n!},
  \tag{6.5.5}
\end{equation}
and this continues to hold in \(\mathbb Z[1/(p-1)!]\langle\!\langle X,Y\rangle\!\rangle\). Substituting
\(D'\) and \(D\) for \(X\) and \(Y\), we get
\begin{equation}
  f(\exp(D')\cdot\exp(D))=f(\exp(D'\circ D))
  \tag{6.5.6}
\end{equation}
for \(f\in\Fil_{p-1}\). Hence \(\exp(D')\cdot\exp(D)=\exp(D'\circ D)\).

Define \(\Delta_a f\) to be \(f(xa)-f(x)\). For \(f\in\Fil_{p-1}\), (6.5.2) reads
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\exp(D)} f=\sum_{0<n<p}\frac{D^n}{n!}(f).
  \tag{6.5.7}
\end{equation}
In \(\mathbb Q[[X]]\), one has \(\log\exp(X)=X\). Modulo monomials of degree \(\ge p\), one still has
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{0<n<p}(-1)^{n+1}\left(\sum_{0<m<p}\frac{X^m}{m!}\right)^n/n \equiv X,
  \tag{6.5.8}
\end{equation}
and this continues to hold in \(\mathbb Z[1/(p-1)!][[X]]\). Substituting \(D\) for \(X\) in
(6.5.8), we obtain from (6.5.7) that, for \(f\in\Fil_{p-1}\),
\begin{equation}
  Df=\sum_{0<n<p}(-1)^{n+1}\Delta_{\exp(D)}^n f/n.
  \tag{6.5.9}
\end{equation}
This gives a formula for a left inverse \(a\mapsto\log a\) of
\(\exp:\Lie(U)\to U\); viewed as a derivation,
\begin{equation}
  \log a=\sum_{0<n<p}(-1)^{n+1}\Delta_a^n/n
  \tag{6.5.10}
\end{equation}
when applied to \(f\in\Fil_{p-1}\). This shows that the exponential is injective and hence,
being an étale homomorphism of connected groups, it is an isomorphism.

In the case of the unipotent radical \(U\) of a Borel subgroup, the given construction of the
exponential map, depending only on the filtration \(\Fil\) of \(\OO(U)\), is invariant by
\(B\)-conjugation.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Remark 6.6.}
(i) Let \(I\subset R^+\) be the set of simple roots, and for \(J\subset I\), let \(P_J\) be the
corresponding parabolic subgroup and \(U_J\) its unipotent radical. Let
\(R_J^+\subset R^+\) be the set of \(\alpha\in R^+\) which are linear combinations of simple
roots in \(J\). For \(\alpha=\sum n_i\alpha_i\) in \(R^+-R_J^+\), define
\(d_J(\alpha)=\sum_{i\notin J} n_i\). Then, if all \(d_J(\alpha)\) are \(<p\), the construction
we have given of the exponential map gives a similar map
\begin{equation}
  \exp:\Lie(U_J)\longrightarrow U_J.
  \tag{6.6.1}
\end{equation}
It suffices to replace \(d\) by \(d_J\) in the earlier proofs.

(ii) More generally, let \(\Gamma\subset R^+\) be a closed set of positive roots, and let
\(U_\Gamma\) be the corresponding subgroup of \(U\). For any fixed total order on \(\Gamma\), the
corresponding product map
\begin{equation}
  \prod_{\alpha\in\Gamma}U_\alpha\longrightarrow U_\Gamma
  \tag{6.6.2}
\end{equation}
is an isomorphism of schemes. For \(\alpha\in\Gamma\), define \(d_\Gamma(\alpha)\) to be the
largest number of elements of \(\Gamma\) of which \(\alpha\) is the sum, with repetitions allowed.

In \([\mathrm{Sei}]\), Seitz states that when all \(d_\Gamma(\alpha)\) are \(<p\), an exponential
map from \(\Lie(U_\Gamma)\) to \(U_\Gamma\) is defined. He writes, before \([\mathrm{Sei},\)
Proposition 5.1, page 483], that the argument in Section 4 of \([\mathrm{S1}]\) yields this
result. The reference is mistaken and should be replaced by Section 2.1 of \([\mathrm{S4}]\). The
argument given in the preceding paragraphs, with \(d\) replaced by \(d_\Gamma\), also yields
the result.

\subsection*{6.7}
Let \(\mathcal B\) be the variety of Borel subgroups of \(G\). We have fiber spaces \(X\) and
\(Y\) over \(\mathcal B\), where the fibers at a Borel subgroup \(B\) in \(\mathcal B\) are
respectively the unipotent radical \(U\) and its Lie algebra. The given construction of the
exponential map works over any base on which the primes \(\ell<h\) are invertible. Over
\(\mathcal B\), we therefore obtain an isomorphism \(\exp:Y\xrightarrow{\sim}X\). The natural
map from \(X\) to \(G\), respectively from \(Y\) to \(\Lie(G)\), is proper, being the restriction
to a closed subset of \(\mathcal B\times G\), respectively \(\mathcal B\times\Lie(G)\), of the
second projection.

The reduced subscheme \(\mathfrak g_{\nilp}\), respectively \(G^{\unip}\), of \(\Lie(G)\),
respectively \(G\), with points the nilpotent, respectively unipotent, elements, is the
schematic image of \(Y\) in \(\Lie(G)\), respectively of \(X\) in \(G\). We want to complete the
following \(G\)-equivariant diagram in the solid arrows by an isomorphism at the dotted arrow:
\begin{equation}
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large,row sep=large]
  Y \arrow[r,"\exp"] \arrow[d] & X \arrow[d] \\
  \mathfrak g_{\nilp} \arrow[r,dashed] & G^{\unip}
\end{tikzcd}
\tag{6.7.1}
\end{equation}

Replacing \(G\) by its derived group \(G'\) does not change the diagram, nor does replacing a
semi-simple \(G\) by its simply connected covering \(\widetilde G\), provided
\(\widetilde G\) is étale over \(G\). Under our assumptions this reduces us to the case where
\(G\) is semi-simple and simply connected. Here are references for the existence of the dotted
arrow isomorphism in (6.7.1).

(A) By \([\mathrm{St},\) 6.11, 8.1], \(G^{\unip}\) is normal with a smooth dense orbit whose
complement is of codimension \(\ge 2\). This holds in any characteristic for any simply
connected \(G\). Normality results from \(G^{\unip}\) being the complete intersection in \(G\)
defined by the equations \(\chi(g)=\chi(e)\) for \(\chi\) the characters of the fundamental
representations, and being smooth outside codimension \(2\).

(B) The same holds for \(\mathfrak g_{\nilp}\) when \(G\) is simply connected and \(p\) good,
because \(\mathfrak g_{\nilp}\) is then equivariantly isomorphic to \(G^{\unip}\). A nice proof
is in \([\mathrm{BR},\) Corollary 9.3.4]. The isomorphism uses a representation \((V,\rho)\)
such that the pairing \(\Tr(\rho(X)\rho(Y))\) on \(\Lie(G)\) is non-degenerate. It is then
defined as induced by the map \(g\mapsto \rho(g)-1\) from \(G\) to \(\End(V)\), composed with
the orthogonal projection of \(\End(V)\), endowed with the symmetric bilinear form \(\Tr(XY)\),
to \(\Lie(G)\subset\End(V)\). This does not work for \(\SL(n)\), for which one uses
\(X\mapsto 1+X\).

(C) Over the dense orbits of \(\mathfrak g_{\nilp}\) and \(G^{\unip}\), (6.7.1) is a system of
isomorphisms, and the composite isomorphism extends across codimension \(2\) by normality.

\section*{Appendix. Coxeter number and root systems, by Zhiwei Yun}

\begin{propositionA1}
Let \(R\) be an irreducible root system associated to a simple group \(G\) with Coxeter number
\(h\), and let \(X\) be the lattice spanned by \(R\). Let
\(\varphi:X\to\mathbb R/\mathbb Z\) be a homomorphism. Then there exists a basis \(B\) for
\(R\) such that if \(\alpha\in R\) satisfies
\(\varphi(\alpha)\in(0,1/h)\bmod\mathbb Z\), then \(\alpha\) is positive with respect to
\(B\).
\end{propositionA1}

\begin{proof}
Pick any basis \(B_0=\{\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_r\}\) of \(R\). We lift \(\varphi\) to a linear
map \(y:X\to\mathbb R\), which is viewed as a point in \(V=\operatorname{Hom}(X,\mathbb R)\).

Let \(W\) be the Weyl group of \(R\). Then there is an action of the extended Weyl group
\(\operatorname{Hom}(X,\mathbb Z)\rtimes W\) on \(V\), and the validity of the statement for
the linear map \(y\) is invariant for this action. Therefore we may assume that \(y\) lies in
the fundamental alcove attached to \(B_0\), i.e.
\begin{align}
  \alpha_j(y)&\ge 0,\qquad 1\le j\le r, \tag{A1.1}\\
  \theta(y)&\le 1, \tag{A1.2}
\end{align}
where \(\theta\) is the highest root with respect to \(B_0\). Then, for all \(\alpha\in R\),
\begin{equation}
  |\alpha(y)|\le 1.
  \tag{A1.3}
\end{equation}

We claim that there exists a basis \(B\) of \(R\) such that, for any \(\beta\in R\) with
\begin{equation}
  \beta(y)\in (-1,-1+1/h)\cup(0,1/h),
  \tag{A1.4}
\end{equation}
\(\beta\) is positive with respect to \(B\).

Write \(\theta=\sum_i n_i\alpha_i\), and let \(\alpha_0=1-\theta\) and \(n_0=1\), as usual.
Then we have the equality \(\sum_{i=0}^r n_i\alpha_i=1\), viewed as affine functions on \(V\),
and we have
\begin{equation}
  h=\sum_{i=0}^r n_i.
  \tag{A1.5}
\end{equation}
Therefore, for some \(0\le i\le r\), we have \(\alpha_i(y)\ge 1/h\).

If \(i=0\), then \(\theta(y)\le 1-1/h\), and in this case we can take \(B=B_0\). In fact, for
any negative root \(-\beta\) with respect to \(B_0\), we have
\(0\ge -\beta(y)\ge -1+1/h\), so that \(-\beta\) cannot satisfy condition (A1.4).

If \(i>0\), let \(v_i\) be the vertex of the fundamental alcove opposite to the root
hyperplane \(\alpha_i\). Thus \(\alpha_j(v_i)=0\) if \(j\ne i\), and
\(\alpha_i(v_i)=1/n_i\). We take a basis \(B\) of \(R\) such that the vector \(y-v_i\) is
dominant, i.e. for any \(\beta\in R\) positive for \(B\), one has \(\beta(y)\ge\beta(v_i)\).

Such a basis \(B\) satisfies the requirements of the claim. In fact, let \(\beta\in R\) be
negative with respect to \(B\). Write \(\beta=\sum_{j=1}^r m_j\alpha_j\), with
\(-n_j\le m_j\le n_j\). Then \(\beta(v_i)=m_i/n_i\). On the other hand,
\(\beta(y)=\sum_{j=1}^r m_j\alpha_j(y)\). Since \(\beta\) is negative for \(B\), we have
\begin{equation}
  \beta(y)\le \beta(v_i)=m_i/n_i.
  \tag{A1.6}
\end{equation}
We have two cases.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item If \(\beta>0\) with respect to \(B_0\), then \(\beta(y)\ge0\) and
\(0\le m_j\le n_j\) for all \(j\). If \(m_i=0\), then (A1.6) implies that \(\beta(y)=0\), and
so \(\beta\) fails to satisfy (A1.4). If \(m_i\ge1\), then
\(\beta(y)\ge\alpha_i(y)\ge1/h\), again not satisfying (A1.4).

\item If \(\beta<0\) with respect to \(B_0\), then \(-1\le\beta(y)\le0\) and
\(-n_j\le m_j\le0\) for all \(j\). If \(m_i=-n_i\), then \(\beta(v_i)=-1\), which forces
\(\beta(y)=-1\) by (A1.6). If \(m_i\ge -n_i+1\), then, comparing \(|\beta(y)|\) with
\(\theta(y)\), it misses at least one copy of \(\alpha_i(y)\); therefore
\(|\beta(y)|\le \theta(y)-\alpha_i(y)\le1-1/h\), and again \(\beta(y)\) does not satisfy
(A1.4).
\end{itemize}

This proves the claim and the proposition.
\end{proof}
