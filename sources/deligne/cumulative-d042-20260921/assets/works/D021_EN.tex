\DTitle{MODULAR FORMS OF WEIGHT 1}

\DByline{BY PIERRE DELIGNE AND JEAN-PIERRE SERRE}

\DDedication{To Henri Cartan,\\on the occasion of his\\70th birthday}

\DSection{Introduction}{introduction-1}

The Euler product decomposition and the functional equation of the Dirichlet series associated by Hecke with modular forms of weight 1 suggest that these forms correspond to Artin \(L\)-functions of degree 2 over \(\mathbf{Q}\), in other words to representations of \(\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{\mathbf{Q}}/\mathbf{Q})\) in \(\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbf{C})\). It is such a correspondence, conjectured by Langlands, that we establish here.\par

The first three sections are preliminary. Section 4 contains the statement of the main theorem, together with several supplements. The proof occupies §§ 5 through 9. Its principle is as follows: for every prime number \(\ell\), one first constructs a representation of \(\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{\mathbf{Q}}/\mathbf{Q})\) in characteristic \(\ell\) (cf. § 6); one then shows that the images of \(\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{\mathbf{Q}}/\mathbf{Q})\) in these various representations are “small,” which makes it possible to lift them to characteristic 0 and obtain the desired complex representation (§§ 7 and 8); the “smallness” in question itself follows from an average bound for the eigenvalues of the Hecke operators (Rankin, cf. § 5). Section 9 contains an estimate for the coefficients of modular forms of weight 1.\par

We point out that at one essential point (§ 6, Theorem 6.1) we have used results proved by one of us (P. Deligne), but for which no complete proof has yet been published; pending such a publication (as well as that of SGA 5, on which these results depend), we ask the reader to accept them.\par

1.1. Let \(N\) be an integer \(\geq 1\). With \(N\) we associate the subgroups\par

\begin{equation*}
\Gamma(N)\subset\Gamma_1(N)\subset\Gamma_0(N).
\end{equation*}

of \(\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbf{Z})\) defined by\par

\begin{equation*}
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma(N)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 a\equiv d\equiv 1\pmod N\quad\text{and}\quad b\equiv c\equiv 0\pmod N,
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma_1(N)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 a\equiv d\equiv 1\pmod N\quad\text{and}\quad c\equiv 0\pmod N,
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma_0(N)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 c\equiv 0\pmod N.
\end{equation*}

1.2. Let \(f\) be a function on the upper half-plane \(H=\{z\mid\operatorname{Im}(z)>0\}\). If \(k\) is an integer and \(\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\) is an element of \(\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbf{R})\), set\par

\begin{equation*}
(f\mid_k\gamma)(z)=(cz+d)^{-k}f(\gamma z),
\qquad\text{where}\qquad
\gamma z=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}.
\end{equation*}

Let \(\Gamma\) be a subgroup of \(\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbf{Z})\) containing \(\Gamma(N)\). We say that \(f\) is modular of weight \(k\) on \(\Gamma\) if:\par

(1.2.1) \(f\mid_k\gamma=f\) for every \(\gamma\in\Gamma\);\par

(1.2.2) \(f\) is holomorphic on \(H\);\par

(1.2.3) \(f\) is “holomorphic at the cusps,” i.e., for every \(\sigma\in\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbf{Z})\), the function \(f\mid_k\sigma\) has a power-series expansion in \(e^{2\pi iz/N}\) with exponents \(\geq 0\).\par

If, in (1.2.3), “exponents \(\geq 0\)” is replaced by “exponents \(>0\),” one obtains the notion of a cusp form.\par

1.3. Let \(f\) be a modular form of weight \(k\) on \(\Gamma(N)\). For \(f\) to be a modular form on \(\Gamma_1(N)\), it is necessary and sufficient that \(f(z+1)=f(z)\), or equivalently that \(f\) have an expansion of the form\par

\begin{equation*}
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nq^n,
\qquad\text{where}\qquad q=e^{2\pi iz}.
\end{equation*}

In what follows, this expansion will be denoted by \(f_\infty(q)\), or simply by \(f\).\par

1.4. Let \(f\) be a modular form of weight \(k\) on \(\Gamma_1(N)\). If \(\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\) is an element of \(\Gamma_0(N)\), the form \(f\mid_k\gamma\) depends only on the image of \(d\) in \((\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z})^*\); it is denoted by \(f\mid R_d\). We have \(f\mid R_{-1}=(-1)^kf\).\par

1.5. Let \(\varepsilon\) be a Dirichlet character modulo \(N\), that is, a homomorphism\par

\begin{equation*}
\varepsilon:(\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z})^*\longrightarrow\mathbf{C}^*.
\end{equation*}

We say that \(\varepsilon\) is even (respectively odd) if \(\varepsilon(-1)=1\) (respectively if \(\varepsilon(-1)=-1\)).\par

Let \(k\) be an integer of the same parity as \(\varepsilon\) [i.e., \(\varepsilon(-1)=(-1)^k\)]. A modular form of type \((k,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\) is a modular form \(f\) of weight \(k\) on \(\Gamma_1(N)\) such that\par

\begin{equation*}
f\mid R_d=\varepsilon(d)f
\end{equation*}

for every \(d\in(\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z})^*\), i.e.,\par

\begin{equation*}
f\!\left(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\right)=\varepsilon(d)(cz+d)^kf(z)
\quad\text{for every}\quad
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma_0(N).
\end{equation*}

(Note that if \(\varepsilon\) and \(k\) did not have the same parity, this formula would imply \(f=0\).)\par

Every modular form of weight \(k\) on \(\Gamma_1(N)\) is a linear combination of forms of types \((k,\varepsilon_i)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\), where the \(\varepsilon_i\) are the various characters of \((\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z})^*\) having the same parity as \(k\).\par

This follows (cf. [16], p. IV-13) by observing that the map\par

\begin{equation*}
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\longmapsto d\pmod N
\end{equation*}

induces an isomorphism from \(\Gamma_0(N)/\Gamma_1(N)\) onto \((\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z})^*\).\par

1.6. HECKE OPERATORS. — Let \(f=\sum a_nq^n\) be a modular form of type \((k,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\), and let \(p\) be a prime number. Set\par

\begin{equation*}
\tag{1.6.1}
f\mid T_p=\sum a_{pn}q^n+\varepsilon(p)p^{k-1}\sum a_nq^{pn}
\qquad\text{if }p\nmid N,
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
\tag{1.6.2}
f\mid U_p=\sum a_{pn}q^n
\qquad\text{if }p\mid N.
\end{equation*}

This gives another modular form of type \((k,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\), which is a cusp form if \(f\) is.\par

1.7. PRIMITIVE FORMS. — We refer to [2] (when \(\varepsilon=1\)) and to [6], [12], [13] (in the general case) for the definition of primitive cusp forms (“newforms”) of type \((k,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\).\par

If \(f=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_nq^n\) is such a form, then \(a_1=1\), and \(f\) is an eigenform for the Hecke operators \(T_p\) and \(U_p\), with corresponding eigenvalues \(a_p\). It follows that the Dirichlet series\par

\begin{equation*}
\tag{1.7.1}
\Phi_f(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_nn^{-s}
\end{equation*}

has the Euler product\par

\begin{equation*}
\tag{1.7.2}
\Phi_f(s)=
\prod_{p\mid N}\frac{1}{1-a_pp^{-s}}
\prod_{p\nmid N}\frac{1}{1-a_pp^{-s}+\varepsilon(p)p^{k-1-2s}}.
\end{equation*}

1.8. If \(f\) is as above and \(p\mid N\), the absolute value of \(a_p\) is given by the following rule ([12], [14]):\par

\begin{equation*}
a_p=0\quad\text{if }p^2\mid N\text{ and if }\varepsilon\text{ can be defined modulo }N/p;
\end{equation*}

\newpage

\DPageUnit{5}{510}{4}

\begin{equation*}
|a_p|=p^{(k-1)/2}\quad\text{if }\varepsilon\text{ cannot be defined modulo }N/p;
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
|a_p|=p^{k/2-1}\quad\text{if }p^2\nmid N\text{ and if }\varepsilon\text{ can be defined modulo }N/p.
\end{equation*}

(It will follow from Theorem 4.6 below that the last case does not occur when \(k=1\).)\par

1.9. Every form \(f\) of type \((k,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\) can be written\par

\begin{equation*}
f(z)=E(z)+\sum\lambda_i f_i(d_i z),
\end{equation*}

where \(E\) is an Eisenstein series and \(f_i\) is a primitive cusp form of type \((k,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N_i)\), with \(N_i\) a divisor of \(N\) such that \(\varepsilon\) can be defined modulo \(N_i\), and \(d_i\) a divisor of \(N/N_i\). Moreover, this decomposition is unique in the evident sense.\par

\DSectionNamed{§ 2. Review (geometric) of modular forms}{Section 2: Review (geometric) of modular forms}{section-2}

2.1. Let \(k\) and \(N\) be integers \(\geq 1\), and let \(\mu_N\) be the group scheme of \(N\)th roots of unity. From the geometric point of view, a modular form of weight \(k\) on \(\Gamma_1(N)\) is a rule that assigns, to every elliptic curve \(E\) equipped with an embedding \(\alpha:\mu_N\longrightarrow E\), a section of the \(k\)th tensor power \(\omega_E^{\otimes k}\) of \(\omega_E\), where \(\omega_E\) is the dual of the Lie algebra of \(E\).\par

More precisely:\par

(a) An elliptic curve over a scheme \(S\) is a proper smooth morphism \(E\longrightarrow S\), equipped with a section \(e:S\longrightarrow E\), whose geometric fibers are elliptic curves. When \(S\) is the spectrum of a commutative ring \(A\), we also say that \(E\) is an elliptic curve over \(A\). We set \(\omega_E=e^*\Omega^1_{E/S}\); when \(S=\operatorname{Spec}(A)\), \(\omega_E\) is identified with an invertible \(A\)-module.\par

(b) Let \(R\) be a commutative ring in which \(N\) is invertible. A modular form of weight \(k\) on \(\Gamma_1(N)\), meromorphic at infinity and defined over \(R\), is a rule that assigns, to every elliptic curve \(E\) over an \(R\)-algebra \(A\), equipped with an embedding \(\alpha:\mu_N\longrightarrow E\), an element \(f(E,\alpha)\) of \(\omega_E^{\otimes k}\). This rule is required to be compatible with isomorphisms and with extension of scalars.\par

(c) We say that \(f\) is holomorphic at infinity if it extends to a rule \(\widetilde f\) defined for pairs \((E,\alpha)\) in which \(E\) is a generalized elliptic curve ([7], II. 1.12) and \(\alpha\) is an embedding of \(\mu_N\) into \(E\) whose image meets every irreducible component of every geometric fiber ([7], IV. 4.14). If it exists, the rule \(\widetilde f\) is unique.\par

Suppose that \(R\) is a field. We say that \(f\) is a cusp form if it is holomorphic at infinity and if \(\widetilde f(E,\alpha)=0\) whenever \(E\) is a degenerate (i.e., nonsmooth) elliptic curve over an algebraically closed extension of \(R\).\par

These notions could also be defined in terms of Laurent expansions in \(q\) (cf. [7], VII, § 3).\par

2.2. Let \(f\) be as above. If \(d\in(\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z})^*\), the modular form \(f\mid R_d\) is defined by\par

\begin{equation*}
\tag{2.2.1}
(f\mid R_d)(E,\alpha)=f(E,d\alpha).
\end{equation*}

If \(\varepsilon\) is a homomorphism from \((\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z})^*\) to \(R^*\), we say that \(f\) is of type \((k,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\) if \(f\mid R_d=\varepsilon(d)f\) for every \(d\in(\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z})^*\).\par

2.3. Let \(p\) be a prime number not dividing \(N\). The Hecke operator \(T_p\) is then defined on the spaces of modular forms. If \(f\) is such a form and \((E,\alpha)\) is defined over an algebraically closed field of characteristic \(\neq p\), then\par

\begin{equation*}
(f\mid T_p)(E,\alpha)=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\varphi}\varphi^*\bigl(f(\varphi E,\varphi\circ\alpha)\bigr),
\end{equation*}

where \(\varphi\) runs through the classes of isogenies of degree \(p\) with source \(E\) (two isogenies being in the same class if their kernels are equal).\par

The operators \(T_p\) commute with one another and with the \(R_d\).\par

2.4. Take \(R=\mathbf{C}\). The datum of \(\alpha:\mu_N\longrightarrow E\) is then equivalent to that of the point\par

\begin{equation*}
\alpha\bigl(\exp(2\pi i/N)\bigr),
\end{equation*}

which has order \(N\). To a modular form \(f\) as above, we associate a function (also denoted \(f\)) on the upper half-plane \(H\) by the rule\par

\begin{equation*}
\tag{2.4.1}
f(z)=f(E_z,1/N)/(2\pi i\,du)^{\otimes k},
\end{equation*}

where \(E_z\) denotes the elliptic curve \(\mathbf{C}/(\mathbf{Z}\oplus z\mathbf{Z})\).\par

Put \(f(z)=f_\infty(e^{2\pi iz})\). Formula (2.4.1) can be rewritten as\par

\begin{equation*}
\tag{2.4.2}
f_\infty(q)=f(\mathbf{C}^*/q^{\mathbf{Z}},\operatorname{Id})/(dt/t)^{\otimes k}
\qquad(0<|q|<1),
\end{equation*}

where \(\operatorname{Id}\) is induced by the inclusion of \(\mu_N\) in \(\mathbf{C}^*\).\par

This construction identifies the spaces of modular forms in the sense of 2.1 and 2.2 with the spaces having the same names in § 1; the same holds for the operators \(T_p\) and \(R_d\).\par

2.5. For the definition of the Tate curve \(\mathbf{G}_m/q^{\mathbf{Z}}\) over the ring \(\mathbf{Z}((q))=\mathbf{Z}[[q]](q^{-1})\), we refer to [7], VII, § 1. This curve is equipped with an invariant differential \(dt/t\) and a natural embedding \(\operatorname{Id}:\mu_N\longrightarrow\mathbf{G}_m/q^{\mathbf{Z}}\). If \(f\) is a modular form of weight \(k\) on \(\Gamma_1(N)\), meromorphic at infinity and defined over a ring \(R\), set\par

\begin{equation*}
f_\infty(q)=f(\mathbf{G}_m/q^{\mathbf{Z}},\operatorname{Id})/(dt/t)^{\otimes k}
\in\mathbf{Z}((q))\otimes R\subset R((q)).
\end{equation*}

(In this formula, \(\mathbf{G}_m/q^{\mathbf{Z}}\) denotes the curve over \(\mathbf{Z}((q))\otimes R\) obtained from the Tate curve by extension of scalars.)\par

Put\par

\begin{equation*}
f_\infty(q)=\sum a_nq^n
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
(f\mid R_d)_\infty(q)=\sum a_n(d)q^n,
\quad d\in(\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z})^*;
\end{equation*}

if \(p\) is a prime number not dividing \(N\), then\par

\begin{equation*}
\tag{2.5.1}
(f\mid T_p)_\infty(q)=\sum a_{pn}q^n+p^{k-1}\sum a_n(p)q^{np}.
\end{equation*}

In particular, if \(f\) is of type \((k,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\), then\par

\begin{equation*}
\tag{2.5.2}
(f\mid T_p)_\infty(q)=\sum a_{pn}q^n+\varepsilon(p)p^{k-1}\sum a_nq^{pn}.
\end{equation*}

When \(R=\mathbf{C}\), \(f_\infty(q)\) is the series expansion in (2.4.2); this is proved in [7], VII, § 4 (at least for holomorphic \(f\), the only case that concerns us). Formula (2.5.2) recovers (1.6.1).\par

2.6. If \(K\) is a field of characteristic 0, let \(S_K\) denote the vector space of cusp forms of weight \(k\) on \(\Gamma_1(N)\) that are defined over \(K\). We have\par

\begin{equation*}
\tag{2.6.1}
S_K=K\otimes_{\mathbf{Q}}S_{\mathbf{Q}};
\end{equation*}

this follows by interpreting \(S_K\) as the space of sections of an invertible sheaf on the “algebraic stack” corresponding to \(\Gamma_1(N)\) (cf. [7], VII, 3.2).\par

If \(K'\) is a subfield of \(K\), a form \(f\in S_K\) belongs to \(S_{K'}\) if and only if the coefficients of the series \(f_\infty(q)\) belong to \(K'\). This follows by reducing to the case in which \(K\) is algebraically closed and observing that, for every \(K'\)-automorphism \(\sigma\) of \(K\), the forms \(f\) and \(\sigma(f)\) have the same series expansion and therefore coincide.\par

\noindent\textsc{PROPOSITION 2.7.} \textemdash\ Let \(L\) be the set of \(f\in S_{\mathbf{C}}\) such that \((f\mid R_d)_\infty(q)\in\mathbf{Z}[[q]]\) for every \(d\in(\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z})^*\). Then:\par

(2.7.1) \(L\) is a free \(\mathbf{Z}\)-module of finite type, stable under the operators \(T_p\) and \(R_d\).\par

(2.7.2) For every field \(K\) of characteristic 0, one has \(S_K=K\otimes L\).\par

(2.7.3) The eigenvalues of the \(T_p\) on \(S_{\mathbf{C}}\) are algebraic integers in a finite extension of \(\mathbf{Q}\).\par

(2.7.4) If \(f\in S_{\mathbf{C}}=\mathbf{C}\otimes L\) satisfies \(f\mid T_p=a_pf\), then, for every automorphism \(\sigma\) of \(\mathbf{C}\), the form \(\sigma(f)\) satisfies \(\sigma(f)\mid T_p=\sigma(a_p)\sigma(f)\). If \(f\) is of type \((k,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\), then \(\sigma(f)\) is of type \((k,\sigma(\varepsilon))\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\).\par

If \(f\in S_{\mathbf{Q}}\), then \(f\mid R_d\in S_{\mathbf{Q}}\) for every \(d\in(\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z})^*\), and the series \((f\mid R_d)_\infty(q)\) belong to \(\mathbf{Z}[[q]]\otimes\mathbf{Q}\) and hence have bounded denominators. It follows that a nonzero multiple of \(f\) belongs to \(L\), whence \(\mathbf{Q}\otimes L=S_{\mathbf{Q}}\) and, by (2.6.1), \(K\otimes L=S_K\) for every field \(K\) of characteristic 0. The fact that \(L\) is of finite type follows because the linear functionals “\(n\)th coefficients of \((f\mid R_d)_\infty(q)\)” separate the elements of \(S_{\mathbf{Q}}\).\par

The stability of \(L\) under the \(R_d\) (respectively the \(T_p\)) is evident (respectively follows from (2.5.1)). Assertions (2.7.3) and (2.7.4) follow.\par

\noindent\textsc{REMARK 2.8.} \textemdash\ The fact that the series \(f_\infty(q)\), for \(f\in S_{\mathbf{Q}}\), has bounded denominators was deduced here from the fact that the Tate curve is defined over \(\mathbf{Z}((q))\otimes\mathbf{Q}\). One could also have used Shimura's Theorem 3.5.2 [24], valid when \(k\geq 2\), and reduced weight 1 to weight 13 by multiplication by \(\Delta\).\par

\DSectionNamed{§ 3. Review of Galois representations}{Section 3: Review of Galois representations}{section-3}

3.1. Let \(\overline{\mathbf{Q}}\) be an algebraic closure of \(\mathbf{Q}\), and let \(G=\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{\mathbf{Q}}/\mathbf{Q})\). We shall have to consider linear representations of \(G\), that is, continuous homomorphisms\par

\begin{equation*}
\rho:G\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_n(k),
\end{equation*}

\newpage

% AUTHORITY_UNIT physical=8 article=7 printed=513

\DPageUnit{8}{513}{7}

where \(k\) is of one of the following types:\par

(a) the field \(\mathbf{C}\) (with the discrete topology);\par

(b) a finite field (with the discrete topology);\par

(c) a finite extension of an \(\ell\)-adic field \(\mathbf{Q}_\ell\) (with its natural topology).\par

In the first two cases, the image of \(\rho\) is finite.\par

If \(p\) is a prime number, we say that \(\rho\) is unramified at \(p\) if it is trivial on the inertia group of a place of \(\overline{\mathbf{Q}}\) extending \(p\). We then denote by \(F_{p,\rho}\) the image under \(\rho\) of the Frobenius substitution \footnotemark[1] at \(p\); it is an element of \(\operatorname{GL}_n(k)\) defined up to conjugacy. Set\par

\begin{equation*}
\tag{3.1.1}
P_{p,\rho}(T)=\det(1-F_{p,\rho}T)
=1-\operatorname{Tr}(F_{p,\rho})T+\cdots+(-1)^n\det(F_{p,\rho})T^n.
\end{equation*}

Knowledge of the polynomials \(P_{p,\rho}(T)\) almost determines \(\rho\). More precisely:\par

\noindent\textsc{LEMMA 3.2.} \textemdash\ Let \(X\) be a set of prime numbers of density 1, and let \(\rho\) and \(\rho'\) be two semisimple linear representations of \(G\). Suppose that, for every \(p\in X\), \(\rho\) and \(\rho'\) are unramified and that \(P_{p,\rho}(T)=P_{p,\rho'}(T)\) (respectively that \(\operatorname{Tr}(F_{p,\rho})=\operatorname{Tr}(F_{p,\rho'})\) when \(k\) has characteristic 0). Then \(\rho\) and \(\rho'\) are isomorphic.\par

This follows from the Chebotarev density theorem, combined with the fact that a semisimple linear representation of a group is determined up to isomorphism by the corresponding characteristic polynomials (respectively by the traces if the field has characteristic 0) ([3], § 30.16).\par

REMARKS\par

3.3. In what follows, we shall apply Lemma 3.2 to the particular case in which \(X\) is the set of prime numbers not dividing a given integer \(N\); we shall then say that \(\rho\) and \(\rho'\) are unramified outside \(N\).\par

3.4. When \(k=\mathbf{C}\), the semisimplicity condition is automatically satisfied, since \(\rho(G)\) and \(\rho'(G)\) are finite.\par

\DSectionNamed{§ 4. Results}{Section 4: Results}{section-4}

(a) STATEMENT OF THE MAIN THEOREM\par

\noindent\textsc{THEOREM 4.1.} \textemdash\ Let \(N\) be an integer \(\geq 1\), let \(\varepsilon\) be a Dirichlet character modulo \(N\) such that \(\varepsilon(-1)=-1\), and let \(f\) be a nonzero modular form of type \((1,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\). Suppose that \(f\) is an eigenform for the \(T_p\), \(p\nmid N\), with eigenvalues \(a_p\).\par

\footnotetext[1]{We adopt here Artin's conventions [1]. Our “Frobenius substitution” is therefore the element denoted \(\varphi\) in [5]; its inverse is the “geometric Frobenius.”}

\newpage

% AUTHORITY_UNIT physical=9 article=8 printed=514

\DPageUnit{9}{514}{8}

There then exists a linear representation\par

\begin{equation*}
\rho:G\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbf{C}),
\qquad\text{where}\qquad
G=\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{\mathbf{Q}}/\mathbf{Q}),
\end{equation*}

which is unramified outside \(N\) and such that\par

\begin{equation*}
\tag{4.1.1}
\operatorname{Tr}(F_{p,\rho})=a_p
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\det(F_{p,\rho})=\varepsilon(p)
\quad\text{for every }p\nmid N.
\end{equation*}

This representation is irreducible if and only if \(f\) is a cusp form.\par

The proof will be given in § 8.\par

\noindent\textsc{COROLLARY 4.2.} \textemdash\ The eigenvalues \(a_p\) are sums of two roots of unity; in particular, \(|a_p|\leq 2\).\par

In other words, the “Ramanujan-Petersson conjecture” is true in weight 1; it is also known to be true in weights \(\geq 2\), cf. [5], 8.2.\par

REMARKS\par

4.3. By Lemma 3.2, the representation \(\rho\) associated with \(f\) by 4.1 is unique up to isomorphism.\par

4.4. The formula \(\det(F_{p,\rho})=\varepsilon(p)\) shows that\par

\begin{equation*}
\det(\rho)=\varepsilon,
\end{equation*}

where \(\varepsilon\) is identified with the character \(G\longrightarrow\mathbf{C}^*\) corresponding to it under class field theory (it is simply the composite of \(\varepsilon\) with the homomorphism \(G\longrightarrow(\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z})^*\) afforded by the action of \(G\) on the \(N\)th roots of unity).\par

4.5. Let \(c\) denote the element of \(G\) corresponding to complex conjugation (for an embedding of \(\overline{\mathbf{Q}}\) into \(\mathbf{C}\)). Since \(\varepsilon\) is odd, 4.4 shows that \(\det(\rho(c))=-1\); since \(c\) has order 2, this means that \(\rho(c)\) is conjugate to the matrix\par

\begin{equation*}
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation*}

(b) ARTIN CONDUCTOR AND LOCAL FACTORS. — We retain the hypotheses and notation of Theorem 4.1.\par

\noindent\textsc{THEOREM 4.6.} \textemdash\ Suppose that \(f\) is a primitive cusp form, with coefficients \(a_n\), \(n\geq 1\). Let \(\rho\) be the corresponding representation of \(G\). Then:\par

a. The Artin conductor of \(\rho\) is equal to \(N\);\par

b. The Artin \(L\)-function \(L(s,\rho)\) is equal to\par

\begin{equation*}
\Phi_f(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_nn^{-s}.
\end{equation*}

(For the definition of the \(L\)-series and the conductor of a representation, see [1].)\par

\noindent\textsc{COROLLARY 4.7.} \textemdash\ The representation \(\rho\) is ramified at every prime divisor of \(N\).\par

This follows from (a).\par

\newpage

% AUTHORITY_UNIT physical=10 article=9 printed=515

\DPageUnit{10}{515}{9}

\noindent\textsc{COROLLARY 4.8.} \textemdash\ The function \(L(s,\rho)\) is entire.\par

[In other words, the “Artin conjecture” holds for \(\rho\), cf. (c) below.]\par

Indeed, Hecke theory shows that \(\Phi_f(s)\) is entire.\par

\noindent\textsc{Proof of 4.6.} \textemdash\ It uses the functional equations satisfied by \(\Phi_f(s)\) and \(L(s,\rho)\) (compare [9], pp. 172-177).\par

(i) Put \(\widetilde f=\sum\overline{a_n}q^n\). Since \(f\) is primitive, there exists a constant \(\lambda\neq 0\) such that \(f(-1/Nz)=\lambda z\widetilde f(z)\), cf. [12] and [13]. Mellin transformation then gives\par

\begin{equation*}
\Psi_f(1-s)=\mu\widetilde\Psi_f(s)
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
\mu=i\lambda/N^{1/2},
\end{equation*}

where\par

\begin{equation*}
\Psi_f(s)=N^{s/2}(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)\Phi_f(s)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\widetilde\Psi_f(s)=\Psi_{\widetilde f}(s).
\end{equation*}

(ii) By 4.5, the “factor at infinity” of \(L(s,\rho)\) is \((2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)\). If \(M\) is the conductor of \(\rho\), and if we set\par

\begin{equation*}
\xi(s,\rho)=M^{s/2}(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)L(s,\rho),
\end{equation*}

then\par

\begin{equation*}
\xi(1-s,\rho)=\nu\cdot\xi(s,\overline\rho)
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
\nu\in\mathbf{C}^*.
\end{equation*}

(iii) Put\par

\begin{equation*}
F(s)=(N/M)^{s/2}\Psi_f(s)/\xi(s,\rho)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\widetilde F(s)=(N/M)^{s/2}\widetilde\Psi_f(s)/\xi(s,\overline\rho).
\end{equation*}

The preceding formulas show that\par

\begin{equation*}
F(1-s)=\omega\cdot\widetilde F(s)
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
\omega=\mu/\nu.
\end{equation*}

But if \(p\) is a prime number not dividing \(N\), the \(p\)-factors of \(\Psi_f(s)\) and \(\xi(s,\rho)\) agree by 4.1. Hence\par

\begin{equation*}
F(s)=A^s\prod_{p\mid N}F_p(s),
\end{equation*}

where\par

\begin{equation*}
A=(N/M)^{1/2}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
F_p(s)=\frac{1-a_pp^{-s}}{(1-b_pp^{-s})(1-c_pp^{-s})},
\end{equation*}

where \(1-a_pp^{-s}\) is the \(p\)-factor of \(\Psi_f(s)\) and \((1-b_pp^{-s})(1-c_pp^{-s})\) is that of \(\xi(s,\rho)\) (note that \(b_p\) and \(c_p\) may be zero). It remains only to show that \(A\) and the \(F_p\) are equal to 1. For this we use the following elementary lemma:\par

\noindent\textsc{LEMMA 4.9.} \textemdash\ Let \(G(s)=A^s\prod_pG_p(s)\) and \(H(s)=A^s\prod_pH_p(s)\) be two finite Euler products. Suppose that\par

\begin{equation*}
\tag{4.9.1}
G(1-s)=\omega\cdot H(s)
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
\omega\in\mathbf{C}^*;
\end{equation*}

\newpage

% AUTHORITY_UNIT physical=11 article=10 printed=516

\DPageUnit{11}{516}{10}

(4.9.2) Each of \(G_p\) and \(H_p\) is a finite product of terms of the form \((1-\alpha_p^{(i)}p^{-s})^{\pm1}\), with \(|\alpha_p^{(i)}|<p^{1/2}\).\par

Then \(A=1\) and \(G_p=H_p=1\) for every \(p\).\par

If \(H_p\) is not equal to 1, the function \(H\) has infinitely many zeros (or poles) of the form\par

\begin{equation*}
(\log(\alpha_p^{(i)})+2\pi in)/\log p,
\qquad n\in\mathbf{Z},
\end{equation*}

and it is easy to see that these cannot all be zeros (or poles) of \(G(1-s)\); indeed, the hypothesis \(|\alpha_p^{(i)}|<p^{1/2}\) ensures that none of the \(\alpha_p^{(i)}\) can equal a \(p/\alpha_p^{(j)}\).\par

(iv) It remains to verify that \(a_p\), \(b_p\), \(c_p\), and their conjugates satisfy (4.9.2), i.e., have absolute value \(<p^{1/2}\). This is clear for \(b_p\) and \(c_p\), which are either 0 or roots of unity. For \(a_p\), one may invoke 1.8, which shows that \(|a_p|\leq 1\); alternatively, one may use Rankin's inequality:\par

\begin{equation*}
|a_n|=O(n^{1/2-1/5}),\qquad cf.\ [18];
\end{equation*}

applying it to \(n=p^m\) and observing that \(a_n=(a_p)^m\), one obtains\par

\begin{equation*}
|a_p|\leq p^{1/2-1/5}<p^{1/2}.
\end{equation*}

This completes the proof of 4.6.\par

(c) CHARACTERIZATION OF THE REPRESENTATIONS ATTACHED TO FORMS OF WEIGHT 1. — Retain the notation of (a), and suppose that the form \(f\) under consideration is a cusp form. The corresponding representation\par

\begin{equation*}
\rho:G\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbf{C})
\end{equation*}

then has the following properties:\par

(i) \(\rho\) is irreducible (4.1);\par

(ii) \(\det\rho\) is an odd character (4.4);\par

(iii) For every continuous character \(\chi:G\longrightarrow\mathbf{C}^*\), the Artin \(L\)-function \(L(s,\rho\otimes\chi)\) is entire [this follows from 4.8 applied to the cusp form\par

\begin{equation*}
f_\chi=\sum\chi(n)a_nq^n].
\end{equation*}

Conversely:\par

\noindent\textsc{THEOREM 4.10. (Weil-Langlands).} \textemdash\ Let \(\rho:G\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbf{C})\) be a continuous representation of \(G=\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{\mathbf{Q}}/\mathbf{Q})\) satisfying conditions (i), (ii), and (iii) above. Put\par

\begin{equation*}
L(s,\rho)=\sum a_nn^{-s},
\qquad f=\sum a_nq^n,
\qquad\varepsilon=\det\rho,
\qquad N=\operatorname{conduct.}\rho.
\end{equation*}

Then \(f\) is a primitive cusp form of type \((1,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\), and \(\rho\) is the representation attached to \(f\).\par

\newpage

% AUTHORITY_UNIT physical=12 article=11 printed=517

\DPageUnit{12}{517}{11}

According to Langlands (cf. [27], pp. 152 and 160), the constants in the functional equations of the series \(\sum a_n\chi(n)n^{-s}\) satisfy the identity required to apply the Hecke-Weil characterization of modular forms ([12], [26]). It follows that \(f\) is modular of type \((1,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\); it is clear that \(f\) is an eigenform for the \(T_p\) and the \(U_p\), and that the representation associated with it is isomorphic to \(\rho\). By 4.1, \(f\) is a cusp form. Let \(f'\) be the unique primitive cusp form (on some \(\Gamma_0(N')\), where \(N'\) is a suitable divisor of \(N\)) such that \(f'\mid T_p=a_pf'\) for \(p\nmid N\). By 4.6, the Dirichlet series associated with \(f'\) is \(L(s,\rho)=\sum a_nn^{-s}\). It follows that \(f'=f\), which proves that \(f\) is primitive.\par

4.11. A generalization of Theorem 4.10 to all global fields may be found in [27], p. 163.\par

4.14. Theorem 4.6 even gives a way to verify the Artin conjecture in particular cases. Given a representation \(\rho\) satisfying (i) and (ii), with conductor \(N\) and determinant \(\varepsilon\), one can numerically determine the coefficients \(a_n\) of the series \(L(s,\rho)=\sum a_nn^{-s}\) for \(n\) less than a given integer \(A\), and one can seek to construct a primitive cusp form \(f\) of type \((1,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\) whose expansion begins with \(\sum_{n\leq A}a_nq^n\). If \(A\) is sufficiently large,\par

\begin{equation*}
\text{for example}\qquad
A\geq(N/12)\prod_{p\mid N}(1+p^{-1}),
\end{equation*}

\DSectionNamed{§ 5. Exploitation of a Result of Rankin}{Section 5: Exploitation of a Result of Rankin}{section-5}

\noindent\textsc{PROPOSITION 5.1.} \textemdash\ Let \(f\) be a nonzero cusp form of type \((k,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\). Suppose that \(f\) is an eigenform for the \(T_p\), \(p\nmid N\), with eigenvalues \(a_p\). Then the series \(\sum_{p\nmid N}|a_p|^2p^{-s}\) converges for real \(s>k\), and\par

\begin{equation*}
\tag{5.1.1}
\sum_{p\nmid N}|a_p|^2p^{-s}\leq\log(1/(s-k))+O(1)
\qquad\text{as }s\longrightarrow k.
\end{equation*}

\noindent\textsc{Proof 5.2.} \textemdash\ One immediately reduces to the case in which \(f\) is a primitive form \(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_nq^n\). For every \(p\nmid N\), let \(\varphi_p\in\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbf{C})\) satisfy \(\operatorname{Tr}(\varphi_p)=a_p\) and \(\det(\varphi_p)=\varepsilon(p)p^{k-1}\). The Dirichlet series\par

\begin{equation*}
\Phi_f(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_nn^{-s}
\end{equation*}

may then be written\par

\begin{equation*}
\Phi_f(s)=\prod_{p\mid N}(1-a_pp^{-s})^{-1}
\prod_{p\nmid N}\det(1-\varphi_pp^{-s})^{-1},
\qquad cf.\ (1.7.2).
\end{equation*}

Set\par

\begin{equation*}
L(s)=\prod_{p\nmid N}\det(1-\varphi_p\otimes\overline{\varphi}_p\,p^{-s})^{-1}.
\end{equation*}

This is an Euler product with four factors: if \(\lambda_p\) and \(\mu_p\) denote the eigenvalues of \(\varphi_p\), then\par

\begin{equation*}
L(s)=\prod_{p\nmid N}
\bigl[(1-\lambda_p\overline{\lambda}_pp^{-s})
(1-\lambda_p\overline{\mu}_pp^{-s})
(1-\mu_p\overline{\lambda}_pp^{-s})
(1-\mu_p\overline{\mu}_pp^{-s})\bigr]^{-1}.
\end{equation*}

Using the formula \(\lambda_p\overline{\lambda}_p\mu_p\overline{\mu}_p=|\varepsilon(p)p^{k-1}|^2=p^{2k-2}\), one proves (cf., for example, [10], p. 33, or [12], [15]) that\par

\begin{equation*}
L(s)=H(s)\zeta(2s-2k+2)\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_n|^2n^{-s}\right),
\end{equation*}

where\par

\begin{equation*}
H(s)=\prod_{p\mid N}(1-p^{-2s+2k-2})(1-|a_p|^2p^{-s}).
\end{equation*}

According to [18] (cf. also [12], [13], and [15]), the series \(\sum|a_n|^2n^{-s}\) converges for \(\operatorname{Re}(s)>k\), and its product with \(\zeta(2s-2k+2)\) extends to a meromorphic function throughout the\par

\newpage

% AUTHORITY_UNIT physical=14 article=13 printed=519

\DPageUnit{14}{519}{13}

complex plane, with its only pole at \(s=k\). Since \(|a_p|<p^{k/2}\) when \(p\mid N\) (cf. 1.8), the function \(H(s)\) is holomorphic and nonzero in \(\operatorname{Re}(s)\geq k\). It follows that \(L(s)\) is meromorphic throughout the complex plane and holomorphic for \(\operatorname{Re}(s)\geq k\), with the sole exception of \(s=k\), where it has a simple pole; moreover, \(L(s)\neq0\) for real \(s>k\), because this is true of \(H(s)\), \(\zeta(2s-2k+2)\), and \(\sum|a_n|^2n^{-s}\).\par

Set\par

\begin{equation*}
g_m(s)=\sum_{p\nmid N}|\operatorname{Tr}(\varphi_p^m)|^2p^{-ms}/m
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
g(s)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}g_m(s).
\end{equation*}

The series \(g(s)\) is a Dirichlet series with nonnegative coefficients. For sufficiently large \(s\), a direct calculation shows that it equals \(\log L(s)\). Since \(L(s)\) is holomorphic and nonzero for real \(s>k\), a classical lemma of Landau ([21], p. 112) implies that \(g(s)\) converges for \(\operatorname{Re}(s)>k\). Since \(L(s)\) has a simple pole at \(s=k\), one has\par

\begin{equation*}
g(s)=\log(1/(s-k))+O(1)
\qquad\text{as }s\longrightarrow k.
\end{equation*}

But \(g_1(s)=\sum|a_p|^2p^{-s}\) is plainly \(\leq g(s)\). Hence\par

\begin{equation*}
\sum|a_p|^2p^{-s}\leq\log(1/(s-k))+O(1)
\qquad\text{as }s\longrightarrow k.
\end{equation*}

\noindent\textsc{REMARKS 5.3.} \textemdash\ Proposition 5.1 can be strengthened in various ways. First, once the Petersson conjecture is available, an easy bound shows that the series\par

\begin{equation*}
\sum_{p\nmid N}\sum_{m\geq2}|\operatorname{Tr}(\varphi_p^m)|^2p^{-ms}/m
=g_2(s)+g_3(s)+\cdots
\end{equation*}

converges for \(\operatorname{Re}(s)\geq k\), and this permits the inequality (5.1.1) to be replaced by the equality\par

\begin{equation*}
\tag{5.3.1}
\sum|a_p|^2p^{-s}=\log(1/(s-k))+O(1)
\qquad\text{as }s\longrightarrow k.
\end{equation*}

On the other hand, a Hadamard-de la Vallée Poussin argument shows that \(L(s)\neq0\) for every \(s\) such that \(\operatorname{Re}(s)\geq k\) (including the critical line \(\operatorname{Re}(s)=k\)), and applying the Wiener-Ikehara theorem to \(L'(s)/L(s)\) gives\par

\begin{equation*}
\tag{5.3.2}
\sum_{p\leq x}|a_p|^2p^{-(k-1)}\sim x
\qquad\text{as }x\longrightarrow\infty,
\end{equation*}

cf. Rankin [19].\par

5.4. APPLICATION TO FORMS OF WEIGHT 1. — Let \(P\) be the set of prime numbers and let \(X\) be a subset of \(P\). Set\par

\begin{equation*}
\tag{5.4.1}
\operatorname{dens.sup}X=
\limsup_{\substack{s\to1\\s>1}}
\frac{\sum_{p\in X}p^{-s}}{\log(1/(s-1))}.
\end{equation*}

This is the upper density of \(X\); it lies between 0 and 1.\par

\noindent\textsc{PROPOSITION 5.5.} \textemdash\ Retain the hypotheses of 5.1, and suppose in addition that the weight \(k\) of \(f\) is 1. Then, for every \(\eta>0\), there exists a set \(X_\eta\) of prime\par

\newpage

% AUTHORITY_UNIT physical=15 article=14 printed=520

\DPageUnit{15}{520}{14}

numbers and a finite subset \(Y_\eta\) of \(\mathbf{C}\) such that\par

\begin{equation*}
\operatorname{dens.sup}X_\eta\leq\eta
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
a_p\in Y_\eta\quad\text{for every }p\notin X_\eta.
\end{equation*}

By 2.7, the \(a_p\) are algebraic integers in a finite extension \(K\) of \(\mathbf{Q}\). If \(c\) is a constant \(\geq0\), let \(Y(c)\) be the set of integers \(a\) of \(K\) such that \(|\sigma(a)|^2\leq c\) for every embedding \(\sigma\) of \(K\) into \(\mathbf{C}\); this is a finite set. Let \(X(c)\) be the set of \(p\) such that \(a_p\notin Y(c)\); it is enough to prove that \(\operatorname{dens.sup}X(c)\leq\eta\) when \(c\) is sufficiently large.\par

We know from 2.7 that the \(\sigma(a_p)\) are likewise eigenvalues of the \(T_p\) in weight 1. By (5.1.1), therefore,\par

\begin{equation*}
\sum_\sigma\sum_p|\sigma(a_p)|^2p^{-s}
\leq r\log(1/(s-1))+O(1)
\qquad\text{as }s\longrightarrow1,
\end{equation*}

where \(r=[K:\mathbf{Q}]\). Since \(\sum_\sigma|\sigma(a_p)|^2\geq c\) if \(p\in X(c)\), it follows that\par

\begin{equation*}
c\sum_{p\in X(c)}p^{-s}
\leq r\log(1/(s-1))+O(1)
\qquad\text{as }s\longrightarrow1,
\end{equation*}

and hence\par

\begin{equation*}
\operatorname{dens.sup}X(c)\leq r/c.
\end{equation*}

It is therefore enough to take \(c\geq r/\eta\).\par

\noindent\textsc{REMARK 5.6.} \textemdash\ By using (5.3.2) instead of (5.1.1) in the preceding proof, one could have replaced the “analytic” density (5.4.1) by the “natural” density (cf. [21], VI, no. 4.5). In any event, 5.5 is only of provisional interest: once Theorem 4.1 has been proved, we shall know that the set of the \(a_p\) is finite.\par

\noindent\textsc{THEOREM 6.1.} \textemdash\ Let \(f\) be a nonzero modular form of type \((k,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\). \qquad\text{where}\qquad

\end{equation*}

\tag{6.1.1}
\operatorname{Tr}(F_{\rho_\lambda,p})=a_p
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\det(F_{\rho_\lambda,p})=\varepsilon(p)p^{k-1}
\quad\text{if }p\nmid N\ell.
\end{equation*}

By 3.2, condition (6.1.1) determines \(\rho_\lambda\) uniquely up to isomorphism.\par

6.4. The image of \(G\) under \(\rho_\lambda\) is a compact subgroup of \(\operatorname{GL}_2(K_\lambda)\), hence an \(\ell\)-adic Lie group; it is not a finite group.\par

(b) REDUCTION mod \(\ell\)\par

6.6. Let \(K\subset\mathbf{C}\) be a number field, let \(\lambda\) be a finite place of \(K\), let \(\mathcal{O}_\lambda\) be the corresponding valuation ring, \(\mathfrak{m}_\lambda\) its maximal ideal, \(k_\lambda=\mathcal{O}_\lambda/\mathfrak{m}_\lambda\) its residue field, and \(\ell\) the characteristic of \(k_\lambda\). In what follows, we shall write “mod \(\lambda\)” for “mod \(\mathfrak{m}_\lambda\).”\par

Let \(f\) be a modular form of type \((k,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\). We say that \(f\) is \(\lambda\)-integral (respectively, that \(f\equiv0\pmod\lambda\)) if the coefficients of the series \(f_\infty(q)\) belong to \(\mathcal{O}_\lambda\) (respectively, to \(\mathfrak{m}_\lambda\)). Suppose that \(f\) is \(\lambda\)-integral; we say that \(f\) is an eigenvector of \(T_p\) mod \(\lambda\), with eigenvalue \(a_p\in k_\lambda\), if\par

\begin{equation*}
\tag{6.6.1}
f\mid T_p-a_pf\equiv0\pmod\lambda.
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
\rho:G\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_2(k_f)
\end{equation*}

Proof of Theorem 6.7\par

6.8. Let \((K',\lambda',f',k',\varepsilon',(a'_p))\) be as in Theorem 6.7, where \(K'\) contains \(K\) and \(\lambda'\) extends \(\lambda\). If \(a_p\equiv a'_p\pmod{\lambda'}\) and \(\varepsilon(p)p^{k-1}\equiv\varepsilon'(p)p^{k'-1}\pmod{\lambda'}\) for every \(p\nmid N\ell\), the theorem for \(f\) is equivalent to the theorem for \(f'\). The second condition holds whenever \(\varepsilon=\varepsilon'\) and \(k\equiv k'\pmod{\ell-1}\), and it implies the first provided that \(f\equiv f'\pmod{\lambda'}\).\par

6.9. REDUCTION TO THE CASE \(k\geq2\). — For even \(n>2\), let \(E_n\) be the Eisenstein series of weight \(n\) on \(\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbf{Z})\), normalized so that its constant term is 1. If \(n\) is chosen divisible by \(\ell-1\), the series expansion of \(E_n\) is \(\ell\)-integral, and \(E_n\equiv1\pmod\ell\), cf. [25]. The product \(f\cdot E_n\) is therefore congruent to \(f\) mod \(\lambda\); its weight \(k+n\) is congruent to \(k\) mod \((\ell-1)\). By 6.8, the theorem for \(f\) is equivalent to the theorem for \(f\cdot E_n\), which has weight \(>2\).\par

6.10. REDUCTION TO THE CASE IN WHICH \(f\) IS AN EIGENVECTOR OF THE \(T_p\). — It is enough to verify that there exists an \(f'\) as in 6.8, with \((k',\varepsilon')=(k,\varepsilon)\), which is an eigenvector of the \(T_p\). This follows from the next lemma, applied to the \(T_p\) acting on the \(\mathcal{O}_\lambda\)-module \(M\) of modular forms of type \((k,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\) with coefficients in \(\mathcal{O}_\lambda\):\par

\noindent\textsc{LEMMA 6.11.} \textemdash\ Let \(M\) be a free module of finite type over a discrete valuation ring \(\mathcal{O}\); let \(\mathfrak m\) denote the maximal ideal of \(\mathcal{O}\), \(k\) its residue field, and \(K\) its field of fractions. Let \(\mathcal T\) be a set of pairwise commuting endomorphisms of \(M\). Let \(f\in M/\mathfrak mM\) be a common nonzero eigenvector of the \(T\in\mathcal T\), and let \(a_T\in k\) be the corresponding eigenvalues. There then exists a discrete valuation ring \(\mathcal{O}'\) containing \(\mathcal{O}\), with maximal ideal \(\mathfrak m'\) such that \(\mathcal{O}\cap\mathfrak m'=\mathfrak m\), whose field of fractions \(K'\) is finite over \(K\), and a nonzero element \(f'\) of\par

\begin{equation*}
M'=\mathcal{O}'\otimes_{\mathcal{O}}M,
\end{equation*}

which is an eigenvector of the \(T\in\mathcal T\), with eigenvalues \(a'_T\) such that \(a'_T\equiv a_T\pmod{\mathfrak m'}\).\par

(Note that the assertion is not that the eigenvectors lift, but only that the eigenvalues do.)\par

Let \(\mathcal H\) be the subalgebra of \(\operatorname{End}(M)\) generated by \(\mathcal T\). After making a finite extension of the scalars if necessary, we may suppose that \(K\otimes\mathcal H\) is a product of Artinian rings with residue field \(K\). Let \(\chi:\mathcal H\longrightarrow k\) be the homomorphism such that \(h\cdot f=\chi(h)f\) for every \(h\in\mathcal H\). Since \(\mathcal H\) is free over \(\mathcal O\), there exists a prime ideal \(\mathfrak p\) of \(\mathcal H\) contained in the maximal ideal \(\ker(\chi)\) and such that \(\mathfrak p\cap\mathcal O=0\); it is the kernel of a homomorphism \(\chi':\mathcal H\longrightarrow\mathcal O\) whose reduction mod \(\mathfrak m\) is \(\chi\). The ideal of \(K\otimes\mathcal H\) generated by \(\mathfrak p\) belongs to the support of the module \(K\otimes M\); it follows that there exists a nonzero element \(f''\) of \(K\otimes M\) annihilated by this ideal, i.e. such that \(hf''=\chi'(h)f''\) for every \(h\in\mathcal H\). We then take for \(f'\) a nonzero multiple of \(f''\) belonging to \(M\).\par

Variant. — Reduce to the case in which \(M\) is \(\mathcal T\)-indecomposable and the eigenvalues of the \(T\in\mathcal T\) belong to \(K\). Show that there then exists a basis \((e_1,\ldots,e_n)\) of \(M\) relative to which the elements \(T\) of \(\mathcal T\) have upper triangular matrices \((T_{ij})\); use the indecomposability of \(M\) to prove that \(T_{ii}\equiv a_T\pmod{\mathfrak m}\) for every \(T\) and every \(i\). The element \(f'=e_1\) then answers the question.\par

6.12. End of the proof of 6.7. — By 6.9 and 6.10, we may suppose that \(k\geq2\) and that \(f\) is an eigenvector of the \(T_p\), \(p\nmid N\ell\); since \(T_\ell\) commutes with the \(T_p\), we may also suppose that \(f\) is an eigenvector of \(T_\ell\) if \(\ell\nmid N\). Let\par

\rho_\lambda:G\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_2(K_\lambda)
\end{equation*}

be the representation associated with \(f\) by Theorem 6.1. \begin{equation*}

\end{equation*}

Let \(\varphi\) be the semisimplification of \(\widetilde\rho_\lambda\); it is a semisimple representation, unramified outside \(N\ell\), and satisfies (6.7.1). The group \(\varphi(G)\) is finite; by the Čebotarev density theorem, every element of \(\varphi(G)\) is of the form \(F_{\varphi,p}\), with \(p\nmid N\ell\). From the definition of \(k_f\), therefore:\par

(6.12.1) For every \(s\in\varphi(G)\), the coefficients of the polynomial \(\det(1-sT)\) belong to \(k_f\).\par

The existence of the desired representation \(\rho:G\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_2(k_f)\) now follows from the next lemma:\par

\noindent\textsc{LEMMA 6.13.} \textemdash\ Let \(\varphi:\Phi\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_n(k')\) be a semisimple representation of a group \(\Phi\) over a finite field \(k'\). Let \(k\) be a subfield of \(k'\) containing the coefficients of the polynomials \(\det(1-\varphi(s)T)\), \(s\in\Phi\). Then \(\varphi\) is realizable over \(k\), i.e. it is isomorphic to a representation \(\rho:\Phi\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_n(k)\).\par

For \(\varphi\) to be realizable over \(k\), it is enough to verify that \(\varphi\) is isomorphic to \(\sigma(\varphi)\) for every \(k\)-automorphism \(\sigma\) of \(k'\): this follows from the fact that the Brauer group of a finite field is trivial, so there is no “Schur index” to consider. But \(\varphi\) and \(\sigma(\varphi)\) have the same characteristic polynomials and are semisimple; they are therefore isomorphic by [3], th. 30.16.\par

\DSectionNamed{§ 7. Bounding the Orders of Certain Subgroups of \(\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbf{F}_\ell)\)}{Section 7: Bounding the Orders of Certain Subgroups of }{section-7}

If \(\ell\) is a prime number, let \(\mathbf{F}_\ell\) denote the field \(\mathbf{Z}/\ell\mathbf{Z}\) with \(\ell\) elements.\par

7.1. Let \(\eta\) and \(M\) be two positive numbers. We shall consider the following property of a subgroup \(G\) of \(\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbf{F}_\ell)\):\par

\(C(\eta,M)\). — There exists a subset \(H\) of \(G\) such that \(|H|\geq(1-\eta)|G|\), and the set of polynomials \(\det(1-hT)\), \(h\in H\), has at most \(M\) elements.\par

(If \(X\) is a finite set, \(|X|\) denotes its cardinality.)\par

We shall say that \(G\) is semisimple if the identity representation\par

\begin{equation*}
G\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbf{F}_\ell)
\end{equation*}

is semisimple.\par

\noindent\textsc{PROPOSITION 7.2.} \textemdash\ Let \(\eta<1/2\) and \(M\geq0\). There exists a constant \(A=A(\eta,M)\) such that, for every prime number \(\ell\) and every semisimple subgroup \(G\) of \(\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbf{F}_\ell)\) satisfying \(C(\eta,M)\), one has \(|G|\leq A\).\par

\noindent\textsc{Proof.} \textemdash\ Let \(G\) be a semisimple subgroup of \(\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbf{F}_\ell)\). Recall (cf. [22], § 2, props. 15 and 16) that one of the following conditions holds:\par

(a) \(G\) contains \(\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbf{F}_\ell)\);\par

(b) \(G\) is contained in a Cartan subgroup \(T\);\par

(c) \(G\) is contained in the normalizer of a Cartan subgroup \(T\), but is not contained in \(T\);\par

(d) the image of \(G\) in \(\operatorname{PGL}_2(\mathbf{F}_\ell)=\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbf{F}_\ell)/\mathbf{F}_\ell^*\) is isomorphic to \(\mathfrak{A}_4\), \(\mathfrak{S}_4\), or \(\mathfrak{A}_5\).\par

We shall bound the order of \(G\) in each case.\par

Case (a). — Put \(r=(G:\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbf{F}_\ell))\). Then \(|G|=r\ell(\ell^2-1)\). On the other hand, the number of elements of \(\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbf{F}_\ell)\) with a given characteristic polynomial is \(\ell^2+\ell\), \(\ell^2\), or \(\ell^2-\ell\) according as the polynomial in question has 2, 1, or 0 roots in \(\mathbf{F}_\ell\). If \(G\) satisfies \(C(\eta,M)\), then\par

\begin{equation*}
(1-\eta)r\ell(\ell^2-1)=(1-\eta)|G|\leq|H|\leq M(\ell^2+\ell),
\end{equation*}

and hence\par

\begin{equation*}
(1-\eta)r(\ell-1)\leq M
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\ell\leq1+\frac{M}{(1-\eta)r}\leq1+\frac{M}{1-\eta};
\end{equation*}

this gives a bound for \(\ell\), and therefore a fortiori a bound for \(|G|\).\par

Case (b). — At most 2 elements of \(T\) have a given characteristic polynomial. The hypothesis \(C(\eta,M)\) (with \(\eta<1\)) therefore implies\par

\begin{equation*}
(1-\eta)|G|\leq2M,
\end{equation*}

and hence the bound\par

\begin{equation*}
|G|\leq\frac{2M}{1-\eta}.
\end{equation*}

Case (c). — The group \(G'=G\cap T\) has index 2 in \(G\). If \(G\) satisfies \(C(\eta,M)\), then \(G'\) satisfies \(C(2\eta,M)\). Applying (b) to \(G'\), one obtains\par

\begin{equation*}
|G|\leq\frac{4M}{1-2\eta}.
\end{equation*}

Case (d). — The image of \(G\) in \(\operatorname{PGL}_2(\mathbf{F}_\ell)\) has order at most 60. The group\par

\begin{equation*}
G\cap\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbf{F}_\ell)
\end{equation*}

therefore has order at most 120, and \(G\) has at most 120 elements with a given determinant, and a fortiori with a given characteristic polynomial. If \(G\) satisfies \(C(\eta,M)\), then\par

\begin{equation*}
(1-\eta)|G|\leq120M,
\qquad\text{whence}\qquad
|G|\leq\frac{120M}{1-\eta}.
\end{equation*}

We may suppose that the modular form \(f\) under consideration is either an Eisenstein series or a cusp form.\par

8.1. If \(f\) is an Eisenstein series, there exist characters \(\chi_1\) and \(\chi_2\) of \((\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z})^*\) such that \(\chi_1\chi_2=\varepsilon\) and \(a_p=\chi_1(p)+\chi_2(p)\) for \(p\nmid N\) (cf. [9], p. 690). We then take for \(\rho\) the reducible representation\par

\begin{equation*}
\rho=\chi_1\oplus\chi_2,
\end{equation*}

where the \(\chi_i\) are identified with one-dimensional representations of \(G\), cf. 4.4.\par

8.2. From now on, suppose that \(f\) is cuspidal. By 2.7, the \(a_p\) and the \(\varepsilon(p)\) belong to the ring of integers \(\mathcal O_K\) of a number field \(K\), which we may suppose to be Galois over \(\mathbf{Q}\). Let \(L\) be the set of prime numbers \(\ell\) that split completely in \(K\). For every \(\ell\in L\), choose a place \(\lambda_\ell\) of \(K\) above \(\ell\); the corresponding residue field is \(\mathbf{F}_\ell\). By Theorem 6.7, there exists a continuous semisimple representation\par

\begin{equation*}
\rho_\ell:\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{\mathbf{Q}}/\mathbf{Q})\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbf{F}_\ell),
\end{equation*}

which is unramified outside \(N\ell\) and satisfies\par

\begin{equation*}
\det(1-F_{\rho_\ell,p}T)\equiv1-a_pT+\varepsilon(p)T^2\pmod{\lambda_\ell}
\qquad\text{if }p\nmid N\ell.
\end{equation*}

Let \(G_\ell\) be the subgroup of \(\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbf{F}_\ell)\) that is the image of \(\rho_\ell\).\par

\noindent\textsc{LEMMA 8.3.} \textemdash\ For every \(\eta>0\), there exists a constant \(M\) such that \(G_\ell\) satisfies condition \(C(\eta,M)\) of 7.1 for every \(\ell\in L\).\par

By Proposition 5.5, there exists a subset \(X_\eta\) of the set \(P\) of prime numbers such that \(\operatorname{dens.sup}X_\eta\leq\eta\) and the \(a_p\), for \(p\notin X_\eta\), form a finite set. Let \(\mathcal M\) denote the finite set of polynomials \(1-a_pT+\varepsilon(p)T^2\), for \(p\notin X_\eta\), and put \(M=|\mathcal M|\).\par

The group \(G_\ell\) satisfies \(C(\eta,M)\) for every \(\ell\in L\). Indeed, let \(H_\ell\) be the subset of \(G_\ell\) formed by the Frobenius elements \(F_{\rho_\ell,p}\), \(p\notin X_\eta\), and their conjugates. By the Čebotarev density theorem, \(|H_\ell|\geq(1-\eta)|G_\ell|\). On the other hand, if \(h\in H_\ell\), the polynomial \(\det(1-hT)\) is the reduction modulo \(\lambda_\ell\) of an element of \(\mathcal M\), and therefore belongs to a set with at most \(M\) elements. Thus condition \(C(\eta,M)\) is indeed satisfied.\par

\noindent\textsc{LEMMA 8.4.} \textemdash\ There exists a constant \(A\) such that \(|G_\ell|\leq A\) for every \(\ell\in L\).\par

This follows from the preceding lemma and Proposition 7.2.\par

8.5. Choose a constant \(A\) satisfying 8.4. After enlarging the field \(K\) if necessary (which decreases \(L\)), we may suppose that it contains all \(n\)th roots of unity for \(n\leq A\). Let \(Y\) be the set of polynomials \((1-\alpha T)(1-\beta T)\), where \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are roots of unity of order \(\leq A\). If \(p\nmid N\), then for every \(\ell\in L\) with \(\ell\neq p\) there exists \(R(T)\in Y\) such that\par

\begin{equation*}
1-a_pT+\varepsilon(p)T^2\equiv R(T)\pmod{\lambda_\ell}.
\end{equation*}

Since \(Y\) is finite, there is an \(R\) for which the preceding congruence holds for infinitely many \(\ell\), and hence\par

\begin{equation*}
1-a_pT+\varepsilon(p)T^2=R(T),
\end{equation*}

that is, the polynomials \(1-a_pT+\varepsilon(p)T^2\) belong to \(Y\).\par

8.6. Let \(L'\) be the set of \(\ell\in L\) such that \(\ell>A\) and such that, for \(R,S\in Y\), \(R\neq S\) implies \(R\not\equiv S\pmod{\lambda_\ell}\); the set \(L-L'\) is finite, and therefore \(L'\) is infinite. Let \(\ell\in L'\). The order of the group \(G_\ell\) is prime to \(\ell\). It follows, by a standard argument, that the identity representation \(G_\ell\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbf{F}_\ell)\) is the reduction modulo \(\lambda_\ell\) of a representation \(G_\ell\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathcal O_{\lambda_\ell})\), where \(\mathcal O_{\lambda_\ell}\) is the valuation ring for \(\lambda_\ell\). Composing the latter with the canonical map \(G\longrightarrow G_\ell\), we obtain a representation\par

\begin{equation*}
\rho:G\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathcal O_{\lambda_\ell}).
\end{equation*}

By construction, \(\rho\) is unramified outside \(N\ell\). If \(p\nmid N\ell\), the eigenvalues of the Frobenius element \(F_{\rho,p}\) are roots of unity of order \(\leq A\) (since the image of \(\rho\) is isomorphic to \(G_\ell\), and hence has order \(\leq A\)); consequently \(\det(1-F_{\rho,p}T)\in Y\). On the other hand, since the reduction of \(\rho\) modulo \(\lambda_\ell\) is \(\rho_\ell\), one has\par

\begin{equation*}
\det(1-F_{\rho,p}T)\equiv1-a_pT+\varepsilon(p)T^2\pmod{\lambda_\ell}.
\end{equation*}

But the two polynomials \(\det(1-F_{\rho,p}T)\) and \(1-a_pT+\varepsilon(p)T^2\) belong to \(Y\). Since they are congruent modulo \(\lambda_\ell\), they are equal, and\par

\begin{equation*}
\det(1-F_{\rho,p}T)=1-a_pT+\varepsilon(p)T^2
\qquad\text{for every }p\nmid N\ell.
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
\det(1-F_{\rho,p}T)=\det(1-F_{\rho',p}T)
\qquad\text{for }p\nmid N\ell\ell'.
\end{equation*}

By Lemma 3.2, this implies that \(\rho\) and \(\rho'\) are isomorphic as representations in \(\operatorname{GL}_2(K)\), and a fortiori as complex representations. It follows that \(\rho\) is unramified outside \(N\) and that\par

\begin{equation*}
\det(1-F_{\rho,p}T)=1-a_pT+\varepsilon(p)T^2
\qquad\text{for every }p\nmid N.
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}

\qquad\text{for }p\nmid N.
\end{equation*}

Then\par

\begin{equation*}
\sum|a_p|^2p^{-s}

\begin{equation*}
\sum\chi_1(p)\overline{\chi}_2(p)p^{-s}=O(1)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad

\end{equation*}

(a) BOUND FOR \(|a_n|\)\par

\noindent\textsc{THEOREM 9.1.} \textemdash\ One has \(|a_n|=O(d(n))\) as \(n\longrightarrow\infty\).\par

(Recall that \(d(n)\) denotes the number of divisors \(\geq1\) of \(n\).)\par

\noindent\textsc{COROLLARY 9.2.} \textemdash\ One has \(|a_n|=O(n^\delta)\) for every \(\delta>0\).\par

Indeed, \(d(n)\) is known to have this property ([8], th. 315).\par

\noindent\textsc{Proof of 9.1.} \textemdash\ If \(n_0\) is an integer \(\geq1\), then \(d(n_0n)/d(n)\) lies between 1 and \(d(n_0)\). It is therefore equivalent to prove estimate (9.1) for \(f(z)\) or for \(f(n_0z)\), and this allows us to suppose that \(M=1\), i.e. that \(f(z+1)=f(z)\). Using 1.5 and 1.9, we are reduced to the following two special cases:\par

(i) \(f\) is an Eisenstein series, in which case (9.1) follows from the formula for the \(a_n\) ([9], p. 475);\par

(ii) \(f\) is a primitive cusp form of type \((1,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\), for suitable \(N\) and \(\varepsilon\). In this case one has the more precise result\par

\begin{equation*}
\tag{9.3}
|a_n|\leq d_N(n)\leq d(n),
\end{equation*}

where \(d_N(n)\) is the number of positive divisors of \(n\) that are prime to \(N\). Indeed, by the multiplicativity of \(a_n\) and \(d_N(n)\), it is enough to verify (9.3) when \(n\) is a power \(p^m\) of a prime number \(p\). We distinguish two cases:\par

\((\mathrm{ii}_1)\) \(p\mid N\).\par

One has \(a_n=(a_p)^m\), and Theorem 4.6 shows that \(a_p\) is either 0 or a root of unity. Hence\par

\begin{equation*}
|a_n|\leq1=d_N(n).
\end{equation*}

\((\mathrm{ii}_2)\) \(p\nmid N\).\par

If the polynomial \(1-a_pT+\varepsilon(p)T^2\) is written in the form \((1-\lambda T)(1-\mu T)\), then\par

\begin{equation*}
a_n=\lambda^m+\lambda^{m-1}\mu+\cdots+\lambda\mu^{m-1}+\mu^m.
\end{equation*}

By Theorem 4.1, \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) are roots of unity. Thus\par

\begin{equation*}
|a_n|\leq m+1=d_N(n).
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
|b_n|=O\bigl(n^{(k-1)/2}d(n)\bigr)
\qquad\text{as }n\longrightarrow\infty.
\end{equation*}

(b) MAXIMAL ORDER OF MAGNITUDE OF \(|a_n|\). — It is known ([8], th. 317) that the “maximal” order of magnitude of \(d(n)\) is \(2^{\log n/\log\log n}\), in the sense that\par

\begin{equation*}
\limsup\frac{\log d(n)\log\log n}{\log n}=\log2.
\end{equation*}

The same result holds for the \(|a_n|\):\par

\noindent\textsc{PROPOSITION 9.5.} \textemdash\ If \(f\neq0\), then\par

\begin{equation*}
\limsup\frac{\log|a_n|\log\log n}{\log n}=\log2.
\end{equation*}

\noindent\textsc{LEMMA 9.6.} \textemdash\ Let \(N\) be an integer \(\geq1\). There exist sets \(X_N\) and \(Y_N\) of prime numbers, of densities \(>0\), such that:\par

(x) For every \(p\in X_N\), one has \(p\equiv1\pmod N\) and \(g\mid T_p=2g\) for every modular form \(g\) of weight 1 on \(\Gamma_1(N)\);\par

(y) For every \(p\in Y_N\), one has \(p\equiv-1\pmod N\) and \(g\mid T_p=0\) for every modular form \(g\) of weight 1 on \(\Gamma_1(N)\).\par

Let \(\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_h\) be the representations of \(G\) associated with the different systems of eigenvalues of the \(T_p\) acting on forms of type \((1,\varepsilon)\) on \(\Gamma_0(N)\), as \(\varepsilon\) ranges over the odd characters of \((\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z})^*\). Let \(X_N\) be the set of \(p\equiv1\pmod N\) such that\par

\begin{equation*}
F_{\rho_i,p}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation*}

for \(i=1,\ldots,h\), and let \(Y_N\) be the set of \(p\equiv-1\pmod N\) such that \(F_{\rho_i,p}\) is conjugate to \(\rho_i(c)\), cf. 4.5. By the Čebotarev density theorem, \(X_N\) and \(Y_N\) have densities \(>0\). If \(p\in X_N\), then 2 is the only eigenvalue of \(T_p\); since \(T_p\) is semisimple, one indeed has \(g\mid T_p=2g\) for every \(g\). The same argument shows that \(g\mid T_p=0\) if \(p\in Y_N\), since the trace of the matrix \(\rho_i(c)\) is 0.\par

\noindent\textsc{Proof of 9.5.} \textemdash\ As in (a), we reduce to the case in which \(f\) is a modular form of weight 1 on \(\Gamma_1(N)\). Let \(X_N\) be as in Lemma 9.6, and choose an integer \(m\) such that \(a_m\neq0\). If \(x\) is an integer \(\geq1\), let \(p_1,\ldots,p_{i(x)}\) be the distinct prime numbers \(p\in X_N\) that are \(\leq x\) and do not divide \(m\). Put \(n(x)=mp_1p_2\cdots p_{i(x)}\). Since the \(p_i\) belong to \(X_N\), one has \(f\mid T_{p_i}=2f\) and \(f\mid R_{p_i}=f\); by (2.5.1), this gives\par

\begin{equation*}
a_{n(x)}=2^{i(x)}a_m,
\end{equation*}

and hence\par

\begin{equation*}
\log|a_{n(x)}|\sim i(x)\log2
\qquad\text{as }x\longrightarrow\infty.
\end{equation*}

If \(c\) is the density of \(X_N\), then \(i(x)\sim cx/\log x\) and \(\sum_{i\leq i(x)}\log p_i\sim cx\). It follows that\par

\begin{equation*}
\log|a_{n(x)}|\sim cx\log2/\log x,
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
\log n(x)\sim cx,
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
\log\log n(x)\sim\log x,
\end{equation*}

and hence\par

\begin{equation*}
\limsup\frac{\log|a_n|\log\log n}{\log n}\geq\log2.
\end{equation*}

The opposite inequality follows from \(|a_n|=O(d(n))\).\par

(c) NORMAL ORDER OF MAGNITUDE OF \(|a_n|\). — The “normal” (i.e. most frequent) order of magnitude of \(d(n)\) is \(2^{\log\log n}\) (cf. [8], th. 432). That of \(|a_n|\) is smaller:\par

\noindent\textsc{PROPOSITION 9.7.} \textemdash\ The set of \(n\) such that \(a_n=0\) has density 1.\par

(A subset \(S\) of \(\mathbf{N}\) is said to have density \(c\) if the number of elements of \(S\) that are \(\leq x\) is \(cx+o(x)\) as \(x\longrightarrow\infty\).)\par

Here again, we may suppose that \(f\) is a modular form of weight 1 on \(\Gamma_1(N)\). Let \(Y_N\) be as in Lemma 9.6. If \(p\in Y_N\), then \(f\mid T_p=0\) and \(f\mid R_p=-f\). By (2.5.1), it follows that if \(n\) is divisible by \(p\) but not by \(p^2\), then \(a_n=0\). Now, if \(Y\) is a finite set of prime numbers, the set \(S_Y\) of integers \(n\) having the preceding property (for at least one \(p\in Y\)) has density\par

\begin{equation*}
1-\prod_{p\in Y}\left(1-\frac{p-1}{p^2}\right).
\end{equation*}

Since \(Y_N\) has density \(>0\), the series \(\sum_{p\in Y_N}1/p\) diverges, and the product\par

\begin{equation*}
\prod_{p\in Y_N}\left(1-\frac{p-1}{p^2}\right)
\end{equation*}

has value 0. It follows that the union of the \(S_Y\), \(Y\subset Y_N\), has density 1, which proves the proposition.\par

\noindent\textsc{REMARK 9.8.} \textemdash\ For each \(x\), let \(M(x)\) denote the number of \(n\leq x\) such that \(a_n\neq0\). Proposition 9.7 says that\par

\begin{equation*}
M(x)=o(x)
\qquad\text{as }x\longrightarrow\infty.
\end{equation*}

Using Theorem 2 of [23], one can prove the following more precise result: there exists \(\alpha>0\) such that\par

\begin{equation*}
M(x)=O(x/\log^\alpha x)
\qquad\text{as }x\longrightarrow\infty.
\end{equation*}
