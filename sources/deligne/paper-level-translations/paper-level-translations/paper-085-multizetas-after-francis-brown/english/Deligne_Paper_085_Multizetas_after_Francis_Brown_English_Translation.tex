\section*{0. Introduction}

Let $k\geq 0$ and let $s=(s_1,\ldots,s_k)$ be a sequence of $k$ integers $\geq 1$.
The multizeta number $\zeta(s)$ is the iterated sum (the name is explained in 1.1)
\begin{equation}
\zeta(s):=\sum_{n_1>\cdots>n_k>0}\frac{1}{n_1^{s_1}\cdots n_k^{s_k}}
\tag{0.1}
\end{equation}
(the sum being over strictly decreasing sequences of $k$ integers $>0$).
For $k=0$, this definition gives $\zeta(s)=1$.
For $k\geq 1$, the sum (0.1) converges if and only if $s_1\geq 2$.
We shall consider only convergent $\zeta(s)$, i.e. those for which (0.1) converges.

The product of two multizeta numbers is a linear combination, with integer coefficients, of multizeta numbers. See 3.6.
It is therefore equivalent to determine the polynomial relations with rational coefficients among the multizeta numbers, or to determine the $\Q$-linear relations among them.

For $k=1$, the convergent $\zeta(s)$ are the values at the integers $n\geq 2$ of Riemann's zeta function.
Euler showed that for $n$ even, $\zeta(n)$ is a rational multiple of $\pi^n$ ([3]).
Computing $\zeta(3)$ to 10 significant figures, he also checked that $\zeta(3)$ was not the product of $\pi^3$ by a rational number of small denominator.
Following a correspondence with Goldbach, Euler studied in [4], for $k=2$, the variant of the sums (0.1) in which one sums over $n_1\geq n_2$:
\[
 \zeta_{\mathrm{Euler}}(s_1,s_2)=\zeta(s_1,s_2)+\zeta(s_1+s_2).
\]
He proves a series of $\Q$-linear relations among multizeta numbers, among them $\zeta(2,1)=\zeta(3)$.
One of his motivations was the hope of obtaining information on the values of $\zeta$ at the odd integers $\geq 3$ (see the end of his letter to Goldbach of January 5, 1743).

The problem of determining all $\Q$-linear relations among multizeta numbers has two aspects:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\Alph*)]
\item What relations should one expect? Prove them.
\item Prove that there are no others.
\end{enumerate}

The available evidence, both numerical and theoretical, suggests that every $\Q$-linear relation among the $\zeta(s)$ is a sum of isobaric relations, i.e. relations among $\zeta(s)$ of the same weight $\sum s_i$.
If one restricts oneself to considering only isobaric relations, one knows nothing about problem (B).
We shall speak only of (A).

F. Brown has defined a class of $\Q$-linear relations among the $\zeta(s)$, the motivic relations.
More precisely, he defines a graded $\Q$-algebra $M$, homogeneous elements $\zeta^m(s)$ of $M$, of degree the weight $\sum s_i$ of $s$, and a homomorphism $\real:M\to\R$ such that $\real(\zeta^m(s))=\zeta(s)$.
As a vector space, the algebra $M$ is generated by the $\zeta^m(s)$.
The motivic relations among the $\zeta(s)$ are those that come from relations among the $\zeta^m(s)$.

A variant of Grothendieck's period conjecture implies that every $\Q$-linear relation among the $\zeta(s)$ is motivic.
This prediction has been verified for many of the known relations.
Warning: although some proofs of relations among the $\zeta(s)$ are easy to lift to proofs of the same relations among the $\zeta^m(s)$, this is far from always being the case.

\paragraph{Theorem 0.2 (F. Brown [1]).}
The $\zeta^m(s_1,\ldots,s_k)$ for which each $s_i$ is equal to $2$ or $3$ form a basis of $M$ over $\Q$.

Each $\zeta(s)$ can therefore be expressed uniquely, in a motivic way, as a linear combination with rational coefficients of $\zeta(s_1,\ldots,s_k)$ with $k\geq 0$ and each $s_i\in\{2,3\}$.
Unfortunately, Brown's proof does not provide an algorithm, or at least not a usable algorithm, for finding what this linear combination is.
From Theorem 0.2, F. Brown deduces that the category of mixed Tate motives over $\Z$ is generated by the fundamental group of $\PP^1-\{0,1,\infty\}$. See 7.18.

Some of our notations differ from those of [1].
The correspondence between the notations is involutive: $\zeta(s_1,\ldots,s_k)$ becomes $\zeta(s_k,\ldots,s_1)$, the composition of paths $\alpha\beta$ becomes $\beta\alpha$, a monomial in the $e_t$ (respectively a sequence of $0$ and $1$) is to be replaced by the same one read from right to left, and $e_1$ is to be replaced by $-e_1$.

\section*{1. Integral formula}

\subsection*{1.1}
Let $\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_N$ be $1$-forms on the interval $[0,1]$.
The iterated integral $\It\int_0^1\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_N$ is the integral, over the simplex
\begin{equation}
\sigma_N:=\{(t_1,\ldots,t_N)\mid 1\geq t_1\geq\cdots\geq t_N\geq 0\},
\tag{1.1.1}
\end{equation}
contained in the cube $[0,1]^N$, of
\[
 \operatorname{pr}_1^*\omega_1\wedge \operatorname{pr}_2^*\omega_2\wedge\cdots\wedge \operatorname{pr}_N^*\omega_N.
\]
Let $S$ be the operation that attaches to a $1$-form $\omega$ the function
\[
 S[\omega](t):=\int_0^t\omega.
\]
The iterated integral $\It\int_0^1\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_N$ can be constructed as follows: apply $S$ to $\omega_N$, multiply the function obtained by $\omega_{N-1}$, apply $S$, multiply by $\omega_{N-2}$, and so on; at the end, evaluate at $1$:
\begin{equation}
\It\int_0^1\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_N
 = S[\omega_1.S[\omega_2.\cdots S[\omega_N]\cdots]](1).
\tag{1.1.2}
\end{equation}
It is this construction that explains the name ``iterated integral''.
A sum such as (0.1) admits an analogous description in terms of the operator $P$ which, to a function $f(n)$ ($n\geq1$), attaches the function
\[
 P[f](n):=\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}f(m);
\]
this explains the name ``iterated sum'' given to (0.1).

Let us compute in this way the iterated integral
\begin{equation}
\It\int_0^1
 \underbrace{\frac{dt}{t},\ldots,\frac{dt}{t}}_{(s_1-1)\text{ times}},
 \frac{dt}{1-t},
 \underbrace{\frac{dt}{t},\ldots,\frac{dt}{t}}_{(s_2-1)\text{ times}},
 \frac{dt}{1-t},\ldots,
 \underbrace{\frac{dt}{t},\ldots,\frac{dt}{t}}_{(s_k-1)\text{ times}},
 \frac{dt}{1-t}.
\tag{1.1.3}
\end{equation}
We have
\[
 \frac{dt}{1-t}=\sum_{n\geq0}t^n dt.
\]
Applying $S$ term by term, one obtains $\sum_{n\geq1}t^n/n$.
Multiplying by $dt/t$ and applying $S$, one obtains $\sum_{n\geq1}t^n/n^2$.
Iterating $(s_k-1)$ times, one obtains $\sum_{n\geq1}t^n/n^{s_k}$.
Multiplying by $dt/(1-t)=\sum_{m\geq0}t^m dt$ and applying $S$, one obtains
\[
S\left(\sum_{m\geq0,n\geq1}\frac{t^{m+n}}{n^{s_k}}dt\right)
 =\sum_{m,n\geq1}\frac{t^{m+n}}{(m+n)n^{s_k}}
 =\sum_{m>n>0}\frac{t^m}{mn^{s_k}}.
\]
Continuing in this way, one finally obtains:

\paragraph{Proposition 1.2.}
The iterated integral (1.1.3) is equal to $\zeta(s_1,\ldots,s_k)$.

\subsection*{1.3}
Whereas the notion of an infinite sum is foreign to algebraic geometry, the study of integrals of algebraic quantities is one of its sources.
It is thanks to Proposition 1.2 that algebraic geometry, more precisely the theory of mixed Tate motives, is useful in the study of multizeta numbers.

\section*{2. Pro-unipotent fundamental group and cohomology}

\subsection*{2.1}
We shall need a variant $Z'$ of the descending central series $Z$ of a group $\Gamma$.
For each $i\geq1$, $\Gamma/Z_i$ is a nilpotent group.
The set $T_i$ of its torsion elements is therefore a subgroup.
We define $Z'_i$ as the inverse image of $T_i$ in $\Gamma$.
Below, we shall always assume $\Gamma$ to be finitely generated.
This hypothesis implies that the $Z'_i/Z'_{i+1}$ are finitely generated free abelian groups.

First suppose $\Gamma$ nilpotent and torsion-free, i.e. that there is an $A$ such that $Z'_A=0$.
For each $i$ ($1\leq i<A$), choose a basis of $Z'_i/Z'_{i+1}$ and lift it to $Z'_i$.
Let $\gamma_1,\ldots,\gamma_M$ be the elements of $\Gamma$ obtained, arranged in an arbitrary order, and let $w(j)$ be the integer $i$ such that $\gamma_j$ lifts an element of a basis of $Z'_i/Z'_{i+1}$.
One checks by induction on $A$ that the map
\[
 \Z^M\longrightarrow \Gamma: n\longmapsto \gamma_1^{n_1}\cdots\gamma_M^{n_M}
\]
is bijective.
In such a ``system of coordinates'', the group law of $\Gamma$ is given by polynomials with rational coefficients and integer values on the integers.
More precisely, if the coordinate of index $j$ is viewed as having weight $w(j)$, the $j$-th coordinate of $m\circ n$ is a polynomial of weight $\leq w(j)$ in the $m_k$ and $n_l$.

The same polynomials define a unipotent algebraic group over $\Q$, the unipotent envelope $\Gamma^{\mathrm{un}}$ of $\Gamma$.
The group $\Gamma^{\mathrm{un}}(\Q)$ of its rational points is $\Q^N$, endowed with the group law given by the same formulae as that of $\Gamma$.
The group $\Gamma^{\mathrm{un}}(\Q)$ is the Malcev completion $\Gamma\otimes\Q$ of $\Gamma$.

Let $\Q[\Gamma]$ be the group algebra of $\Gamma$, and let $I$ be its augmentation ideal.
Quillen gave an elegant description of the affine algebra $O(\Gamma^{\mathrm{un}})$ of $\Gamma^{\mathrm{un}}$: it is the inductive limit of the duals of the $\Q[\Gamma]/I^N$.
More precisely, the inclusion of $\Gamma$ in $\Q[\Gamma]$ identifies the dual of $\Q[\Gamma]$ with the space of functions $\Gamma\to\Q$, and under this identification the dual of $\Q[\Gamma]/I^{N+1}$ becomes the space of polynomials in the $n_j$ of weight $\leq N$.

For an arbitrary finitely generated $\Gamma$, the preceding applies to the $\Gamma/Z'_i$, and one defines $\Gamma^{\mathrm{un}}$ as the projective limit group scheme of the $(\Gamma/Z'_i)^{\mathrm{un}}$.
The affine algebra of $\Gamma^{\mathrm{un}}$ is still the inductive limit of the duals of the $\Q[\Gamma]/I^N$.

\subsection*{2.2}
Let $E$ be a reasonable topological space, for example a complex algebraic variety.
Assume $E$ connected.
A path $\gamma$ from $a$ to $b$, $[0,1]\to E$, gives
\[
 \gamma_N:[0,1]^N\to E^N
\]
and, by restriction to the simplex $\sigma_N$ of (1.1.1), a singular chain of $E^N$ which is a cycle modulo the union of $b=x_1$, $x_1=x_2$, $\ldots$, $x_N=a$.
The homology class of this cycle depends only on the homotopy class of $\gamma$.

Make $a=b$.
Elaborating the preceding construction (cf. [2] \S3), one obtains an isomorphism of $\Q[\pi_1(E,a)]/I^{N+1}$ with a relative homology group of $E^N$ and, for its dual $(\Q[\pi_1(E,a)]/I^{N+1})^\vee$, with a relative cohomology group of $E^N$.
From this, by passage to the inductive limit, one obtains a cohomological description of the affine algebra of $\pi_1(E,a)^{\mathrm{un}}$.

No longer suppose that $a=b$.
Let $\pi_1(E;b,a)$ be the set of homotopy classes of paths from $a$ to $b$.
The group $\pi_1(E,a)$ acts on the right, by composition of paths, on $\pi_1(E;b,a)$.
This action makes $\pi_1(E;b,a)$ a principal homogeneous space, we prefer to say torsor, under $\pi_1(E,a)$.
We write $\pi_1(E;b,a)^{\mathrm{un}}$ for the $\pi_1(E,a)^{\mathrm{un}}$-torsor deduced from it by pushing forward by $\pi_1(E,a)\to\pi_1(E,a)^{\mathrm{un}}$.
Let $\Q[\pi_1(E;b,a)]$ be the space of formal linear combinations of elements of $\pi_1(E;b,a)$.
It is a right $\Q[\pi_1(E,a)]$-module, free of rank one.
Set
\[
 \Q[\pi_1(E;b,a)]/I^N
 :=\Q[\pi_1(E;b,a)]/\Q[\pi_1(E;b,a)]I^N.
\]
The preceding construction again provides a cohomological description of the $(\Q[\pi_1(E;b,a)]/I^N)^\vee$, and of the affine algebra of $\pi_1(E;b,a)^{\mathrm{un}}$ which is its inductive limit.

\subsection*{2.3}
Let $X$ be an algebraic variety defined over a subfield $K$ of $\C$.
The Betti cohomology $H^*_B(X)$ of $X$ is the rational cohomology of the space $X(\C)$ of complex points of $X$, endowed with its classical topology.
It is endowed with structures reflecting the algebraic structure of $X$, in particular with a mixed Hodge structure.
One also has the algebraic de Rham cohomology $H^*_{\dR}(X)$.
If $X$ is nonsingular, $H^*_{\dR}(X)$ is the hypercohomology of the de Rham complex of sheaves of algebraic differential forms.
It is a $K$-vector space, one has a comparison isomorphism
\begin{equation}
\comp_{B,\dR}:H^i_{\dR}(X)\otimes_K\C\xrightarrow{\sim}H^i_B(X)\otimes_\Q\C,
\tag{2.3.1}
\end{equation}
and the filtrations $W$ and $F$ of the mixed Hodge structure of $H^i_B(X)$ come from filtrations of $H^i_{\dR}(X)$.
The coefficients of the matrix of the isomorphism (2.3.1), in a basis of $H^i_{\dR}(X)$ and one of $H^i_B(X)$, are called periods.

Thanks to 2.2, all this applies to the affine algebras of the group schemes $\pi_1(X,a)^{\mathrm{un}}$ or of their homogeneous spaces $\pi_1(X;b,a)^{\mathrm{un}}$.
We shall write $\pi_1(X,a)_B$ for $\pi_1(X,a)^{\mathrm{un}}$, $\pi_1(X,a)_{\dR}$ for its de Rham variant, and similarly for the $\pi_1(X;b,a)$.
We shall put $B/\dR$ in the subscript when the statement is valid equally well in Betti and in de Rham.

\subsection*{2.4. Example}
Take $K=\Q$ and let $X$ be the multiplicative group $\Gm$, whose group of complex points is $\C^*$.
Here the fundamental group, which is commutative, is independent of the base point and is identified with the homology group $H_1$.
In de Rham, the dual $H^1_{\dR}$ of $H^{\dR}_1$ is $\Q\,dz/z$.
In Betti, $H_1(\C^*,\Z)$ is $\Z$, generated by a positive turn around $0$.
If one does not want to speak of a ``positive turn'', i.e. to choose which of $i$ and $-i$ is $i$, it is better to say that $H_1=\pi_1=2\pi i\Z$, with $\ell\in2\pi i\Z$ corresponding to the loop $\exp(\ell t)$ ($0\leq t\leq1$), on which the integral of $dz/z$ is $\ell$.

The $H^B_1(\Gm)=H_1(\C^*,\Q)$ and $H^{\dR}_1(\Gm)$ are the Betti and de Rham realizations of the Tate motive $\Q(1)$ (see \S5).
We have $\Q(1)_{\dR}=\Q$ ($1$ being dual to $dz/z\in H^1_{\dR}(\Gm)$), and $\Q(1)_B$, included in $\Q(1)_{\dR}\otimes\C$ by $\comp_{\dR,B}$, is $2\pi i\Q$; $\Q(1)$ is purely of Hodge type $(-1,-1)$.

The algebraic group $\pi_1(\Gm,1)_{\dR}$ is the additive group $\Ga$, whose group of rational points is $\Q(1)_{\dR}=\Q$, and whose affine algebra is $\Q[T]$ (more precisely: $\Sym^*(H^1_{\dR}(\Gm))$).
The algebraic group $\pi_1(\Gm,1)_B$ is the additive group whose group of rational points is $2\pi i\Q$.
Affine algebra: $\Sym^*(H^1_B(\Gm))$.
It is the algebra of functions $f$ on $2\pi i\Z$, with rational values, and such that, if one identifies $2\pi i\Z$ with $\Z$ by $n\mapsto2\pi in$, the following equivalent conditions are verified for $N$ large enough: (a) $f$ is a polynomial of degree $<N$; (b) denoting by $\Delta$ the operator $g\mapsto(g(n+1)-g(n))$, one has $\Delta^Nf=0$; (c) the linear form on the group algebra $\Q[\Z]$ defined by $f$ is zero on $I^N$.

By abuse of language, we shall sometimes write $\Q(1)_{B/\dR}$ for these algebraic groups.

\section*{3. The case of $\PP^1-\{0,1,\infty\}$}

\subsection*{3.1}
If $X$ is the complement in $\PP^1$ of a finite set $T$ of rational points, the variety $X$ is defined over $\Q$ and the mixed Hodge structures of 2.2, 2.3 are Hodge-Tate: $\Gr^W_n$ is zero for $n$ odd, and for $n=2m$ is purely of Hodge type $(m,m)$.
For each of the groups $M$ considered in 2.2, let $M_{\dR}$ be its de Rham variant (2.3).
After renumbering, the weight and Hodge filtrations $W$ and $F$ of $M_{\dR}$ are opposite.
They define the grading of $M_{\dR}$ for which
\begin{align}
 W_{-2n}(M_{\dR})&=\bigoplus_{m\geq n}M^m_{\dR},
\tag{3.1.1}\\
 F^{-p}(M_{\dR})&=\bigoplus_{m\leq p}M^m_{\dR}.
\notag
\end{align}
The convention (3.1.1) ensures that $\Q(1)_{\dR}$ is of degree one.
It is sometimes convenient to grade by the opposite of the degree, the codegree, and to set $(M_{\dR})_m:=(M_{\dR})^{-m}$.

Special case: the affine algebra of $\pi_1(X;b,a)_{\dR}$ is graded.
It is in degrees $\leq0$ and is reduced to $\Q$ in degree $0$.
The augmentation morphism $O(\pi_1(X;b,a)_{\dR})\to\Q$ is a point ${}_b1_a$ of $\pi_1(X;b,a)_{\dR}$.
In de Rham, one thus has a canonical path ${}_b1_a$ from one base point to another: for a certain pro-unipotent group scheme $\Pi$, each $\pi_1(X;b,a)_{\dR}$ is identified with a copy ${}_b\Pi_a$ of $\Pi$, and composition of paths is the group law.

The group $\Pi$ has the following description.
Let $L$ be the Lie algebra over $\Q$ generated by elements $e_t$ ($t\in T$) subject only to the relation $\sum e_t=0$.
It is endowed with the grading for which each $e_t$ is homogeneous of degree $1$.
The descending central series $Z$ is the filtration by ``degrees $\geq i$''.
Let $L^\wedge$ be the completion of $L$ for this filtration, and let $\exp(L/Z_i)$ be the unipotent algebraic group with Lie algebra $L/Z_i$.
We have
\begin{equation}
\Pi=\exp(L^\wedge):=\varprojlim \exp(L/Z_i).
\tag{3.1.2}
\end{equation}
Another description: the enveloping algebra $A$ of $L$ is the associative algebra generated by elements $e_t$ ($t\in T$) subject only to the relation $\sum e_t=0$.
It is graded, each $e_t$ being homogeneous of degree one, and the $N$-th power of the augmentation ideal $I$ is the part of $A$ of degree $\geq N$.
The completion $A^\wedge=\varprojlim A/I^N$ of $A$ is endowed with a coproduct
\[
 \Delta:A^\wedge\to A^\wedge\widehat\otimes A^\wedge:
 e_t\longmapsto e_t\otimes1+1\otimes e_t,
\]
$L^\wedge$ is the Lie algebra of primitive elements, and for every $\Q$-algebra $R$, if one sets
\[
 A^\wedge\widehat\otimes R:=\varprojlim_N (A/I^N)\otimes_\Q R,
\]
the group $\Pi(R)$ of $R$-points of $\Pi$ is the group of group-like elements of $(A^\wedge\widehat\otimes R)^*$:
\begin{equation}
\Pi(R)=\{g\in(A^\wedge\widehat\otimes R)^*\mid \Delta g=g\otimes g\}.
\tag{3.1.3}
\end{equation}
The affine algebra $O(\Pi)$ of $\Pi$ (with the discrete topology) and the completed enveloping algebra $A^\wedge$ (with its projective-limit topology) are dual to one another.
The topological dual of $A^\wedge$ is identified with the graded dual $\bigoplus(A_n)^\vee$ of $A$, and this gives the grading (in degrees $\leq0$) of $O(\Pi)$.

We shall write $\omega$ for the logarithmic $1$-form on $X$ with values in $L$:
\begin{equation}
 \omega:=\sum_{t\ne\infty} e_t\frac{dz}{z-t}.
\tag{3.1.4}
\end{equation}
At each $t\in T$, it has residue $e_t$.

Suppose that $\infty\in T$ and put $T':=T-\{\infty\}$.
Then $A=\Q\langle(e_t)_{t\in T'}\rangle$ and
\[
 A^\wedge=\Q\langle\!\langle(e_t)_{t\in T'}\rangle\!\rangle
\]
(associative formal series in the $e_t$).
For every word $w=t_1\cdots t_k$ in the alphabet $T'$, let $c(w)$ be the linear form on $A^\wedge$ ``coefficient of the monomial $e_{t_1}\cdots e_{t_k}$''.
We again write $c(w)$ for the restriction of $c(w)$ to $\Pi\subset A^{\wedge *}$, and for a formal linear combination of words we put $c(\sum\lambda_w w)=\sum\lambda_w c(w)$.
The $c(w)$ form a vector-space basis of $O(\Pi)$.
The product in $O(\Pi)$ corresponds to the shuffle product of words:
\begin{equation}
 c(w')c(w'')=c(w'\shuffle w''),
\tag{3.1.5}
\end{equation}
where, for $w'=t_1\cdots t_k$ and $w''=t_{k+1}\cdots t_{k+\ell}$, $w'\shuffle w''$ is the sum of the $t_{\sigma^{-1}(1)}\cdots t_{\sigma^{-1}(k+\ell)}$ for $\sigma$ running through the set $S_{k,\ell}$ of permutations of $\{1,\ldots,k+\ell\}$ increasing on $\{1,\ldots,k\}$ and on $\{k+1,\ldots,k+\ell\}$.

\subsection*{3.2}
A path $\gamma$ from $a$ to $b$ defines a rational point of $\pi_1(X;b,a)_B=\pi_1(X(\C);b,a)^{\mathrm{un}}$.
Let $\comp_{\dR,B}(\gamma)$ be the image of this rational point by the comparison isomorphism
\[
 \comp_{\dR,B}:\pi_1(X;b,a)_B(\C)\xrightarrow{\sim}\pi_1(X;b,a)_{\dR}(\C)=\Pi(\C).
\]
This image is obtained by ``integration'' along $\gamma$, in the noncommutative group $\Pi$, of the form $\omega$: it is the value at $1$ of the solution $g:[0,1]\to\Pi(\C)$, equal to $1$ at $0$, of
\begin{equation}
 dg(t).g(t)^{-1}=\omega\left(\frac{d\gamma}{dt}\right)dt.
\tag{3.2.1}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{3.3}
The same formalism applies when one allows the base points $a,b$ to be tangential base points (a nonzero tangent vector at a ``rational point at infinity'' $t\in T$ of $X$).
Let $\Theta_t$ be the tangent space to $\PP^1$ at $t$, $\Theta_t^*:=\Theta_t-\{0\}$, and $a\in\Theta_t^*$ a tangential base point at $t$.
Everything happens as if one had a morphism from $\Theta_t^*$ to $X$: one has morphisms
\begin{equation}
 \pi_1(\Theta_t^*,a)_{B/\dR}=\Q(1)_{B/\dR}\longrightarrow\pi_1(X,a)_{B/\dR}
\tag{3.3.1}
\end{equation}
compatible with the comparison isomorphisms.
On the Betti side, this morphism is induced by the germs of maps $\varphi$ from $(\Theta_t,0)$ to $(\PP^1,t)$ with differential the identity.
The space of these germs is contractible, and each $\varphi$ induces a map from a small punctured disk around $0$ (homotopic to $\Theta_t^*$) to $X$.
In de Rham, and after passage to Lie algebras, (3.3.1) is the morphism from $\Q(1)_{\dR}=\Q$ to $L^\wedge$ which sends $1$ to $e_t$.

The $\Q$-form $\pi_1(X;b,a)_B$ of $\Pi(\C)$ is characterized by the property that the images of paths from $a$ to $b$ are rational points.

Warning: $\pi_1(X;b,a)_{\dR}$ is independent of $a$ and $b$, and $\pi_1(X;b,a)_B$ is locally constant in $a$ and $b$, but the comparison isomorphism between their complexifications is not locally constant.
The independence of $\pi_1(X;b,a)_{\dR}$ from $a$ and $b$ comes from the fact that one has the canonical de Rham path ${}_b1_a$ between any two base points.
The image of this path by $\comp_{B,\dR}$ is not locally constant.

\subsection*{3.4}
The case that interests us is that in which $X=\PP^1-\{0,1,\infty\}$ and in which, as base points, one takes the tangential base points $1$ at $0$ and $-1$ at $1$.
They will be denoted $0$ and $1$.
Here
\[
 \omega=e_0\frac{dz}{z}+e_1\frac{dz}{z-1}
\]
and for every $\Q$-algebra $R$,
\[
 \Pi(R)=\{\text{group-like elements of }R\langle\!\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\!\rangle^*\}
\]
(3.1.3).
In Betti, one has the ``straight path'' $dch$ from $0$ to $1$.
We shall again write $dch$ for its image by $\comp_{\dR,B}$ in $\Pi(\C)$.
This image is the following limit for $\epsilon,\eta\to0$ through values $>0$:
\begin{equation}
\comp_{\dR,B}(dch)=\lim \exp(-\log\eta\,e_1)\comp_{\dR,B}([\epsilon,1-\eta])\exp(\log\epsilon\,e_0).
\tag{3.4.1}
\end{equation}
The third (respectively first) factor is to be viewed as computing, in the punctured tangent space at $0$ (respectively at $1$), $\comp_{\dR,B}([1,\epsilon])$ (respectively $\comp_{\dR,B}([-\eta,-1])$), the form $\omega$ being replaced by $(du/u)e_0$ (respectively $(du/u)e_1$).

In general one can express the solution of (3.2.1) as a sum of iterated integrals.
Applying 1.2, one obtains that, for convergent $\zeta(s_1,\ldots,s_k)$, one has:

\paragraph{Proposition 3.5.}
The coefficient of $e_0^{s_1-1}e_1\cdots e_0^{s_k-1}e_1$ in
$dch\in\Pi(\C)\subset\C\langle\!\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\!\rangle^*$ is
$(-1)^k\zeta(s_1,\ldots,s_k)$.

This proposition determines the coefficients $c(w)$ of monomials of $dch$ only for $w$ a word in the alphabet $\{0,1\}$ which does not begin with $1$ and does not end with $0$.
The coefficients $c(0)$ of $e_0$ and $c(1)$ of $e_1$ in $dch$ are zero.
This, together with the fact that $dch$ is group-like, determines the missing coefficients.
More precisely, the vanishing of $c(0)$ and $c(1)$ means that $dch$ belongs to the derived group $\Pi'$ of $\Pi$, whose graded Lie algebra is the part of degree $\geq2$ of that of $\Pi$.
On $\Pi'$, writing $\{p\}$ for a power in the monoid of words,
\begin{equation}
 c(0^{\{p\}})=c(1^{\{p\}})=0\quad\text{for }p>0,
\tag{3.5.1}
\end{equation}
and, applying (3.1.5) to $w10^{\{p-1\}}$ and $0$ (respectively to $1$ and $1^{\{p-1\}}0w$), one checks by induction on $p\geq0$ that
\begin{equation}
 c(w10^{\{p\}})=(-1)^p c((w\shuffle 0^{\{p\}})1),
\tag{3.5.2}
\end{equation}
respectively
\[
 c(1^{\{p\}}0w)=(-1)^p c(0(1^{\{p\}}\shuffle w))
\]
on $\Pi'$.

\subsection*{3.6}
The identity (3.1.5), evaluated at $dch$, expresses the product of two multizeta numbers as a linear combination with integer coefficients of multizeta numbers.

\section*{4. Motivic multizetas}

\subsection*{4.1}
Continue to put $X:=\PP^1-\{0,1,\infty\}$ and to take as base points the tangential base points $0$ or $1$ (see 3.4).
In 5.6 we shall give a precise meaning to the expression ``motivic structure on the affine algebras of the $\pi_1(X;b,a)_{B/\dR}$''.
We shall also say ``motivic structure on the $\pi_1(X;b,a)_{B/\dR}$''.
Heuristically, these are structures coming from geometry, and hence having a meaning both for Betti and for de Rham, and respected by the comparison isomorphisms.
Examples: the product giving the algebra structure, the coproducts coming from composition of paths, the weight filtration, the isomorphism $\pi_1(X;b,a)_{B/\dR}\to\pi_1(X;\sigma(b),\sigma(a))_{B/\dR}$ induced by the involution $\sigma:x\mapsto1-x$ of $\PP^1$.
Likewise, the morphisms (3.3.1) are a motivic structure on $\Q(1)_{B/\dR}$ and $\pi_1(X,a)_{B/\dR}$.
By contrast, neither the Hodge filtration of the affine algebra of $\pi_1(X;b,a)_{\dR}$, nor therefore its grading, nor the independence of $\pi_1(X;b,a)_{\dR}$ from $a$ and $b$, is motivic.
They are available only for de Rham.
The structures in question will be structures of multilinear algebra.
Let $H_B$ (respectively $H_{\dR}$) be the group scheme over $\Q$ of automorphisms of $\pi_1(X;1,0)_B$ (respectively $\pi_1(X;1,0)_{\dR}$) respecting all its motivic structures.
The weight filtration $W$ of the affine algebra is a filtration by finite-dimensional subspaces, and $H_B$ (respectively $H_{\dR}$) is a projective limit of algebraic subgroups of the $\GL(W_i)$.

\subsection*{4.2}
Let $P_{\dR,B}$ be the scheme of isomorphisms from $\pi_1(X;1,0)_B$ to $\pi_1(X;1,0)_{\dR}$ respecting all motivic structures.
The group $H_B$ acts on the right on $P_{\dR,B}$.
The comparison isomorphism $\comp_{\dR,B}$ is a complex point of $P_{\dR,B}$.
The scheme $P_{\dR,B}$ is therefore nonempty.
It is an $H_B$-torsor (terminology of 2.2).

If $Z_B$ is a subscheme of $\pi_1(X;1,0)_B$ stable under $H_B$, twisting by the $H_B$-torsor $P_{\dR,B}$ transforms $\pi_1(X;1,0)_B$ into $\pi_1(X;1,0)_{\dR}={}_1\Pi_0$, and $Z_B$ into a subscheme $Z_{\dR}$ of ${}_1\Pi_0$.
Over $\C$, $Z_{\dR}(\C)$ is the image of $Z_B(\C)$ by $\comp_{\dR,B}$.

I like to think of the $H_B$-orbit of $dch$ as the space of points $ch$ of $\pi_1(X;1,0)_B$ having all the properties of $dch$ expressible in motivic terms.
For example: the morphism $\pi_1(X;1,0)_B\to\pi_1(X;0,1)_B$ induced by the involution $x\mapsto1-x$ of $\PP^1$ must transform $ch$ into its inverse.

Let $M_B\subset\pi_1(X;1,0)_B$ be the closure of the $H_B$-orbit of $dch$.
The twist of $M_B$ by $P_{\dR,B}$ is a closed subscheme $M_{\dR}$ of ${}_1\Pi_0$.
We again write $dch$ for the image of $dch$ by $\comp_{\dR,B}$.
It is a complex point of $M_{\dR}$, and $M_{\dR}(\C)\subset{}_1\Pi_0(\C)$ is the closure of its $H_{\dR}(\C)$-orbit.

\paragraph{Definition 4.3.}
Let $s=(s_1,\ldots,s_k)$.
If $k\ne0$, we assume $s_1\geq2$.
Let $w$ be the word $0^{\{s_1-1\}}1\cdots 0^{\{s_k-1\}}1$ in the alphabet $\{0,1\}$, and let $c(w)\in O(\Pi)$ be the function ``coefficient of the monomial $e_0^{s_1-1}e_1\cdots e_0^{s_k-1}e_1$'' (3.1).
We denote by $\zeta^m(s)$ the restriction to $M_{\dR}$ of $(-1)^k c(w)$.

The $\zeta^m(s)$ are the motivic multizetas (F. Brown [1] 2.3).
The algebra denoted $M$ in the introduction is $O(M_{\dR})$.
Before [1], only the restriction of the $\zeta^m(s)$ to the orbit of $1$ was considered.
This gave rise to painful contortions.
By 3.5, the value of $\zeta^m(s)$ at the complex point $dch$ of $M_{\dR}$ is $\zeta(s)$.
Every polynomial identity with rational coefficients in the $\zeta^m(s)$ therefore gives, by evaluation at $dch$, a polynomial relation among the $\zeta(s)$.
Grothendieck's yoga of periods predicts that every polynomial relation among the $\zeta(s)$ comes from geometry; more precisely, that it is obtained as above.

For every word $w$ in $0$ and $1$, similarly write $c^m(w)$ for the restriction of $c(w)$ to $M_{\dR}$.
Relying on the remark following 3.5, one can show that the $c^m(w)$ are linear combinations with integer coefficients of the $\zeta^m(s)$.

By construction, $H_{\dR}$ acts on $M_{\dR}$.
Its Lie algebra therefore acts by derivations on the affine algebra of $M_{\dR}$, a quotient of that of $\Pi$.
These derivations, which have a meaning only once one has lifted from the $\zeta(s)$ to the $\zeta^m(s)$, will play a key role in F. Brown's arguments.

\section*{5. Mixed Tate motives}

\subsection*{5.1}
We are here in one of the cases in which the philosophy of motives is not only a precious guide, but allows proofs.
Over every field $K$, Voevodsky has defined a motivic triangulated category $V_K$, endowed with a tensor product, and containing Tate objects $\Z(n)$ ($n\in\Z$).
If $K$ is a number field, after tensoring with $\Q$ one can construct a subcategory $MT(K)$ of $V_K\otimes\Q$, the category of mixed Tate motives over $K$, and this category is Tannakian.
This rests on our knowledge of the $K$-theory groups of a number field, due to the transcendental determination of $H_i(B\GL(n,K),\C)$ for $n\gg i$.

All this is for me a black box.
It is difficult to open, and this is why the identities promised by 0.2 are not explicit.

\subsection*{5.2}
Let us review some properties of the category $MT(K)$.
It is a Tannakian category over $\Q$.
It contains a rank-one object $\Q(1)$, the Tate motive.
Since this motive has rank one, $\Q(n):=\Q(1)^{\otimes n}$ is defined for $n\in\Z$.
The objects of $MT(K)$ are endowed with a finite increasing functorial filtration, compatible with the tensor product, the weight filtration $W$.
It is indexed by even integers.
The functor $M\mapsto\Gr^W(M)$ is exact, and $\Gr^W_{-2n}(M)$ is a sum of copies of $\Q(n)$.

Suppose, for simplicity, that $K=\Q$.
Then one has Betti and de Rham fiber functors
\[
 \omega_B,\omega_{\dR}:MT(K)\longrightarrow \Q\text{-vector spaces}
\]
and a comparison isomorphism
\[
 \comp_{\dR,B}:\omega_B\otimes\C\longrightarrow \omega_{\dR}\otimes\C.
\]
Note that, because $K=\Q$, the fiber functor $\omega_{\dR}$ coincides with the fiber functor $\omega$ of [2].
For the motive $\Q(1)$, $\omega_B(\Q(1))$, $\omega_{\dR}(\Q(1))$, and $\comp_{\dR,B}$ are as in 2.4.

The functor $\omega_{\dR}$ is endowed with a Hodge filtration $F$, opposite, up to a renumbering, to the weight filtration of $\omega_{\dR}(M)$ by the $\omega_{\dR}(W_{2n}M)$.
This makes it into a graded functor, as in 3.1.

\subsection*{5.3}
If $T$ is a Tannakian category, we shall call a $T$-algebra an Ind-object $A$ of $T$, endowed with a product $A\otimes A\to A$.
One defines the category of affine $T$-schemes as the opposite of that of commutative $T$-algebras with unit, and an affine $T$-group scheme as a group in this category.
For $T$ a category of motives, one replaces the prefix $T$ by the adjective motivic.

\subsection*{5.4}
The $\pi_1(X;b,a)_B$, $\pi_1(X;b,a)_{\dR}$ of 3.1, 3.3, and the comparison isomorphism between their complexifications are motivic: they are images of a motivic group scheme $\pi_1(X;b,a)_{\mot}$ ([2] \S4).
The morphisms ``composition of paths'' and (3.3.1) are motivic as well.

If $X=\PP^1-\{0,1,\infty\}$ and $a,b$ are chosen among the tangential base points $0,1$ of 3.4, the points $0,1,\infty$ remain distinct after reduction modulo $p$, and the tangent vectors chosen at $0$ and $1$ remain nonzero.
One can deduce that the affine algebras $O(\pi_1(X;b,a)_{\mot})$ are Ind-objects of a Tannakian subcategory $MT(\Z)$ of $MT(\Q)$, that of mixed Tate motives with good reduction over $\Spec(\Z)$ ([2] 1.7, 1.4, 1.8).
In $MT(\Z)$,
\begin{align}
 \Ext^1(\Q(0),\Q(n))&\text{ is }
 \begin{cases}
 \text{one-dimensional over }\Q,& n\text{ odd and }n\geq3,\\
 0,&\text{otherwise,}
 \end{cases}
\tag{5.4.1}\\
 \Ext^2(\Q(0),\Q(n))&=0.
\tag{5.4.2}
\end{align}

\subsection*{5.5}
Let $T$ be a Tannakian category over a field $K$, and let $\omega$ be a fiber functor over an extension $K'$ of $K$.
The family of the $\omega(Y)$, for $Y$ in $T$, endowed with the $\omega(f):\otimes\omega(Y_i)\to\otimes\omega(Z_j)$ for $f:\otimes Y_i\to\otimes Z_j$ a morphism of $T$, is a family of $K'$-vector spaces endowed with a structure of multilinear algebra.
The subgroup scheme of $\prod \GL(\omega(Y))$ (product over $\Ob(T)$) that respects this structure is the group scheme $\Aut(\omega)$ of automorphisms of the fiber functor $\omega$.
It is denoted $\pi(T,\omega)$ and is called the fundamental group of $T$ at $\omega$.
One will see in examples that it is of the ``same nature'' as the $\omega(Y)$, for example that its affine algebra is graded if the functor $\omega$ is graded.
The explanation of this fact is that there exists a $T$-group scheme $\pi(T)$, acting functorially on each object of $T$, such that for every fiber functor $\omega$, $\pi(T,\omega)$ is $\omega(\pi(T))$.

Suppose $K'=K$.
In this case, $\omega$ induces an equivalence of $T$ with the category of finite-dimensional linear representations of $\pi(T,\omega)$.
If $T$ is a category of motives, the fundamental groups are also called motivic Galois groups.

\subsection*{5.6}
Let $\langle(Y_i)\rangle$ be the Tannakian subcategory of $T$ generated (by subquotients, sums, duals, and tensor products) by a family $(Y_i)$ of objects of $T$.
A $T$-structure on the family of the $\omega(Y_i)$ is the image by $\omega$ of a morphism of $\langle(Y_i)\rangle$.
More precisely, it is the datum of the image by $\omega$ of subquotients of sums of tensor products of the $Y_i$ and $Y_i^\vee$, and of morphisms between such subquotients.
If $T$ is a category of motives, one will rather say motivic structure.
The subgroup of $\prod\GL(\omega(Y_i))$ which respects all $T$-structures is therefore the fundamental group of $\langle(Y_i)\rangle$ at $\omega$.
Every algebraic subgroup of a group $\GL(V)$ is the subgroup respecting a few subspaces and elements in tensor spaces on $V$.
The equivalence 5.5 therefore ensures that this fundamental group of $\langle(Y_i)\rangle$ at $\omega$ is the quotient of $\pi(T,\omega)$ which acts faithfully on the family of the $\omega(Y_i)$.
Same terminology if one starts from a family of ind-objects of $T$: one replaces it by the family of subobjects in $T$.

Take $T:=MT(\Z)$.
With the notation $B/\dR$ of 2.3, set
\begin{equation}
 G_{B/\dR}:=\pi(MT(\Z),\omega_{B/\dR}).
\tag{5.6.1}
\end{equation}
The group scheme $H_{B/\dR}$ of automorphisms of $\pi_1(X;1,0)_{B/\dR}$ ``respecting all motivic structures'' considered in 4.1 is the quotient of $G_{B/\dR}$ acting faithfully on
\[
 \pi_1(X;1,0)_{B/\dR}=\omega_{B/\dR}(\pi_1(X;1,0)_{\mot}).
\]
By the equivalence 5.5, a closed subscheme of $\pi_1(X;1,0)_B$ stable under $H_B$ is the Betti realization of a closed subscheme of $\pi_1(X;1,0)_{\mot}$.
Example: there is a unique motivic subscheme $M_{\mot}$ of $\pi_1(X;1,0)_{\mot}$ whose Betti realization is the orbit closure $M_B$; the subscheme $M_{\dR}$ (4.2) of $\pi_1(X;1,0)_{\dR}$ is its de Rham realization.

\subsection*{5.7}
The action of $G_{B/\dR}$ on $\Q(1)_{B/\dR}$ defines a morphism $t$ from $G_{B/\dR}$ to the multiplicative group $\Gm$.
For every $M$ in $MT(\Z)$, $G_{B/\dR}$ respects the weight filtration of $\omega_{B/\dR}M$, and since $\Gr^W_{-2n}(M)$ is a sum of copies of $\Q(n)$, the action of $g$ in $G_{B/\dR}$ on $\Gr^W_{-2n}(M)$ is multiplication by $t(g)^n$.
The kernel $U_{B/\dR}$ of $t$ therefore acts trivially on the $\Gr^W_{-2n}(M)$: it is a pro-unipotent group scheme.

Let $\tau(\lambda)$ be multiplication by $\lambda^n$ on $\omega^n_{\dR}(M)$.
The $\tau(\lambda)$ define a section $\tau:\Gm\to G_{\dR}$ of $t$, which makes $G_{\dR}$ a semidirect product $\Gm\ltimes U_{\dR}$.
The action of $\Gm$ on $U_{\dR}$ (by inner automorphisms in $G_{\dR}$) will again be denoted $\tau$.
It permits one to define the graded Lie algebra $\Lie^{\mathrm{gr}}(U_{\dR})$.
The vanishing (5.4.2) implies that this graded Lie algebra is free, and (5.4.1) that it is freely generated by one generator in each odd degree $\geq3$.
The functor $\omega_{\dR}$ induces an equivalence of categories from $MT(\Z)$ with the category of finite-dimensional graded vector spaces, endowed with an action, compatible with the gradings and therefore nilpotent, of $\Lie^{\mathrm{gr}}(U_{\dR})$.

\subsection*{5.8}
Let $P$ be the scheme of isomorphisms of fiber functors from $\omega_{\dR}$ to $\omega_B$.
The comparison isomorphism $\comp_{B,\dR}$ is a complex point of $P$.
The scheme $P$ is therefore nonempty, and is a $G_{\dR}$-torsor.
Since $G_{\dR}$ is an extension of $\Gm$ by $U_{\dR}$, a Galois cohomology argument shows that every $G_{\dR}$-torsor has a rational point: there exists an isomorphism of fiber functors $p:\omega_{\dR}\to\omega_B$.
Write
\begin{equation}
 p=\comp_{B,\dR}\cdot a
\tag{5.8.1}
\end{equation}
with $a\in G_{\dR}(\C)$.
It follows from (5.8.1) that for every $M$ in $MT(\Z)$, if one identifies $\omega_{\dR}(M)\otimes\C$ with $\omega_B(M)\otimes\C$ by $\comp_{B,\dR}$, $\omega_B(M)\subset\omega_B(M)\otimes\C=\omega_{\dR}(M)\otimes\C$ verifies
\begin{equation}
 \omega_B(M)=a(\omega_{\dR}(M)).
\tag{5.8.2}
\end{equation}
Since $\omega_B(\Q(1))=2\pi i\Q$, one has $t(a)\in2\pi i\Q^*$, and $a=u\tau(\lambda)$ with $u\in U_{\dR}(\C)$ and $\lambda\in2\pi i\Q^*$.
A compatibility with complex conjugation makes it possible to show that one can choose ([2] 2.12)
\begin{equation}
 a\in U_{\dR}(\R).\tau(2\pi i).
\tag{5.8.3}
\end{equation}

\section*{6. The space $\widetilde H(2,3)$ of functions on $\pi_1(X;1,0)_{\dR}$}

From now on, $X:=\PP^1-\{0,1,\infty\}$, and the notation of 3.4 is in force.
By abuse of language, write $\Q\langle\!\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\!\rangle$ (respectively $\Q\langle\!\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\!\rangle^*$) for the group scheme over $\Q$ such that, for every $\Q$-algebra $R$, the set of its $R$-points is $R\langle\!\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\!\rangle$ (respectively $R\langle\!\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\!\rangle^*$).
Recall that $\pi_1(X;b,a)_{\dR}$ is a copy ${}_b\Pi_a$ of the subscheme $\Pi$ of group-like elements (3.1.3) of $\Q\langle\!\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\!\rangle^*$.
We shall regard it as contained in a copy $\Q\langle\!\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\!\rangle_{b,a}$ of $\Q\langle\!\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\!\rangle$.
A point $x$ of $\Q\langle\!\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\!\rangle$ will sometimes be denoted ${}_b x_a$ when regarded as belonging to this copy.

\subsection*{6.1}
As a $\Q$-vector space, the affine algebra $O(\Pi)$ of $\Pi$ has for basis the ``coefficients of monomials'' $c(w)$ of 3.1.
It is graded.
If the word $w$ in the alphabet $\{0,1\}$ has length $d$, the element $c(w)$ of $O(\Pi)$ is of degree $-d$, of codegree $d$.
When we write $B'$, with decorations attached, for a set of words, $B$, with the same decorations attached, will be the corresponding set of $c(w)$.

\paragraph{Definition 6.2.}
(i) $B'(2,3)$ is the set of words that are concatenations of the words $01$ and $001$;

(ii) $\widetilde H(2,3)$ is the vector subspace of $O(\Pi)$ with basis $B(2,3)$.

The restrictions to $M_{\dR}$ of the $c(w)$ in $B(2,3)$ are those of the $(-1)^k\zeta^m(s_1,\ldots,s_k)$ where each $s_i$ is $2$ or $3$.

For every graded vector space $Z$, with finite-dimensional homogeneous components, the Poincare series of $Z$ is
\begin{equation}
 P(Z,t)=\sum \dim(Z_d)t^d.
\tag{6.2.1}
\end{equation}
If $\widetilde H(2,3)$ is graded by the codegree, one has
\begin{equation}
 P(\widetilde H(2,3),t)=(1-t^2-t^3)^{-1}.
\tag{6.2.2}
\end{equation}
This is verified by expanding $(1-t^2-t^3)^{-1}=\sum(t^2+t^3)^n$.

\subsection*{6.3}
The words $w\in B'(2,3)$ are characterized by:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*.]
\item they do not begin with $1$ and do not end with $0$;
\item they do not contain two consecutive $1$'s;
\item they do not contain three consecutive $0$'s.
\end{enumerate}
For a function $f\in O(\Pi)$ to be a linear combination of $c(w)$ where $w$ satisfies a, b, or c is equivalent respectively to:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*'.]
\item for $\gamma$ in $\Pi$, $f(\exp(ue_1)\gamma\exp(te_0))$ is independent of $t$ and $u$;
\item for $\gamma,\delta$ in $\Pi$, $f(\gamma\exp(te_1)\delta)$ is of degree $\leq1$ in $t$;
\item for $\gamma,\delta$ in $\Pi$, $f(\gamma\exp(te_0)\delta)$ is of degree $\leq2$ in $t$.
\end{enumerate}
To verify this, note that the linear combinations of elements of $\Pi(\Q)$ are dense in $\Q\langle\!\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\!\rangle$, and that if $f$ is interpreted as a continuous linear form on $\Q\langle\!\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\!\rangle$, condition a' (respectively b', respectively c') is equivalent to the same condition, where one allows $\gamma,\delta$ to be arbitrary in $\Q\langle\!\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\!\rangle$.
The conditions a', b', c' are motivic, because they are expressible in terms of composition of paths and of (3.3.1).
Here one must interpret $\gamma$ (respectively $\gamma,\delta$) as being in ${}_1\Pi_0$ (respectively in ${}_1\Pi_1$ and ${}_1\Pi_0$, respectively in ${}_1\Pi_0$ and ${}_0\Pi_0$).

To be convinced of the motivic character of a', b', c', one can observe that these conditions admit a ``Betti'' formulation.
One obtains it by regarding $f$ as a function on $\pi_1(X;1,0)$, and replacing $\exp(te_0)$ (respectively $\exp(te_1)$) by $\sigma_0^t$ (respectively $\sigma_1^t$) with $t\in\Z$ and $\sigma_0$ (respectively $\sigma_1$) a positive turn around $0$ in the punctured tangent space to $\PP^1$ at $0$ (respectively at $1$) (cf. 3.3).

We leave to the reader the verification of the following lemma:

\paragraph{Lemma 6.4.}
Let $f=\sum\lambda_w c(w)$ in $O(\Pi)$.
In order that, for all $\gamma_0,\ldots,\gamma_r$ in $\Pi$,
\begin{equation}
 f(\gamma_0\exp(te_0)\gamma_1\exp(te_0)\cdots\exp(te_0)\gamma_r)
\tag{6.4.1}
\end{equation}
be of degree $\leq d$ in $t$, it is necessary and sufficient that for every $w$ such that $\lambda_w\ne0$, the total length of at most $r$ disjoint strings of consecutive $0$'s in $w$ be $\leq d$.

\subsection*{6.5}
Let $B'_\ell(2,3)$ (respectively $B'_{\leq\ell}(2,3)$) be the set of words $w$ in $B'(2,3)$ having exactly (respectively at most) $\ell$ factors $001$.
With the convention of 6.1, let $N_\ell$ be the subspace of $\widetilde H(2,3)$ with basis $B_{\leq\ell}(2,3)$.
It follows from 6.4 that for $f$ in $\widetilde H(2,3)$ to be in $N_\ell$, it is necessary and sufficient that for $r=\ell+1$, and every choice of the $\gamma_i$, (6.4.1) be of degree $\leq2\ell+1$ in $t$.
This property is motivic.
The action of $G_{\dR}$ on $O({}_1\Pi_0)$ therefore respects $\widetilde H(2,3)$ and its increasing filtration $N$.

\paragraph{Proposition 6.6.}
The action of $U_{\dR}$ on $\Gr^N\widetilde H(2,3)$ is trivial.

\paragraph{Proof.}
The graded Lie algebra of $U_{\dR}$ is generated by elements $\nu_d$ of odd degrees $d\geq3$, and, if $O({}_1\Pi_0)$ is graded by the degree (6.1), the action is compatible with the gradings.
It suffices to show that each $\nu_d$ acts trivially.
Let $w$ be a word of length $n$, a concatenation of words $01$ and of $\ell$ words $001$.
We have $n\equiv\ell\pmod 2$.
The image of $c(w)$ by $\nu_d$ is a linear combination of $c(w')$ with $w'$ of length $n-d$ and a concatenation of words $01$ and of $\ell'\leq\ell$ words $001$.
We have $n-d\equiv\ell'\pmod2$, and, $d$ being odd, $\ell$ and $\ell'$ do not have the same parity.
That $\ell'<\ell$ proves 6.6.

\subsection*{6.7}
It follows from 6.6 that the action of $\Lie^{\mathrm{gr}}U_{\dR}$ on $N_\ell/N_{\ell-2}$ factors through the abelianization $\Lie^{\mathrm{gr}}U^{\ab}_{\dR}$.
The action of $\nu_d$ on $N_\ell/N_{\ell-2}$ therefore does not depend, up to a factor, on the choice of the generators $\nu_d$.
It is given by a map
\begin{equation}
 \Gr^N(\nu_d):\Gr^N_\ell(\widetilde H(2,3))\longrightarrow\Gr^N_{\ell-1}(\widetilde H(2,3)).
\tag{6.7.1}
\end{equation}
The vector space $\Gr^N_\ell(\widetilde H(2,3))$ has for basis the image of $B_\ell(2,3)$ (6.5).
It is graded by the codegree.
Let $B'_\ell(2,3)_n$ be the part of $B'_\ell(2,3)$ consisting of words of length $n$.
Write $\Gr^N_\ell(\widetilde H(2,3))_n$ for the direct factor of codegree $n$ of $\Gr^N_\ell(\widetilde H(2,3))$.
It has for basis the image of $B_\ell(2,3)_n$.
It is nonzero only for $n\equiv\ell\pmod2$ and $n-3\ell\geq0$.

The morphisms (6.7.1) induce
\begin{equation}
 \sum \Gr^N(\nu_d):\Gr^N_\ell(\widetilde H(2,3))_n
 \longrightarrow \bigoplus_d \Gr^N_{\ell-1}(\widetilde H(2,3))_{n-d},
\tag{6.7.2}
\end{equation}
where it suffices to sum over odd $d$ satisfying $3\leq d\leq3+n-3\ell$.
The left-hand side of (6.7.2) has a basis indexed by $B'_\ell(2,3)_n$.
The right-hand side has a basis indexed by the union of the $B'_{\ell-1}(2,3)_{n-d}$.
If $\ell\geq1$, one obtains a bijection between these sets of words by associating to the word $w\in B'_\ell(2,3)_n$ the word $\bar w$ deduced from it by removing from $w$ its final $(001)(01)\cdots(01)$.

F. Brown computes the morphisms (6.7.2) and proves:

\paragraph{Theorem 6.8.}
For $\ell\geq1$, the morphisms (6.7.2) are isomorphisms.

The strategy that he uses to prove 6.8 will be explained in \S8.

\paragraph{Corollary 6.9.}
The restriction morphism to $M_{\dR}$
\begin{equation}
 \restr:\widetilde H(2,3)\longrightarrow O(M_{\dR})
\tag{6.9.1}
\end{equation}
is injective.

Applying (6.2.2), one deduces from 6.9:

\paragraph{Corollary 6.10.}
The Poincare series of $O(M_{\dR})$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 P(O(M_{\dR}),t)\geq(1-t^2-t^3)^{-1}
\tag{6.10.1}
\end{equation}
(coefficientwise inequality), with equality if and only if (6.9.1) is bijective.

Assume 6.8, and prove 6.9.

\paragraph{Lemma 6.11.}
The morphism (6.9.1) is injective on $N_0$.

\paragraph{Proof.}
The morphism induced by (6.9.1) from $N_0$ to $O(M_{\dR})$ is compatible with the gradings by the codegree.
It therefore suffices to verify its injectivity separately in each codegree.
The $c((01)^n)$ form a basis of $N_0$, and $c((01)^n)$ is of codegree $2n$.
Since these codegrees are distinct, it suffices to verify that for each $n$ the restriction of $c((01)^n)$ to $M_{\dR}$ is nonzero.
The value of $c((01)^n)$ at $dch\in M_{\dR}(\C)$ is $(-1)^n\zeta(2^{\{n\}})$, where $\zeta(2^{\{n\}})=\zeta(2,\ldots,2)$ with $n$ copies of ``2''.
One concludes by observing that every multizeta number is $>0$, hence nonzero.

\paragraph{Remark 6.12.}
Expanding the identities due to Euler
\[
 \prod_{n>0}(1-z^2/n^2)=\sin(\pi z)/\pi z
 =\sum (-1)^n(\pi z)^{2n}/(2n+1)!,
\]
one sees that $\zeta(2^{\{n\}})=\pi^{2n}/(2n+1)!$.
That $\zeta(2^{\{n\}})$ is a rational multiple of $\pi^{2n}$ already followed from the fact that the motivic subspace $N_0$ of $O({}_1\Pi_0)$ is fixed under $U_{\dR}$, hence is the de Rham realization of a sum of Tate motives.
This forces $\zeta(2^{\{n\}})$ to be a period of $\Q(-2n)$.

\subsection*{6.13. Proof of $6.8\Rightarrow6.9$}
Filter the image $\restr(\widetilde H(2,3))$ of $\widetilde H(2,3)$ in $O(M_{\dR})$ by the images of the $N_\ell$.
It suffices to show the injectivity of
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde H(2,3)\longrightarrow\restr(\widetilde H(2,3))
\tag{6.13.1}
\end{equation}
after passage to the associated gradeds.
We prove by induction on $\ell\geq0$ the injectivity on $\Gr^N_\ell(\widetilde H(2,3))$.
Lemma 6.11 proves this injectivity for $\ell=0$.
If $\ell>0$, consider the morphism
\[
 \sum\nu_d:\Gr^N_\ell\widetilde H(2,3)\longrightarrow\bigoplus\Gr^N_{\ell-1}\widetilde H(2,3),
\]
where the target is a sum of copies of $\bigoplus\Gr^N_{\ell-1}\widetilde H(2,3)$ indexed by the odd integers $d\geq3$.
Theorem 6.8 ensures that this morphism is bijective.
The morphism (6.9.1) commutes with the derivations $\nu_d$.
The diagram
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large,row sep=large]
\Gr^N_\ell\widetilde H(2,3) \arrow[r,"\Gr^N_\ell(6.13.1)"] \arrow[d,"\sum\nu_d"',"\sim"]
& \Gr^N_\ell\restr(\widetilde H(2,3)) \arrow[d,"\sum\nu_d"] \\
\bigoplus\Gr^N_{\ell-1}\widetilde H(2,3) \arrow[r,"\Gr^N_{\ell-1}(6.13.1)"]

\end{tikzcd}

is therefore commutative.
The induction hypothesis ensures that $\Gr^N_{\ell-1}(6.13.1)$ is injective.
Since the first vertical arrow is injective, $\Gr^N_\ell(6.13.1)$ is injective too.

\section*{7. Structure of the orbit closure $M_{\dR}$}

\subsection*{7.1}
Consider the groupoid of the ${}_b\Pi_a$ for $a,b\in\{0,1\}$, endowed with $e_0\in\Lie({}_0\Pi_0)$ and $e_1\in\Lie({}_1\Pi_1)$.
Let $V_{\dR}$ be the group scheme of automorphisms of this structure.
The action of $U_{\dR}$ on the groupoid of the ${}_b\Pi_a$ factors through a morphism
\begin{equation}
 I:U_{\dR}\longrightarrow V_{\dR}.
\tag{7.1.1}
\end{equation}
We write $IU_{\dR}$ for the image of $U_{\dR}$ in $V_{\dR}$.

The group of automorphisms that respect $e_0\in\Lie({}_0\Pi_0)$ and $e_1\in\Lie({}_1\Pi_1)$ only up to a factor, the same for $e_0$ and $e_1$, is a semidirect product $\Gm\ltimes V_{\dR}$.
We write $\circ$ for its group law, and $\tau$ for the inclusion of $\Gm$.
The action of $\Gm$ on the ${}_b\Pi_a$ is that of the subgroup $\Gm$ of $G_{\dR}$.
It corresponds to the gradings.

By [2] 5.9,
\begin{equation}
 v\longmapsto v({}_110):V_{\dR}\longrightarrow{}_1\Pi_0
\tag{7.1.2}
\end{equation}
is an isomorphism of schemes.
This isomorphism identifies $V_{\dR}$ with $\Pi$, endowed with a new group law, denoted $\circ$.
The action $\tau$ of $\Gm$ which defines the grading of $O({}_1\Pi_0)$ fixes ${}_110$.
It follows that (7.1.2) is compatible with the gradings.
Since it sends identity element to identity element, it defines an isomorphism of graded vector spaces
\begin{equation}
 \Lie^{\mathrm{gr}}V_{\dR}\xrightarrow{\sim}\Lie^{\mathrm{gr}}\Pi.
\tag{7.1.3}
\end{equation}
The graded Lie algebra $\Lie^{\mathrm{gr}}V_{\dR}$ is thus identified with $\Lie^{\mathrm{gr}}\Pi$, endowed with a new bracket, the Ihara bracket $\{,\}$.

\subsection*{7.2}
Write $x\mapsto \gamma_{b,a}\langle x\rangle$ for the action on ${}_b\Pi_a$ of $\gamma$ in $(\Pi,\circ)$.
It is computed as follows.
The action on ${}_a\Pi_a$ respects the group structure.
For $a=0$, it is induced by the automorphism
\begin{equation}
 \gamma_{00}: e_0\longmapsto e_0,\quad e_1\longmapsto \gamma^{-1}e_1\gamma
\tag{7.2.1}
\end{equation}
of the completed enveloping algebra $\Q\langle\!\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\!\rangle$ of $\Lie\Pi$.
To verify this, note that $e_0\in\Lie({}_0\Pi_0)$ and $e_1\in\Lie({}_1\Pi_1)$ are fixed by definition of $V_{\dR}$.
Writing ${}_0(e_1)_0=({}_110)^{-1}{}_1(e_1)_1({}_110)$, one obtains as promised
\[
 {}_0(e_1)_0\longmapsto \gamma^{-1}{}_1(e_1)_1\gamma.
\]
The automorphism $\gamma_{10}$ of ${}_1\Pi_0$ is induced by the $\gamma_{00}$-semilinear automorphism
\begin{equation}
 \gamma_{10}:x\longmapsto \gamma\,\gamma_{00}\langle x\rangle
\tag{7.2.2}
\end{equation}
of the right $\Q\langle\!\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\!\rangle$-module $\Q\langle\!\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\!\rangle$.
Verify this as above, starting from ${}_1x_0={}_110\,{}_0x_0$.
Since $\gamma\circ x$ sends ${}_110$ to $\gamma_{10}\langle{}_1x_0\rangle$, this is also
\begin{equation}
 \gamma_{10}:x\longmapsto\gamma\circ x,
\tag{7.2.3}
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
 x\circ y=x\,x_{00}\langle y\rangle.
\tag{7.2.4}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{7.3}
Via $\comp_{\dR,B}$, regard $\pi_1(X;b,a)_B$ as a $\Q$-structure on the complexification of $\pi_1(X;b,a)_{\dR}={}_b\Pi_a$, the Betti $\Q$-structure, denoted $({}_b\Pi_a)_B$.
For this new $\Q$-structure, $2\pi i e_0$ in $\Lie({}_0\Pi_0)_\C$ and $2\pi i e_1$ in $\Lie({}_1\Pi_1)_\C$ are rational: their exponentials are the $\sigma_0$ and $\sigma_1$ of 6.3.
One deduces:

\paragraph{Lemma 7.4.}
Let $ch$ be a rational point of $({}_1\Pi_0)_B$.
Regard its image by $\comp_{\dR,B}$, still denoted $ch$, as a complex point of $(\Pi,\circ)$.
Then the complex point $ch\circ\tau(2\pi i)$ of $\Gm\ltimes V_{\dR}=\Gm\ltimes(\Pi,\circ)$ transforms the $\Q$-structure of ${}_b\Pi_a$ into its Betti $\Q$-structure.

\subsection*{7.5}
Let $a=u\tau(2\pi i)\in U_{\dR}(\R)\tau(2\pi i)\subset G_{\dR}(\C)$ be as in (5.8.3).
If $ch$ is as in 7.4, both $ch\tau(2\pi i)$ and $I(u)\tau(2\pi i)$ transform the $\Q$-structure of the ${}_b\Pi_a$ into their Betti $\Q$-structure.
If $\gamma\in V_{\dR}(\C)=\Pi(\C)$ is defined by
\begin{equation}
 ch\circ\tau(2\pi i)=I(u)\circ\tau(2\pi i)\circ\gamma,
\tag{7.5.1}
\end{equation}
then $\gamma$ respects the $\Q$-structure, and hence is in $V_{\dR}(\Q)=\Pi(\Q)$.
Writing again $\tau$ for the action of $\Gm$ on $\Pi$, one can rewrite (7.5.1) in the form
\[
 ch=I(u)\circ\tau(2\pi i)(\gamma).
\]
Apply this to the straight path $dch$:

\paragraph{Lemma 7.6.}
For $u$ as above, there exists $\gamma$ in $\Pi(\Q)$ such that
\begin{equation}
 dch=I(u)\circ\tau(2\pi i)(\gamma).
\tag{7.6.1}
\end{equation}
Every such $\gamma$ verifies
\begin{equation}
 \tau(-1)(\gamma)=\gamma.
\tag{7.6.2}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Proof.}
Equation (7.6.2) follows from the fact that $u$ and $dch$, and hence also $\tau(2\pi i)(\gamma)$, are real.

\subsection*{7.7}
Apply (7.2.3) to (7.6.1).
One obtains that the orbit of $dch$ under $U_{\dR}(\C)$ is
\[
 IU_{\dR}(\C)\circ\tau(2\pi i)(\gamma)\subset \Pi_{\dR}(\C).
\]
To obtain the orbit under $G_{\dR}=\Gm\ltimes U_{\dR}$, it suffices to replace $\tau(2\pi i)(\gamma)$ by its orbit $\tau(\C^*)(\gamma)$ under $\Gm$:
\begin{equation}
 G_{\dR}(\C)(dch)=IU_{\dR}(\C)\circ\tau(\C^*)(\gamma).
\tag{7.7.1}
\end{equation}
Indeed, $IU_{\dR}$ is already stable under $\Gm$.
As we already knew, the orbit (7.7.1) is, in the de Rham sense, defined over $\Q$.
It is the set of complex points of
\begin{equation}
 IU_{\dR}\circ\tau(\Gm)(\gamma)\subset\Pi.
\tag{7.7.2}
\end{equation}
In (7.7.2), the product is that of $(\Pi,\circ)=V_{\dR}$, and the result is regarded as a subscheme of ${}_1\Pi_0$.

In $\Q\langle\!\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\!\rangle$, write $\gamma=\sum c_w w$.
By (7.6.2), the sum runs only over monomials $w$ of even degree.
If
\[
 \rho(\lambda):=\sum \lambda^{\deg(w)/2}c_w w,
\]
then $\rho(\lambda)$ is defined for $\lambda\in\Ga$ and verifies
\begin{equation}
 \rho(\lambda^2)=\tau(\lambda)(\gamma).
\tag{7.7.3}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Proposition 7.8.}
The morphism of schemes
\begin{equation}
 IU_{\dR}\times\Ga\longrightarrow\Pi:(u,\lambda)\longmapsto u\circ\rho(\lambda)
\tag{7.8.1}
\end{equation}
induces an isomorphism from $IU_{\dR}\times\Ga$ to $M_{\dR}\subset{}_1\Pi_0$ defined in 4.2.

\paragraph{Proof.}
If $u$ is in $IU_{\dR}\subset V_{\dR}=\Pi\subset\Q\langle\!\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\!\rangle^*$, it can be written $u=1+\sum e_w w$, where the sum runs only over monomials of degree $\geq3$.
This is a consequence of the fact that $\Lie^{\mathrm{gr}}U_{\dR}$ is zero in degree $<3$.
The coefficient of $e_0e_1$ in $u\circ\rho(\lambda)$ therefore coincides with that in $\rho(\lambda)$.
The coefficient of $e_0e_1$ in $dch$ is $-\pi^2/6=(2\pi i)^2/24$.
By (7.7.3), that in $\gamma$ is therefore $1/24$, and that in $\rho(\lambda)$ is $\lambda/24$.
Starting from $x=u\circ\rho(\lambda)$, one recovers $\lambda$ as $24c(e_0e_1)(x)$.
That $x$ be of the form $u\circ\rho(\lambda)$ is equivalent to saying that $u$ defined by $x=u\circ\rho(24c(e_0e_1)(x))$ is in $IU_{\dR}$.
This is a closed condition, and (7.8.1) is an isomorphism onto a closed subscheme of $\Pi$, in which the orbit of $dch$ is dense.
This proves 7.8.

\subsection*{7.9}
We shall write $D$ for the image of $\Ga$ by $\rho$.
Thus $\rho:\Ga\xrightarrow{\sim}D$, and 7.8 says that the group law $\circ$ induces an isomorphism
\begin{equation}
 \circ:IU_{\dR}\times D\xrightarrow{\sim}M_{\dR}.
\tag{7.9.1}
\end{equation}
This isomorphism is compatible with the gradings of affine algebras, if $O(D)=\Q[\lambda]$ is graded by giving $\lambda$ degree $-2$ (codegree $2$).

\subsection*{7.10}
By 6.6 applied to $N_0$, the $(-1)^n c((01)^n)$, and therefore their restrictions $\zeta^m(2^{\{n\}})$ to $M_{\dR}$, are $U_{\dR}$-invariant.
Transported from $M_{\dR}$ to $IU_{\dR}\times D$ by (7.9.1), the action of $u\in U_{\dR}$ is $(a,\lambda)\mapsto(I(u)a,\lambda)$.
Viewed as functions on $IU_{\dR}\times D$, the $\zeta^m(2^{\{n\}})$ are therefore inverse images of functions on $D$.
Up to a factor, there is only one such function in each even degree.
To determine which one is $\zeta^m(2^{\{n\}})$, it suffices to test on $dch$.
Set $\pi_m^2:=6\zeta^m(2)$, a definition suggested by the identity $\zeta(2)=\pi^2/6$, and $w_m^{2n}:=(\pi_m^2)^n$.
By 6.12, one has:

\paragraph{Lemma 7.11.}
(i) For each $n$,
\[
 \zeta^m(2^{\{n\}})=\pi_m^{2n}/(2n+1)!.
\]
(ii) $IU_{\dR}$ is the subscheme of $M_{\dR}$ defined by $\pi_m^2=0$.

\paragraph{Proposition 7.12.}
For each integer $n\geq1$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $\zeta^m(2n)$ is a rational multiple of $\pi_m^{2n}$ (which multiple is seen by evaluating on $dch$);
\item as a function on $IU_{\dR}\times D$, $\zeta^m(2n+1)$ is the inverse image of a function on $IU_{\dR}$, and this function is a nonzero homomorphism from $IU_{\dR}$ to $\Ga$;
\item on abelianized groups, the $\zeta^m(2n+1)$ for $n\geq1$ induce an isomorphism
\[
 U^{\ab}_{\dR}\xrightarrow{\sim}IU^{\ab}_{\dR}\xrightarrow{\sim}(\text{product of copies of }\Ga).
\]
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Proof (sketch).}
The depth filtration of $\Lie\Pi$ is the filtration by the subalgebras ``degree in $e_1\geq i$''.
Let $D_i\Pi$ be the corresponding subgroups.
If a subgroup $Q$ of $\Pi$, viewed as a subgroup of ${}_0\Pi_0$, is stable under $V_{\dR}$, it follows from (7.2.4) that it is also a subgroup of $(\Pi,\circ)$.
This applies to the $D_i\Pi$.

The subgroup $D_1\Pi$ is defined by the equation $c(0)=0$.
It contains the derived group $\Pi'$, which is defined by $c(0)=c(1)=0$, hence contains both $IU_{\dR}$ and $dch$.
The quotient $D_1\Pi/D_2\Pi$ is also the quotient $D_1\Pi/D_2\Pi$ for the group law $\circ$.
It is abelian, and the $c(0^{\{n-1\}}1)$ define an isomorphism of $D_1\Pi/D_2\Pi$ with a product of copies of $\Ga$.
Because $IU_{\dR}\subset(\Pi,\circ)$ has a Lie algebra generated in odd degrees, if $n$ is even, $c(0^{\{n-1\}}1)$ is zero on $IU_{\dR}$; $\zeta^m(n)$, transported by (7.9.1) into a function on $IU_{\dR}\times D$, is the inverse image of a function on $D$, and (i) is proved by the arguments of 7.10.
By (7.6.2), the $c(0^{\{n-1\}}1)$ are zero on $D$ for $n$ odd.
That $\zeta(n)\ne0$ ensures that, for $n$ odd and $n\geq3$, they are not zero on $IU_{\dR}$.
This proves (ii).
Assertion (iii) follows from (ii) and from the fact that the graded Lie algebra of $U_{\dR}$ has one generator in each odd degree $\geq3$.

\paragraph{Corollary 7.13.}
For all integers $a,b\geq0$, there exist rational numbers $\alpha_{a,b,r}$ ($1\leq r\leq a+b+1$), unique, such that
\begin{equation}
 \zeta^m(2^{\{b\}}3\,2^{\{a\}})=
 \sum_{r=1}^{a+b+1}\alpha_{a,b,r}\zeta^m(2r+1)\zeta^m(2^{\{a+b+1-r\}}).
\tag{7.13.1}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Proof.}
Regard, by (7.9.1), $\zeta^m(2^{\{b\}}3\,2^{\{a\}})$ as a function on $IU_{\dR}\times D$.
Expand it according to powers of a coordinate on $D$:
\[
 \zeta^m(2^{\{b\}}3\,2^{\{a\}})=\sum u_{2r+1}\pi_m^{2(a+b+1-r)},
\]
with $u_{2r+1}$ of codegree $2r+1$ on $IU_{\dR}$.
By 6.6 and 7.9, a left translate of $u_{2r+1}$ differs from $u_{2r+1}$ only by addition of a constant.
It is therefore a homomorphism from $IU_{\dR}$ to the additive group.
Being of weight $2r+1$, it is by 7.12 a rational multiple of $\zeta^m(2r+1)$, and 7.13 follows from 7.11.

\paragraph{Deus ex Machina 7.14 (D. Zagier [6]).}
Set
\[
 A^r_{a,b}=\binom{2r}{2a+2},\qquad
 B^r_{a,b}=(1-2^{-2r})\binom{2r}{2b+1}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}
 \zeta(2^{\{b\}}3\,2^{\{a\}})=2\sum (-1)^r(A^r_{ab}-B^r_{ab})\zeta(2r+1)\zeta(2^{\{a+b+1-r\}}).
\tag{7.14.1}
\end{equation}
This theorem suggests that the $\alpha_{a,b,r}$ of (7.13.1) are the $2(-1)^r(A^r_{a,b}-B^r_{a,b})$.
How to prove this starting from 7.14 will be explained in \S8.

\subsection*{7.15}
The affine algebra $O(U_{\dR})$ of $U_{\dR}$ is in duality, in the graded sense, with the enveloping algebra of $\Lie^{\mathrm{gr}}(U_{\dR})$.
This enveloping algebra is a free associative algebra in generators of degrees the odd integers $\geq3$.
The Poincare series of $O(U_{\dR})$, graded by the codegree, is therefore
\[
P(O(U_{\dR}),t)=\sum_{a\geq0}\left(\sum_{n\geq1}t^{2n+1}\right)^a
=\left(1-\sum_{n\geq1}t^{2n+1}\right)^{-1}
=(1-(t^3/(1-t^2)))^{-1}=\frac{1-t^2}{1-t^2-t^3}.
\]
If the affine algebra of the additive group $\Ga$, with coordinate $\lambda$, is graded by giving $\lambda$ codegree $2$, one has $P(O(\Ga),t)=(1-t^2)^{-1}$ and
\[
 P(O(U_{\dR}\times\Ga),t)=(1-t^2-t^3)^{-1}.
\]
The affine algebra $O(IU_{\dR})$ is a graded subalgebra of $O(U_{\dR})$.
By 7.9, one therefore has:

\paragraph{Proposition 7.16.}
The Poincare series of $O(M_{\dR})$, graded by the codegree, satisfies
\begin{equation}
 P(O(M_{\dR}),t)\leq(1-t^2-t^3)^{-1}
\tag{7.16.1}
\end{equation}
(coefficientwise inequality), with equality if and only if the morphism $I:U_{\dR}\to IU_{\dR}$ is an isomorphism, i.e. if $U_{\dR}$ acts faithfully on $\pi_1(X;1,0)_{\dR}$.

Comparing with 6.10, one obtains:

\paragraph{Theorem 7.17.}
(i) $U_{\dR}$ acts faithfully on ${}_1\Pi_0$.

(ii) The morphism of $\Q$-vector spaces $\widetilde H(2,3)\to O(M_{\dR})$ is an isomorphism.

The affine algebra of $\pi_1(X;1,0)_{\mot}$ contains a $\Q(-1)$ as a subquotient.
It follows therefore from (i) that the action of $G_{\dR}$ on the affine algebra of $\pi_1(X;1,0)_{\dR}$ is faithful.
The theory of Tannakian categories gives, as a consequence of (i):

\paragraph{Corollary 7.18.}
The affine algebra $O(\pi_1(X;1,0)_{\mot})$, which is an Ind-object of $MT(\Z)$, generates the Tannakian category $MT(\Z)$.

More precisely, every mixed Tate motive with good reduction over $\Spec(\Z)$ is deduced from subobjects in $MT(\Z)$ of $O(\pi_1(X;1,0)_{\mot})$ by the operations of tensor products, direct sums, duals, and subquotients.

Assertion (ii) of 7.17 is equivalent to saying that the $\zeta^m(s)$ with $s_i\in\{2,3\}$ form a basis of $O(M_{\dR})$.

\section*{8. Sketches of proofs}

\subsection*{8.1}
The group scheme $V_{\dR}=(\Pi,\circ)$ acts on ${}_1\Pi_0$ (7.1), and therefore on its affine algebra $O({}_1\Pi_0)$.
This action is the action of $(\Pi,\circ)$ on $O(\Pi)$ by left translations (7.2.3): $x$ transforms $f$ into $y\mapsto f(x^{-1}\circ y)$.
These actions are given by (7.2.1), (7.2.2).
Differentiating these formulae, one obtains the action of $\Lie(V_{\dR})=(\Lie\Pi,\{,\})$ on $O({}_1\Pi_0)$, and dually the coaction of the Lie coalgebra.
The latter is $m/m^2$, for $m$ the ideal of functions vanishing at the identity element $1$, and the coaction is the projection onto $m/m^2\otimes O(\Pi)$ of
\[
 f\longmapsto f(x^{-1}\circ y)-f(y)
\]
in $m\otimes O(\Pi)\subset O(\Pi\times\Pi)$.

Goncharov gave a convenient formula for this coaction (see [5] Theorem 1.2).
For $w$ a word in the alphabet $\{0,1\}$, consider the subwords $cId$ of $1w0$, with $I$ nonempty: one considers all decompositions $w=w'Iw''$ of $w$, with $I$ nonempty, and $c,d$ are the letters adjacent to $I$ in $1w0$.
For each decomposition, put $w\setminus I:=w'w''$.
Formula for the coaction:
\begin{equation}
 c(w)\longmapsto -\sum p_{cd}(I)\otimes c(w\setminus I)
\tag{8.1.1}
\end{equation}
where $p_{cd}(I)$ is the projection in $m/m^2$ of the following element of $m\subset O(\Pi)$:
\begin{equation}
 p_{cd}(I)=\text{projection of }
 \begin{cases}
 0,& c=d,\\
 c(I),& c=1,\ d=0,\\
 \text{the antipode }c(I)^*\text{ of }c(I),& c=0,\ d=1,
 \end{cases}
\tag{8.1.2}
\end{equation}
where, if $I=a_1\cdots a_k$, the antipode is $(-1)^kc(a_k\cdots a_1)$.

\subsection*{8.2}
The action of $U_{\dR}$ on $O({}_1\Pi_0)$ factors through that of $IU_{\dR}\subset(\Pi,\circ)$, and to obtain the coaction of the Lie coalgebra it remains to compose (8.1.1) with
\[
 \operatorname{coLie}(\Pi,\circ)\longrightarrow \operatorname{coLie}(IU_{\dR})\longrightarrow \operatorname{coLie}(U_{\dR}).
\]
The $c(I)$ and $c(I)^*$ of (8.1.2) matter no longer except by their restriction to $M_{\dR}$, taken modulo $\pi_m^2$ and the square of the maximal ideal at $1$.

We shall choose the generators $\nu_{2r+1}$ of the Lie algebra $\Lie^{\mathrm{gr}}(U_{\dR})$ so that, writing again $\nu_{2r+1}$ for the image of $\nu_{2r+1}$ in the tangent space of $IU_{\dR}\subset\Pi$ at $1$, one has
\[
 \langle \nu_{2r+1},d\zeta^m(2r+1)\rangle=1
\]
(possible by 7.12(ii)).
This determines the image of $\nu_{2r+1}$ in $\Lie^{\mathrm{gr}}(U^{\ab}_{\dR})$.
If $r=a+b+1$ and $\alpha_{a,b}$ is the coefficient of $\zeta^m(2r+1)$ in (7.13.1), then
\[
 \left\langle \nu_{2r+1},d\zeta^m(2^{\{b\}}3\,2^{\{a\}})\right\rangle=\alpha_{a,b}.
\]

\paragraph{Proposition 8.3.}
Formula (7.14.1) remains true with $\zeta$ replaced by $\zeta^m$.

\paragraph{Proof.}
Proceed by induction on $a+b$.
If $a+b=0$, the identity $(7.14.1)^m$ to be proved reduces to $\zeta^m(3)=\zeta^m(3)$.
Suppose $(7.14.1)^m$ true for $a+b<N$.
This ensures that, for $a+b<N$ and $r=a+b+1$, one has, with the notation of 7.14,
\begin{equation}
 \zeta^m(2^{\{b\}}3\,2^{\{a\}})=2(-1)^r(A^r_{ab}-B^r_{ab})\zeta^m(2r+1)\quad\text{on }IU_{\dR}.
\tag{8.3.1}
\end{equation}
For $a+b=N$, if the $\alpha_{a,b,r}$ are defined as in 7.13, one has, for the action of $\nu_{2r+1}$,
\begin{equation}
 \nu_{2r+1}\zeta^m(2^{\{b\}}3\,2^{\{a\}})
 =-\alpha_{a,b,r}\zeta^m(2^{\{a+b+1-r\}}):
\tag{8.3.2}
\end{equation}
apply 7.12.
For $r\leq N$, compute the left-hand side by (8.1.1), (8.1.2).
One must sum over the subwords $I$ of $w=(01)^b(001)(01)^a$, of length $2r+1$, and surrounded by $0,1$ or $1,0$ in $1w0$.
The $c(w\setminus I)$, restricted to $M_{\dR}$, are $\pm\zeta^m(2^{\{a+b+1-r\}})$.
The $c(I)$ or $c(I)^*$ that appear in (8.1.2), restricted to $M_{\dR}$, are $\pm\zeta^m(2^{\{b'\}}3\,2^{\{a'\}})$ to which (8.3.1) applies by the induction hypothesis, or a $c((01)^m0)$ which, on $\Pi'$, is expressed by (3.5.2) as a sum of $\zeta(2^{\{b'\}}3\,2^{\{a'\}})$ to which (8.3.1) applies.
This permits the computation of the $\alpha_{a,b,r}$ for $r\leq N$, and one finds that they have the expected value.
This leaves unknown in (7.13.1) only the coefficient $\alpha_{a,b,a+b+1}$.
It is determined by evaluating at $dch$ and applying (7.14.1).

\subsection*{8.4}
Armed with (8.3.1), one can now compute the morphisms (6.7.1) by (8.1.1), (8.1.2).
To prove 6.8, the idea is that, in the matrix obtained for (6.7.2), the dominant terms, in the $2$-adic sense, come from the $2^{-2r}$ which appears in $B^r_{ab}$ (7.14).
If, in the target and source of (6.7.2), one orders the basis vectors in suitable orders which correspond by the bijection defined in 6.7, and if in the target one multiplies the basis vectors by suitable powers of $2$, the matrix of (6.7.2) is a square matrix with integer coefficients whose reduction modulo $2$ is triangular with $1$'s on the diagonal.
This ensures that it is invertible.
