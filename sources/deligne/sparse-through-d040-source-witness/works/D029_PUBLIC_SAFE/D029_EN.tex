\noindent GROUP THEORY. — Non-residually finite central extensions of arithmetic groups. Note (*) by Mr. Pierre Deligne, Member of the Academy.\par
\smallskip
\noindent ⟦English translation of the French abstract⟧\par
\noindent A variant of known methods for treating the congruence subgroup problem gives results such as the following: for n \ensuremath{≥} 2, the group \ensuremath{\widetilde{Sp}}(2n, \ensuremath{ℤ}), the inverse image of Sp(2n, \ensuremath{ℤ}) in the universal covering of Sp(2n, \ensuremath{ℝ}) (a central extension of Sp(2n, \ensuremath{ℤ}) by \ensuremath{π₁} Sp(2n, \ensuremath{ℝ}) = \ensuremath{ℤ}), is not Hausdorff for the topology defined by finite-index subgroups. More precisely, every finite-index subgroup of \ensuremath{\widetilde{Sp}}(2n, \ensuremath{ℤ}) contains 2\ensuremath{π₁} Sp(2n, \ensuremath{ℝ}).\par
\smallskip
\noindent ⟦English abstract printed in the 1978 source, retained as source matter⟧\par
\noindent A variant of known methods to handle the congruence subgroup problem yields results like the following. Let \ensuremath{\widetilde{Sp}}(2n, \ensuremath{ℤ}) be the inverse image of Sp(2n, \ensuremath{ℤ}) in the universal covering of Sp(2n, \ensuremath{ℝ}); it is a central extension of Sp(2n, \ensuremath{ℤ}) by \ensuremath{π₁} Sp(2n, \ensuremath{ℝ}) \ensuremath{≃} \ensuremath{ℤ}. If n \ensuremath{≥} 2, the group \ensuremath{\widetilde{Sp}}(2n, \ensuremath{ℤ}) is not residually finite : any subgroup of finite index contains 2\ensuremath{π₁} Sp(2n, \ensuremath{ℝ}).\par
\smallskip
\noindent 1. S-ARITHMETIC GROUPS. — Let G be an almost simple, simply connected group isotropic over a global field F. To simplify the statements, we shall also suppose that G(F) is perfect (i.e. equal to its commutator subgroup); this is the case except, perhaps, when G is a trialitarian form of D\ensuremath{₄}. Let S also be a finite set of places of F containing all the infinite places, and suppose that\par
\smallskip
\noindent (i)    \ensuremath{∑_{v∈S}} rank(G/\ensuremath{F_v}) \ensuremath{≥} 2.\par
\smallskip
\noindent For each place v of F, put \ensuremath{G_v} = G(\ensuremath{F_v}). For each finite set T of places, likewise put \ensuremath{G_T} = \ensuremath{∏_{v∈T}} \ensuremath{G_v} and \ensuremath{G_{T′}} = \ensuremath{∏_{v∉T}} \ensuremath{G_v} (restricted product): for \ensuremath{𝔸}, the ring of adèles of F, one has G(\ensuremath{𝔸}) = \ensuremath{G_T} \ensuremath{×} \ensuremath{G_{T′}}.\par
\smallskip
\noindent A subgroup of G(F) is called an S-congruence subgroup if it is the inverse image of a compact open subgroup of \ensuremath{G_{S′}}. It is called S-arithmetic if it is commensurable with an S-congruence subgroup, i.e. if it contains, with finite index, a subgroup of finite index in an S-congruence group. The S-congruence subgroups (respectively, S-arithmetic subgroups) form a neighborhood basis of the identity for a topology (of a topological group) \ensuremath{𝒯}(c) [respectively, \ensuremath{𝒯}(a)] on G(F). For a fixed S-congruence subgroup \ensuremath{Γ}, one still obtains a neighborhood basis by considering only the S-congruence subgroups normal in \ensuremath{Γ} (respectively, only the normal finite-index subgroups of \ensuremath{Γ}). This ensures the existence of the completion \ensuremath{\widehat{G(F)}}(c) [respectively, \ensuremath{\widehat{G(F)}}(a)] of G(F) for \ensuremath{𝒯}(c) [respectively, \ensuremath{𝒯}(a)]. The topology \ensuremath{𝒯}(c) is the topology induced from that of \ensuremath{G_{S′}}. Since G(F) is dense in \ensuremath{G_{S′}} (the strong approximation theorem), one has \ensuremath{\widehat{G(F)}}(c) = \ensuremath{G_{S′}}. The completion \ensuremath{\widehat{G(F)}}(a) of G(F) is an extension of \ensuremath{\widehat{G(F)}}(c) = \ensuremath{G_{S′}} by the profinite group C(S, G) = lim proj \ensuremath{Γ}/\ensuremath{Δ} (the projective limit over pairs \ensuremath{Δ} \ensuremath{⊂} \ensuremath{Γ}, with \ensuremath{Δ} normal of finite index in \ensuremath{Γ} and \ensuremath{Γ} an S-congruence group).\par
\smallskip
\noindent For G of rank \ensuremath{≥} 2, Raghunathan (1) proved that:\par
\smallskip
\noindent (ii)    \ensuremath{\widehat{G(F)}}(a) is a central extension of \ensuremath{\widehat{G(F)}}(c) = \ensuremath{G_{S′}},\par
\smallskip
\noindent and he completes the verification that the same conclusion holds under hypothesis (i) alone.\par
\smallskip
\noindent Let \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_S} be a topological central extension of \ensuremath{G_S} by a discrete group A, and let \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(F)}} be the inverse image of G(F) in \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_S}. A subgroup of \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(F)}} will be called an S-congruence subgroup if it is the inverse image of an S-congruence subgroup of G(F), and S-arithmetic if it is commensurable with an S-congruence subgroup. We again denote the corresponding topologies by \ensuremath{𝒯}(c) and \ensuremath{𝒯}(a), and the separated completions by \ensuremath{\widehat{\widetilde{G(F)}}}(c), \ensuremath{\widehat{\widetilde{G(F)}}}(a), for these\par

\noindent topologies. One again has \ensuremath{\widehat{\widetilde{G(F)}}}(c) = \ensuremath{G_{S′}}, while \ensuremath{\widehat{\widetilde{G(F)}}}(a) is an extension of \ensuremath{G_{S′}} by a profinite group \ensuremath{\widetilde{C}}(S, G), itself an extension of C(S, G) by\par
\smallskip
\noindent \ensuremath{\widehat{A}} = lim proj A/(A \ensuremath{∩} \ensuremath{Γ})    (\ensuremath{Γ} S-arithmetic).\par
\smallskip
\noindent PROPOSITION 1. — If condition (ii) holds, \ensuremath{\widehat{\widetilde{G(F)}}}(a) is a central extension of \ensuremath{\widehat{\widetilde{G(F)}}}(c) = \ensuremath{G_{S′}}.\par
\smallskip
\noindent Recall that if a group \ensuremath{\widetilde{H}} is a central extension of a group H, the commutator map \ensuremath{\widetilde{H}} \ensuremath{×} \ensuremath{\widetilde{H}} \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{\widetilde{H}} factors through a map, again denoted ( , ) : H \ensuremath{×} H \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{\widetilde{H}}. In particular, since \ensuremath{\widehat{\widetilde{G(F)}}}(a) is a central extension of \ensuremath{\widehat{G(F)}}(a) by \ensuremath{\widehat{A}}, the commutator map \ensuremath{\widetilde{C}}(S, G) \ensuremath{×} \ensuremath{\widehat{\widetilde{G(F)}}}(a) \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{\widehat{\widetilde{G(F)}}}(a) factors through C(S, G) \ensuremath{×} \ensuremath{\widehat{G(F)}}(a). By (ii), it factors through a bimultiplicative map\par
\smallskip
\noindent C(S, G) \ensuremath{×} \ensuremath{\widehat{G(F)}}(a) \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{\widehat{A}};\par
\smallskip
\noindent since G(F) is perfect and dense in \ensuremath{\widehat{G(F)}}(a), this map is trivial.\par
\smallskip
\noindent More concretely, the proposition means that for every S-arithmetic subgroup \ensuremath{Δ} one has:\par
\smallskip
\noindent (1 a) For every conjugate \ensuremath{Δ^g} of \ensuremath{Δ} (g \ensuremath{∈} \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(F)}}), there exists an S-congruence group \ensuremath{Γ} such that \ensuremath{Γ} \ensuremath{∩} \ensuremath{Δ} = \ensuremath{Γ} \ensuremath{∩} \ensuremath{Δ^g}.\par
\smallskip
\noindent (1 b) \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(F)}} acts trivially on the finite group \ensuremath{C_Δ} = lim proj \ensuremath{Γ}/(\ensuremath{Γ} \ensuremath{∩} \ensuremath{Δ}).\par
\smallskip
\noindent These conditions are also equivalent to:\par
\smallskip
\noindent (1 c) For every g \ensuremath{∈} G(F), there exists an S-congruence group \ensuremath{Γ} such that (g, \ensuremath{Γ}) \ensuremath{⊂} \ensuremath{Δ}.\par
\smallskip
\noindent One has \ensuremath{\widetilde{C}}(S, G) = lim proj \ensuremath{C_Δ}.\par
\smallskip
\noindent 2. CENTRAL EXTENSIONS OF ADELIC GROUPS. — From now on we shall suppose that:\par
\smallskip
\noindent (iii) the group \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_S} is perfect.\par
\smallskip
\noindent The most interesting case would be that in which \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_S} is the universal central extension of \ensuremath{G_S} by a discrete group (the “universal covering” of \ensuremath{G_S}), but we do not wish to assume the existence of such a universal extension.\par
\smallskip
\noindent A topological central extension E of G(\ensuremath{𝔸}) by a discrete group X is determined by the extensions \ensuremath{E_S} and \ensuremath{E_{S′}} of \ensuremath{G_S} and \ensuremath{G_{S′}} by X, obtained as the inverse images of \ensuremath{G_S} and \ensuremath{G_{S′}} in E: because \ensuremath{G_S} is perfect, one has\par
\smallskip
\noindent E  \ensuremath{←}\textasciitilde{}  (\ensuremath{E_S} \ensuremath{×} \ensuremath{E_{S′}})/\{(x, \ensuremath{−}x) | x \ensuremath{∈} X\}.\par
\smallskip
\noindent If the extension \ensuremath{E_S} is dominated by \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_S}, i.e. is obtained from \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_S} by pushout along f : A \ensuremath{→} X, then f is unique, and the data of E amount to the data of the extension \ensuremath{E_{S′}} together with f (an equivalence of categories); one has\par
\smallskip
\noindent E  \ensuremath{←}\textasciitilde{}  \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_S} \ensuremath{×} \ensuremath{E_{S′}}/\{(a, \ensuremath{−}f(a)) | a \ensuremath{∈} A\}.\par
\smallskip
\noindent Let \ensuremath{ℒ} be the category of topological central extensions of G(\ensuremath{𝔸}) by a finite group, split over G(F), and with \ensuremath{E_S} dominated by \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_S}:\par
\smallskip
\noindent 1 \ensuremath{→} X \ensuremath{→} E \ensuremath{→} G(\ensuremath{𝔸}) \ensuremath{→} 1\par
\noindent           \ensuremath{↖}s    \ensuremath{↑}\par
\noindent              G(F)\par
\smallskip
\noindent It would amount to the same thing to require “splittable over G(F)”: since G(F) is perfect, the splitting, if it exists, is unique. The category \ensuremath{ℒ} is filtered on the left. For (E, s) in \ensuremath{ℒ}, the closure in E of (image of \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_S})·sG(F) maps onto G(\ensuremath{𝔸}): its image is closed, because X is finite, and it contains \ensuremath{G_S}·G(F), which is dense (strong approximation). The subcategory L of \ensuremath{ℒ} consisting of the pairs (E, s) for which (image of \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_S})·sG(F) is dense in E is therefore cofinal; since there is at most one arrow between any two objects of L, it reduces to a filtered ordered set.\par

\noindent PROPOSITION 2. — Under hypotheses (ii) and (iii), we construct a natural isomorphism between \ensuremath{\widetilde{C}}(S, G) and the projective limit over \ensuremath{ℒ}, or L, lim proj X. Under this isomorphism, the natural map from A to \ensuremath{\widetilde{C}}(S, G) is the limit of the maps \ensuremath{−}f : A \ensuremath{→} X, where f is such that \ensuremath{E_S} is obtained from \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_S} by f.\par
\smallskip
\noindent Let E be a topological central extension of G(\ensuremath{𝔸}) by a discrete group X. Giving a splitting s of E over G(F) amounts to giving an isomorphism between the extension of G(F) by X induced from \ensuremath{E_S} and the opposite of the extension induced from \ensuremath{E_{S′}}. When \ensuremath{E_S} is dominated by \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_S} and f is as above, this amounts to giving a commutative diagram\par
\smallskip
\noindent 1 \ensuremath{→} X \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{E_{S′}} \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{G_{S′}} \ensuremath{→} 1\par
\noindent     \ensuremath{↑−}f    \ensuremath{↑}t         \ensuremath{↑}\par
\noindent 1 \ensuremath{→} A \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(F)}} \ensuremath{→} G(F) \ensuremath{→} 1.\par
\smallskip
\noindent Hence there is an equivalence between the category \ensuremath{ℒ} and the category \ensuremath{ℒ′} of commutative diagrams\par
\smallskip
\noindent (1)    1 \ensuremath{→} X \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{E_{S′}} —q\ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{G_{S′}} \ensuremath{→} 1\par
\noindent                  \ensuremath{↑}t          \ensuremath{↑}\par
\noindent               \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(F)}} \ensuremath{→} G(F)\par
\smallskip
\noindent (where \ensuremath{E_{S′}} is a topological central extension of \ensuremath{G_{S′}} by a finite group): E is reconstructed as \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_S} \ensuremath{×} \ensuremath{E_{S′}}/\{(a, ta) | a \ensuremath{∈} A\}, and the splitting over G(F) is given by s(g) = (\ensuremath{\widetilde{g}}, t\ensuremath{\widetilde{g}}) modulo the elements (a, ta), for \ensuremath{\widetilde{g}} \ensuremath{∈} \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(F)}} \ensuremath{⊂} \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_S} mapping to g in G(F). The subcategory L of \ensuremath{ℒ} corresponds to the subcategory L\ensuremath{′} of diagrams (1) in which t has dense image.\par
\smallskip
\noindent Let (\ensuremath{E_{S′}}, t) be in L\ensuremath{′}, and let \ensuremath{𝒯} be the topology on \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(F)}} induced by that of \ensuremath{E_{S′}}. If K is a compact open subgroup of \ensuremath{E_{S′}} disjoint from X, the intersections of \ensuremath{Δ} = \ensuremath{t^{-1}}K with the S-congruence subgroups form a neighborhood basis for \ensuremath{𝒯}. For \ensuremath{Γ} an S-congruence subgroup defined by a compact open subgroup K\ensuremath{₁} \ensuremath{⊂} qK, one furthermore has\par
\smallskip
\noindent (2)    X  \ensuremath{→}\textasciitilde{}  XK/K  \ensuremath{←}\textasciitilde{}  \ensuremath{q^{-1}}K\ensuremath{₁}/(K \ensuremath{∩} \ensuremath{q^{-1}}K\ensuremath{₁})  \ensuremath{←}\textasciitilde{}  \ensuremath{Γ}/(\ensuremath{Δ} \ensuremath{∩} \ensuremath{Γ})  \ensuremath{←}\textasciitilde{}  \ensuremath{C_Δ}.\par
\smallskip
\noindent Since X is finite, \ensuremath{Δ} is S-arithmetic. Conversely, Proposition 1 ensures that a neighborhood basis of S-arithmetic groups is obtained in this way: the filtered ordered set L\ensuremath{′} identifies with the set of germs (for the S-congruence topology) of S-arithmetic groups, and the isomorphism of Proposition 2 is defined as the projective limit of the isomorphisms (2).\par
\smallskip
\noindent 3. QUASI-SPLIT GROUPS. — Let \ensuremath{μ} be the group of roots of unity in F and; for every non-complex place v of F, let \ensuremath{μ_v} be the group of roots of unity in \ensuremath{F_v}. For complex v, put \ensuremath{μ_v} = \{1\}. Deodhar proved that, for G quasi-split, there exists a universal topological central extension \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_v} of \ensuremath{G_v} by a discrete group (the “universal covering”), and that for v non-real its kernel \ensuremath{π₁}(\ensuremath{G_v}) is canonically a quotient of \ensuremath{μ_v}. I completed this result by proving that in fact \ensuremath{π₁}(\ensuremath{G_v}) = \ensuremath{μ_v}. For real v, one has \ensuremath{π₁}(\ensuremath{G_v}) = \ensuremath{ℤ} or \ensuremath{ℤ}/2. In every case, \ensuremath{μ_v} occurs as a quotient of \ensuremath{π₁}(\ensuremath{G_v}).\par
\smallskip
\noindent For almost every v, the extension \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_v} splits over the natural maximal compact subgroup of \ensuremath{G_v}. This makes it possible to define the restricted product of the \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_v}, an extension of G(\ensuremath{𝔸}) by the direct sum of the \ensuremath{π₁}(\ensuremath{G_v}). Push it out by\par
\smallskip
\noindent R : \ensuremath{⊕_v} \ensuremath{π₁}(\ensuremath{G_v}) \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{⊕_v} \ensuremath{μ_v} —\ensuremath{σ→} \ensuremath{μ} : \ensuremath{σ}((\ensuremath{x_v})) = \ensuremath{∏_v} \ensuremath{x_v^{[μ_v:μ]}}.\par
\clearpage

\noindent This gives a central extension of G(\ensuremath{𝔸}) by \ensuremath{μ}, and it follows from Deodhar (2) and C. Moore (3) that this is the universal topological central extension of G(\ensuremath{𝔸}) by a discrete group, split over G(F):\par
\smallskip
\noindent (3)    1 \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{μ} \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(𝔸)}} \ensuremath{→} G(\ensuremath{𝔸}) \ensuremath{→} 1\par
\noindent                          \ensuremath{↖}          \ensuremath{↑}\par
\noindent                               G(F)\par
\smallskip
\noindent Take \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_S} to be the universal covering of \ensuremath{G_S}, and let \ensuremath{R_S} be the restriction of R to \ensuremath{π₁}\ensuremath{G_S} = \ensuremath{∏_{v∈S}} \ensuremath{π₁}(\ensuremath{G_v}). Proposition 2 identifies the completion \ensuremath{\widehat{\widetilde{G(F)}}}(a) of \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(F)}} with the inverse image \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(𝔸)}_{S′}} of \ensuremath{G_{S′}} = \ensuremath{\widehat{\widetilde{G(F)}}}(c) in (3), and gives an exact commutative diagram\par
\smallskip
\noindent (4)    1 \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{μ} \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(𝔸)}_{S′}} \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{G_{S′}} \ensuremath{→} 1\par
\noindent              \ensuremath{↑−}\ensuremath{R_S}          \ensuremath{↑}i             \ensuremath{↑}\par
\noindent        \ensuremath{∏_{v∈S}} \ensuremath{π₁}(\ensuremath{G_v}) \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{\widehat{\widetilde{G(F)}}}(a) \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{\widehat{G(F)}}(a) \ensuremath{→} 1\par
\smallskip
\noindent In particular:\par
\smallskip
\noindent THEOREM. — Let G/F be quasi-split, and let \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(F)}} be the inverse image of G(F) in the universal covering of \ensuremath{G_S}. If \ensuremath{∑_{v∈S}} rank(\ensuremath{G_v}) \ensuremath{≥} 2, the intersection of the S-arithmetic subgroups of \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(F)}} is the kernel of \ensuremath{R_S} : \ensuremath{∏_{v∈S}} \ensuremath{π₁}(\ensuremath{G_v}) \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{μ}.\par
\smallskip
\noindent For Sp(2n, \ensuremath{ℚ}) and S = \ensuremath{∞}, this is the result announced in the introduction. For SL(n, K), with K real quadratic and S = \ensuremath{∞}, the resulting statement generalizes that of J. Millson (4).\par
\smallskip
\noindent PROBLEM. — Does every discrete finite-covolume subgroup of the universal covering of Sp(2n, \ensuremath{ℝ}) (n \ensuremath{≥} 2) contain 2\ensuremath{π₁} Sp(2n, \ensuremath{ℝ})? (5).\par
\smallskip
\noindent 4. THE SYSTEM OF FACTORS OF THE \ensuremath{θ} FUNCTIONS. — When condition (i) is not satisfied, Proposition 1 is no longer available; it nevertheless remains possible to study S-arithmetic subgroups satisfying a condition of type (1 a).\par
\smallskip
\noindent Let S therefore be a nonempty finite set of places containing all the infinite places, and let T \ensuremath{⊃} S be such that (G, T) satisfies condition (ii). The interesting case is that in which S consists of a single place at which G has rank 1. Let \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_S} again be a topological central extension of \ensuremath{G_S} by a discrete group, and let \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(F)}} be the inverse image of G(F) in \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_S}. For an S-arithmetic subgroup \ensuremath{Δ} of \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(F)}}, consider the condition:\par
\smallskip
\noindent (\ensuremath{\star}) there exists a T-arithmetic subgroup U of \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(F)}} such that, for every conjugate \ensuremath{Δ^g} of \ensuremath{Δ} (g \ensuremath{∈} U), there exists an S-congruence group \ensuremath{Γ} for which \ensuremath{Γ} \ensuremath{∩} \ensuremath{Δ} = \ensuremath{Γ} \ensuremath{∩} \ensuremath{Δ^g} (U-invariance of the germ of \ensuremath{Δ}).\par
\smallskip
\noindent PROPOSITION 3. — The completion \ensuremath{\widehat{\widetilde{G(F)}}}(a*) of \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(F)}} for the topology of S-arithmetic subgroups satisfying (\ensuremath{\star}) is a central extension of \ensuremath{\widehat{\widetilde{G(F)}}}(c) = \ensuremath{G_{S′}}.\par
\smallskip
\noindent More concretely, we must verify that if (U, \ensuremath{Δ}) satisfies (\ensuremath{\star}), then \ensuremath{Δ} satisfies (1 c). To prove this, we may first shrink U and \ensuremath{Δ}. In particular, we may suppose that \ensuremath{Δ} \ensuremath{⊂} U (replacing \ensuremath{Δ} by \ensuremath{Δ} \ensuremath{∩} U). The group U then acts by conjugation on the\par
\clearpage
\phantomsection\hypertarget{authority-page-0005}{}
\section*{Authority page 5 --- printed page 207}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Authority page 5 --- printed page 207}
\noindent finite group \ensuremath{C^U_Δ} = lim proj (\ensuremath{Γ} \ensuremath{∩} U)/(\ensuremath{Γ} \ensuremath{∩} \ensuremath{Δ}) (\ensuremath{Γ} S-congruence); replacing U by the kernel of this action and \ensuremath{Δ} by some \ensuremath{Δ} \ensuremath{∩} \ensuremath{Γ}, we may further suppose that U acts trivially on \ensuremath{C^U_Δ}.\par
\smallskip
\noindent Since U is T-arithmetic, its completion U(c) \ensuremath{⊂} \ensuremath{G_{S′}} has finite index in a subgroup \ensuremath{G_{T−S}} \ensuremath{×} L, with L \ensuremath{⊂} \ensuremath{G_{T′}} compact open; since \ensuremath{G_{T−S}} has no open subgroup of finite index, U(c) itself has the form \ensuremath{G_{T−S}} \ensuremath{×} L. Condition (\ensuremath{\star}) ensures that the subgroups \ensuremath{Δ} \ensuremath{∩} \ensuremath{Γ} (\ensuremath{Γ} S-congruence) form a neighborhood basis of the identity for a group topology on U. The corresponding completion U(\ensuremath{Δ}) is an extension of U(c) by \ensuremath{C^U_Δ}—and hence a central extension of \ensuremath{G_{T−S}} \ensuremath{×} L. Replace L by an open subgroup L\ensuremath{₁} over which this extension is trivial, and replace U and \ensuremath{Δ} by the inverse images of L\ensuremath{₁}. Since \ensuremath{G_{T−S}} is perfect, a central extension of \ensuremath{G_{T−S}} \ensuremath{×} L is determined by its restrictions to the two factors; this reduces us to the case in which the central extension U(\ensuremath{Δ}) of \ensuremath{G_{T−S}} \ensuremath{×} L is the inverse image of a central extension \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_{T−S}} of \ensuremath{G_{T−S}} by \ensuremath{C^U_Δ}. By construction, U lifts to \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_{T−S}}, and \ensuremath{Δ} is the inverse image of a compact open subgroup K of \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_{T−S}} \ensuremath{×} L:\par
\smallskip
\noindent (5)              K \ensuremath{↪} \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_{T−S}} \ensuremath{×} L\par
\noindent                  \ensuremath{↑}                 \ensuremath{↑}\par
\noindent                  \ensuremath{Δ} \ensuremath{\text{---}\text{---}\text{---}\text{---}\text{---}\text{---}\text{---}\text{---}\text{---}\text{---}\text{---}\text{---}\text{---}→} U\par
\smallskip
\noindent Let \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(F)}_T} be the central extension of G(F) obtained as the inverse image of G(F) in \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_T} = \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_S} \ensuremath{×} \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_{T−S}}, and let \ensuremath{\widetilde{U}} be the lifting (5) of U in \ensuremath{\widetilde{G(F)}_T}. For g \ensuremath{∈} G(F), Proposition 1 gives (g, \ensuremath{Γ_T}) \ensuremath{⊂} \ensuremath{\widetilde{U}} for a T-congruence group \ensuremath{Γ_T} defined by a sufficiently small L\ensuremath{′} \ensuremath{⊂} L. If the S-congruence subgroup \ensuremath{Γ} defined by K\ensuremath{′} \ensuremath{⊂} \ensuremath{G_{T−S}} \ensuremath{×} L\ensuremath{′}, whose inverse image in \ensuremath{\widetilde{G}_{T−S}} \ensuremath{×} L\ensuremath{′} is K\ensuremath{″}, is small enough that (g, K\ensuremath{′}) \ensuremath{⊂} K, then (g, \ensuremath{Γ}) \ensuremath{⊂} \ensuremath{Δ}, which verifies (1 c).\par
\smallskip
\noindent The kernel \ensuremath{\widetilde{C}^{*}}(S, G) = Ker(\ensuremath{\widehat{\widetilde{G(F)}}}(a*) \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{\widehat{G(F)}}(c)) is still described by Proposition 2 (with the same proof), and the calculation can be carried through in the quasi-split case (cf. §3). Here is an application, with G = SL(2, \ensuremath{ℚ}), S = \{\ensuremath{∞}\}, and T = \{\ensuremath{∞}, p\}. Let \ensuremath{\widetilde{SL(2, ℝ)}} be the double covering of SL(2, \ensuremath{ℝ}). The factor systems of the \ensuremath{θ} functions, restricted to arbitrarily small congruence subgroups of SL(2, \ensuremath{ℤ}), may be interpreted as defining a germ \ensuremath{τ} (for the congruence-subgroup topology) of liftings of SL(2, \ensuremath{ℤ}) into \ensuremath{\widetilde{SL(2, ℝ)}}. This germ is invariant under GL(2, \ensuremath{ℚ}). If a modular form of half-integral weight k/2 is defined to be a function on the Poincaré half-plane which, under the action of a congruence subgroup of SL(2, \ensuremath{ℤ}), transforms like the \ensuremath{θ} function of a quadratic form in k variables, this amounts to saying that if\par

\noindent (a  b)\par
\noindent (c  d) \ensuremath{∈} GL(2, \ensuremath{ℚ})\par
\smallskip
\noindent has positive determinant and f is modular of weight k/2, then f(\ensuremath{−}\ensuremath{z^{-1}}) and \ensuremath{(cz + d)^{k/2}} f[(az + b)/(cz + d)] are likewise modular. Conversely:\par
\smallskip
\noindent PROPOSITION 4. — For every prime p, the germ \ensuremath{τ} is the unique germ of liftings of SL(2, \ensuremath{ℤ}) into \ensuremath{\widetilde{SL(2, ℝ)}} that is invariant under a finite-index subgroup of SL(2, \ensuremath{ℤ}[1/p]).\par
\smallskip
\noindent The arguments leading to the theorem of §3 show that every arithmetic subgroup of SL(2, \ensuremath{ℤ}) whose germ is invariant under a finite-index subgroup of SL(2, \ensuremath{ℤ}[1/p]) is a congruence subgroup.\par
