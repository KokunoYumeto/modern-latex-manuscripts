\section*{Introduction}

\paragraph{\textbf{Theorem 1.}}
Let $C \subset \PP^2(\CC)$ be a plane curve whose only singularities are ordinary double points with distinct tangents. Then the fundamental group of $\PP^2(\CC)\setminus C$ is abelian.

This theorem was stated by Zariski in 1929~[8]; see also~[9, Chapter~VIII]. His proof used Severi's theorem asserting that the space of irreducible plane curves of fixed degree with a prescribed number of double points is irreducible (\emph{Vorlesungen \"uber algebraische Geometrie}, 1921, Appendix~F). It is still unknown whether Severi's assertion is true.

The group $\pi_1$ appearing in the theorem is the ordinary topological fundamental group. If one replaces $\CC$ by an arbitrary algebraically closed field $k$ of characteristic $0$, the same statement holds for the algebraic fundamental group classifying finite \'etale coverings: by the Lefschetz principle one may reduce to $k=\CC$, and then the algebraic fundamental group is the profinite completion of the ordinary one. In arbitrary characteristic one has the following variant.

\paragraph{\textbf{Theorem 2 (Fulton~\cite{Fulton1980}).}}
Let $k$ be an algebraically closed field, and let $C\subset \PP^2_k$ be a plane curve whose only singularities are ordinary double points with distinct tangents. Then the tame fundamental group of $\PP^2_k\setminus C$ is abelian.

In other words, coverings in the sense of \S0.2 of $\PP^2_k$, ramified only along $C$ and only tamely ramified there, are abelian. Recall that tameness can be tested after base change to the localizations of $\PP^2_k$ at the generic points of the irreducible components of $C$; these localizations are traits (that is, spectra of discrete valuation rings).

Assume now that $k$ has characteristic $p>0$. If $C$ is as in Theorem~2, there always exists in the relative projective plane over $\operatorname{Spec}(W(k))$ a relative normal-crossings divisor $\widetilde C$ whose special fiber is $C$ (see, for example, Mumford, appendix to Chapter~VIII of~[9]). This allows one to deduce Theorem~2 from Theorem~1 by specialization.

Once one knows that $\pi_1(\PP^2(\CC)\setminus C)$ is abelian, homological arguments immediately yield its structure: if $C$ is the union of irreducible curves of degrees $d_1,\dots,d_r$, then
\[
\pi_1(\PP^2(\CC)\setminus C)\cong \ZZ^r/\ZZ\cdot(d_1,\dots,d_r).
\]

In his proof of Theorem~2, Fulton uses on the one hand methods introduced by Abhyankar~[1] to handle the special case where the irreducible components of $C$ are smooth---methods that Serre explained very clearly in~[7]---and on the other hand the following connectedness theorem. An analysis of Fulton's proof then allows the present writer to give a variant that yields Theorem~1.

Abhyankar and Fulton both exploit instances of the principle that, in algebraic geometry, anything sufficiently mobile is connected. Let $C\subset \PP^2$ be as in Theorem~2, let $D$ be an irreducible component of $C$, and let
\[
f\colon X\to \PP^2
\]
be a covering ramified only along $C$ and only tamely ramified there. Since $D\subset \PP^2$ is very mobile, $f^{-1}(D)$ is connected. More precisely, $D$ is ample in $\PP^2$, hence $f^{-1}(D)$ is ample in $X$ because $f$ is finite, and one applies Bertini. A local study of $f$ shows moreover that if $D$ is smooth then $\bigl(f^{-1}(D)\bigr)_{\mathrm{red}}$ is smooth, hence $f^{-1}(D)$ is irreducible.

In the general case let $g\colon \widetilde D\to D$ be the normalization of $D$. The same local study shows that the reduced fiber product
\[
(X\times_{\PP^2}\widetilde D)_{\mathrm{red}}
\]
is smooth, hence is the normalization of $f^{-1}(D)$. If one can prove that it is connected, it follows that $f^{-1}(D)$ is irreducible, and one may then repeat the end of Abhyankar's argument as presented in~[7].

Recall the definition of the fiber product: if
\[
h\colon X\times \widetilde D \to \PP^2\times \PP^2
\]
is the product of $f\colon X\to \PP^2$ and $g\colon \widetilde D\to D\subset \PP^2$, then $X\times_{\PP^2}\widetilde D$ is the inverse image $h^{-1}(\DeltaDiag)$ of the diagonal. Since $\PP^2$ has many automorphisms, the diagonal $\DeltaDiag\subset \PP^2\times \PP^2$ is very mobile, and therefore $h^{-1}(\DeltaDiag)$ is connected. This finishes the argument. More precisely, one applies to $h$ the special case $r=2$, $m=2$ of the following theorem.

\paragraph{\textbf{Theorem 3 (Fulton--Hansen~\cite{FultonHansen1979}).}}
Let
\[
P=(\PP^m)^r
\]
be the product of $r$ copies of projective $m$-space, let
\[
\delta\colon \PP^m\to P
\]
be the diagonal map, let $\DeltaDiag=\delta(\PP^m)$ be the diagonal, let $Z$ be a complete irreducible variety, and let
\[
h\colon Z\to P.
\]
Write $n=\dim h(Z)$ and $c=(r-1)m$, the codimension of $\DeltaDiag$ in $P$. If $n-c\ge 1$, then $h^{-1}(\DeltaDiag)$ is connected.

This theorem has many other applications; for them I refer to~[4] and to the paper of Gaffney and Lazarsfeld~[5].

To end the introduction, let us explain on an example the method used to transform Fulton's proof of Theorem~2 into a proof of Theorem~1. The example is the case where $C$ is smooth (hence irreducible).

Let $U$ be a tubular neighborhood of $C$ in $\PP^2(\CC)$, put $U^*=U\setminus C$, and choose $u\in U^*$. For any covering
\[
f\colon X\to \PP^2(\CC)
\]
ramified only along $C$, one has
\[
\bigl(f^{-1}(C)\ \text{connected}\bigr)\Longleftrightarrow \bigl(f^{-1}(U)\ \text{connected}\bigr)\Longleftrightarrow \bigl(f^{-1}(U^*)\ \text{connected}\bigr).
\]
Thus the key result---that $f^{-1}(C)$ is connected for every such covering $X$---is equivalent to the following: for every finite quotient $H$ of $\pi_1(\PP^2(\CC)\setminus C,u)$, the composite map
\[
\pi_1(U^*,u)\xrightarrow{j_*}\pi_1(\PP^2(\CC)\setminus C,u)\to H
\]
is surjective. Morse theory proves more: the map
\[
j_*\colon \pi_1(U^*,u)\to \pi_1(\PP^2(\CC)\setminus C,u)
\]
is itself surjective.

Once a Riemannian metric on $\PP^2(\CC)$ has been chosen, one may, for sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$, identify $U$ with the image under the exponential map of the radius-$\varepsilon$ subbundle of the normal bundle of $C$ in $\PP^2(\CC)$. For sufficiently small $\varepsilon$, the exponential map identifies $U^*$ with an oriented punctured-disc bundle over $C$, and hence there is an exact sequence
\[
\ZZ\to \pi_1(U^*,u)\to \pi_1(C,\bar u)\to 1
\]
(where $\bar u$ denotes the image of $u$ in $C$). The image of $\ZZ$ is central; the image of $1\in \ZZ$ is represented by a small loop $\gamma$ going once positively around $C$. Since $j_*$ is surjective, $\gamma$ defines a central element of $\pi_1(\PP^2(\CC)\setminus C,u)$. The quotient of $\pi_1(\PP^2(\CC)\setminus C,u)$ by the normal subgroup generated by $\gamma$ is
\[
\pi_1(\PP^2(\CC),u)=\{e\},
\]
so the theorem follows in this special case.

\section*{0. Terminology}

\paragraph{\textbf{0.1.}}
``Connected'' means connected and nonempty. Likewise for ``irreducible.''

\paragraph{\textbf{0.2.}}
A \emph{covering} of a normal connected scheme $S$ is a morphism $f\colon T\to S$ with $T$ normal and connected, whose restriction to some dense open subset of $T$ is \'etale.

\paragraph{\textbf{0.3.}}
Throughout the rest of the paper we work over $\CC$. We simply write $\PP^n$ for $\PP^n(\CC)$. All schemes are assumed separated.

\section{Lefschetz-- and Bertini-type theorems}

The special case of Assertion~1.1 below in which $Z\subset \PP^n$ is the complement of a hypersurface was proved by Hamm and L\^e~[6] by Morse theory. Our goal in this section is to deduce from it the variant, Theorem~1.6, of Theorem~3.

\paragraph{\textbf{Assertion 1.1.}}
Let $Z\subset \PP^n$ be a nonempty Zariski-open subset. If $H$ is a general hyperplane, the natural morphism
\[
\pi_i(Z\cap H)\to \pi_i(Z)
\]
is bijective for $i<n-1$ and surjective for $i=n-1$.

Assertion~1.1 is stated in~[2], but Cheniot's proof covers only the case where the complement of $Z$ has codimension at least $2$. Under that assumption, $Z$ and $Z\cap H$ are simply connected, so it is harmless to work, as he does, with homology rather than homotopy groups. The case $i=1$ may also be deduced from~[6] or~[2~bis].

Recall that the adjective ``general,'' when used of an element $H$ of an irreducible algebraic variety $S$ (here the dual projective space $\PP^{n\vee}$), is a quantifier: it means that there exists a nonempty Zariski-open subset $S'\subset S$ such that the desired conclusion holds for every $H\in S'$.

\paragraph{\textbf{1.2.}}
Let
\[
Y\subset \PP^n\times \PP^{n\vee}
\]
be the set of pairs $(z,H)$ such that $z\in Z$ and $z\in H$. Applying the isotopy theorem to the second projection
\[
\operatorname{pr}_2\colon Y\to \PP^{n\vee}
\]
gives a nonempty Zariski-open subset $U\subset \PP^{n\vee}$ over which $Y$ is a topologically locally trivial fibration. It is enough to take for $U$ the set of hyperplanes transverse to the strata of an algebraic Whitney stratification of the complement of $Z$ in $\PP^n$.

Locally on $U$, after choosing a trivialization of this fibration, the inclusion of the fiber $Z\cap H=\operatorname{pr}_2^{-1}(H)$ into $Z$ appears as a map depending continuously on $H$ from a fixed space into $Z$. Hence the conclusion of Assertion~1.1 either holds for every $H\in U$ or for none of them; in other words, Assertion~1.1 says precisely that it holds for all $H\in U$.

Suppose now that $Z$ depends on parameters: let $S$ be an irreducible scheme, let $Z$ be an open subset of $S\times \PP^n$, write
\[
Z(s)=\operatorname{pr}_2\bigl(\operatorname{pr}_1^{-1}(\{s\})\bigr),
\]
and assume each $Z(s)$ is nonempty. Let
\[
Y\subset S\times \PP^n\times \PP^{n\vee}
\]
be the set of triples $(s,z,H)$ with $z\in Z(s)$ and $z\in H$. Applying the isotopy theorem to the projection of $Y$ to $S\times \PP^{n\vee}$ (respectively of $Z$ to $S$) yields a nonempty Zariski-open subset $U\subset S\times \PP^{n\vee}$ (respectively $V\subset S$) over which $Y$ (respectively $Z$) is a topologically locally trivial fibration. As above, if $s\in V$ and $(s,H)\in U$, then the conclusion of Assertion~1.1 holds for $Z(s)$ and $H$. This parameterized variant will be used implicitly in the proof of Lemma~1.4.

I conjecture more generally the following.

\paragraph{\textbf{Conjecture 1.3.}}
Let $Z$ be smooth and connected of dimension $n$, let
\[
f\colon Z\to \PP^N
\]
be a morphism with finite fibers, let $c$ be an integer, and let $L\subset \PP^N$ be a linear subspace of codimension $c$. Set $n^*=n-c$. Choose a Riemannian metric on $\PP^N$, and let
\[
V(\varepsilon)=\{p\in \PP^N\mid d(p,L)<\varepsilon\}.
\]
Then, for sufficiently small $\varepsilon$, the natural morphism
\[
\pi_i\bigl(f^{-1}(V(\varepsilon))\bigr)\to \pi_i(Z)
\]
is bijective for $i<n^*$ and surjective for $i=n^*$.

For general $L$, the inclusion of $f^{-1}(L)$ in $f^{-1}(V(\varepsilon))$ is, for $\varepsilon$ sufficiently small, a homotopy equivalence; thus Conjecture~1.3 is indeed a generalization of Assertion~1.1.

If one drops the assumption that $f$ is quasi-finite and if the set of points of $\PP^N$ over which the fiber of $f$ has dimension $k$ has dimension $\varphi(k)$, then one should redefine
\[
n^*=2\dim Z-\sup\bigl(2k+\varphi(k)+\inf(\varphi(k),c-1)\bigr)-1.
\]
The conjecture should moreover follow from a more precise statement to the effect that $Z$ is obtained from $f^{-1}(V(\varepsilon))$ by attaching handles that kill spheres of dimension at least $n^*$.

We shall content ourselves with weaker results than Conjecture~1.3, deduced from the special case of Assertion~1.1 where $Z$ is the complement of a hypersurface.

\paragraph{\textbf{Lemma 1.4.}}
Let $Z$ be normal and connected, let
\[
f\colon Z\to \PP^N
\]
be a morphism, let $n=\dim f(Z)$, and let $c$ be an integer. Assume $n-c\ge 1$. If $L$ is a general linear subspace of codimension $c$ in $\PP^N$, then $f^{-1}(L)$ is connected and, for every $z\in f^{-1}(L)$, the natural morphism
\[
\pi_1\bigl(f^{-1}(L),z\bigr)\to \pi_1(Z,z)
\]
is surjective.

One may replace $Z$ by any nonempty Zariski-open subset $Z'$: for general $L$, the scheme $f^{-1}(L)$ is again normal, and $Z'$ (respectively $Z'\cap f^{-1}(L)$) is obtained from $Z$ (respectively $f^{-1}(L)$) by removing an algebraic subset with empty interior. In view of normality, this does not change $\pi_0$ and can only enlarge $\pi_1$, as is seen from the commutative diagram
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
\pi_1\bigl(Z'\cap f^{-1}(L),z\bigr) \arrow[r] \arrow[d] &
\pi_1\bigl(f^{-1}(L),z\bigr) \arrow[d] \\
\pi_1(Z',z) \arrow[r] &
\pi_1(Z,z).
\end{tikzcd}
\]
In particular, we may and do assume that $Z$ is smooth.

Shrinking $Z$, we may also assume that $f(Z)$ is locally closed and that $Z$ is a topologically locally trivial fibration over $f(Z)$.

If $c=1$ and $f$ is dominant, then $f(Z)$ is a Zariski-open subset of $\PP^N$---after shrinking $Z$ we may assume it is the complement of a hypersurface---and the lemma follows from Assertion~1.1 together with the long exact homotopy sequences for $Z\to f(Z)$ and $f^{-1}(L)\to L\cap f(Z)$.

Continue to assume $c=1$. If $f$ is not dominant, let $A$ be a linear subspace of dimension $N-n-1$, and let $B$ be the projective space of linear subspaces of dimension $N-n$ containing $A$. Define
\[
q\colon \PP^N\setminus A \to B,\qquad x\mapsto \text{the linear subspace containing }A\text{ and }x.
\]
If $A$ is disjoint from $f(Z)$, the restriction of $q$ to $f(Z)$ is finite, hence dominant because $\dim f(Z)=\dim B$, and therefore $qf\colon Z\to B$ is dominant. Applying the already proved case of the lemma to $qf$ and $c=1$, we obtain the conclusion for a general hyperplane $L$ among those containing $A$. Since $A$ itself is only required to lie in a suitable dense open subset of a Grassmannian, the conclusion also holds for a general $L$ in the usual sense (cf.~1.2).

The proof is completed by induction on $c$: a general linear subspace of codimension $c-1$ inside a general hyperplane is a general linear subspace of codimension $c$ (again cf.~1.2).

\paragraph{\textbf{Lemma 1.5.}}
Let $Z$, $f$, $n$, and $c$ be as in Lemma~1.4. Every linear subspace $L$ of codimension $c$ in $\PP^N$ admits a fundamental system of open neighborhoods $V$ such that $f^{-1}(V)$ is connected and, for every $z\in f^{-1}(V)$, the natural morphism
\[
\pi_1\bigl(f^{-1}(V),z\bigr)\to \pi_1(Z,z)
\]
is surjective.

Let $\operatorname{Gr}$ be the Grassmannian of linear subspaces of codimension $c$ in $\PP^N$, let
\[
Y\subset Z\times \operatorname{Gr}
\]
be the set of pairs $(z,L)$ such that $f(z)\in L$, and let $G\subset \operatorname{Gr}$ be a nonempty Zariski-open subset over which $Y$ is a topologically locally trivial fibration. By 1.2, the conclusion of Lemma~1.4 holds for all $L\in G$.

For every open subset $U\subset \operatorname{Gr}$, write $U^*$ for the open subset of $\PP^N$ that is the union of the $L\in U$. We claim that if $U$ is connected, then Lemma~1.5 holds for $V=U^*$.

Indeed, if $U$ is connected then $f^{-1}(U^*)$ is connected. The intersection $U'=U\cap G$ is connected, since it is obtained from $U$ by deleting a triangulable subset of real codimension at least $2$. By Lemma~1.4, the union $f^{-1}(U'^*)$ of the sets $f^{-1}(L)$ for $L\in U'$ is therefore connected. We now show that it is dense in $f^{-1}(U^*)$.

The image $f(Z)$ is not contained in the complement $F$ of $G^*$, since $n-c\ge 0$ implies that the members of $G$ meet $f(Z)$. If $z\in Z$, the image of any neighborhood of $z$ cannot be contained in $F$, otherwise the image of all of $Z$ would be, by analytic continuation. Thus if $z\in f^{-1}(U^*)$, there exist points $z'$ arbitrarily close to $z$ such that $f(z')\in G^*$. Among the $L\in \operatorname{Gr}$ containing $z'$, the intersection with $G$ is dense, because it is nonempty. If $z\in f^{-1}(L)$ with $L\in U$, this allows one to find $z'$ as above, moreover lying in some $f^{-1}(L')$ with $L'\in G$ arbitrarily close to $L$. In particular, one may choose $L'\in U'$, so $z'\in f^{-1}(U'^*)$. This proves the density of $f^{-1}(U'^*)$ in $f^{-1}(U^*)$ and hence the connectedness of $f^{-1}(U^*)$.

Finally, if $L\in U'$, then $f^{-1}(L)\subset f^{-1}(U^*)\subset Z$. By Lemma~1.4, $\pi_1\bigl(f^{-1}(L)\bigr)$ maps onto $\pi_1(Z)$, and therefore so does $\pi_1\bigl(f^{-1}(U^*)\bigr)$.

We now derive a variant of Theorem~3.

\paragraph{\textbf{Theorem 1.6.}}
Let
\[
P=(\PP^m)^r
\]
be the product of $r$ copies of projective $m$-space, let
\[
\delta\colon \PP^m\to P
\]
be the diagonal map, let $\DeltaDiag=\delta(\PP^m)$ be the diagonal, let $Z$ be normal and connected, and let
\[
h\colon Z\to P.
\]
Write $n=\dim h(Z)$ and $c=(r-1)m$, the codimension of $\DeltaDiag$ in $P$. If $n-c\ge 1$, then $\DeltaDiag$ admits a fundamental system of open neighborhoods $V$ such that $h^{-1}(V)$ is connected. Whenever $h^{-1}(V)$ is connected and $z\in h^{-1}(V)$, the natural morphism
\[
\pi_1\bigl(h^{-1}(V),z\bigr)\to \pi_1(V,z)
\]
is surjective.

As in Lemma~1.4, we may shrink $Z$, and in particular we may assume it smooth.

Choose a family of hyperplanes $(H_\alpha)_{0\le \alpha\le m}$ in $\PP^m$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item their intersection is empty, so in suitable projective coordinates they are given by $X_\alpha=0$;
\item $h(Z)$ is contained in none of the inverse images $\operatorname{pr}_i^{-1}(H_\alpha)$. After shrinking $Z$ we may and do assume that $h(Z)$ is disjoint from every $\operatorname{pr}_i^{-1}(H_\alpha)$;
\item $h(Z)\cap \DeltaDiag$ is not contained in the union of the $\delta(H_\alpha)$. Since $\DeltaDiag$ belongs to a connected family of cycles filling all of $P$, one has $h(Z)\cap \DeltaDiag\neq\varnothing$, and one can choose the $H_\alpha$ so that~(c) holds.
\end{enumerate}

Set
\[
A_\alpha=\PP^m\setminus H_\alpha,\qquad B_\alpha=A_\alpha^r\subset P,\qquad \Delta_\alpha=\delta(A_\alpha),
\]
the diagonal in $B_\alpha$. The spaces $A_\alpha$ and $B_\alpha$ are affine spaces and $\Delta_\alpha$ is an affine subspace of $B_\alpha$. The projective completion of $A_\alpha$ is $\PP^m$; denote by $\overline B_\alpha$ the completion of $B_\alpha$, and by $\overline\Delta_\alpha\subset \overline B_\alpha$ the closure of $\Delta_\alpha$.

Fix $\alpha$, and for simplicity write $H$, $A$, $B$, $\Delta_A$, $\overline B$, and $\overline \Delta_A$ for $H_\alpha$, $A_\alpha$, $B_\alpha$, $\Delta_\alpha$, $\overline B_\alpha$, and $\overline\Delta_\alpha$.

\paragraph{\textbf{Lemma 1.7.}}
For every neighborhood $U$ of $\DeltaDiag$ in $P$, there exists a neighborhood $V$ of $\overline\Delta_A$ in $\overline B$ such that
\[
V\cap B \subset U\cap B.
\]

Choose on $A$ a Hermitian vector-space structure compatible with its affine structure. A fundamental system of neighborhoods of $\DeltaDiag$ (respectively of $\overline\Delta_A$) has, upon intersection with $B$, the form
\begin{align*}
U\cap B
&=
\left\{(x_i)\ \middle|\ 
\begin{array}{l}
\text{if } |x_i|\le R \text{ for some }i,\ \text{then } |x_j-x_k|<\varepsilon\ \forall j,k,\\
\text{if all }|x_i|\ge R,\ \text{then the angle between the lines } \CC x_j,\CC x_k\\
\text{is }<\varepsilon\ \forall j,k
\end{array}
\right\},\\[1ex]
V\cap B
&=
\left\{(x_i)\ \middle|\ 
\begin{array}{l}
\text{if } |x_i|\le R \text{ for some }i,\ \text{then } |x_j-x_k|<\varepsilon\ \forall j,k,\\
\text{if all }|x_i|\ge R,\ \text{then } |x_j-x_k|\le \varepsilon\, \inf_i |x_i|\ \forall j,k
\end{array}
\right\}.
\end{align*}
The lemma follows immediately.

In the case $r=m=1$, the two compactifications $\PP^1\times \PP^1$ and $\PP^2$ of $\A^2$ are dominated by a third one obtained by blowing up two boundary points; the original paper gives a schematic figure illustrating this and indicating the neighborhoods $U$ and $V$.

We now return to the proof of Theorem~1.6. Apply Lemma~1.5 to
\[
h\colon Z\to \overline B
\]
and to the affine diagonal $\Delta_A\subset \overline B$. We find that $\overline\Delta_A$ has arbitrarily small open neighborhoods $V_\alpha$ such that
\[
h^{-1}(V_\alpha)=h^{-1}(V_\alpha\cap B_\alpha)
\]
is connected and has fundamental group mapping onto that of $Z$. By Lemma~1.7, given any neighborhood $U$ of $\DeltaDiag$ in $P$, one can choose each $V_\alpha$ so that
\[
V_\alpha\cap B_\alpha\subset U.
\]

Then the union $V$ of the opens $V_\alpha\cap B_\alpha$ is a neighborhood, arbitrarily small, of $\DeltaDiag$ in $P$. Hypothesis~(b) on the hyperplanes $H_\alpha$ ensures that the intersection of the sets $V_\alpha\cap B_\alpha$ meets $h(Z)$. Hence
\[
h^{-1}(V),
\]
being a union of connected open sets $h^{-1}(V_\alpha)$ with nonempty mutual intersection, is connected. Since the $\pi_1$ of one of the $h^{-1}(V_\alpha)$ maps onto $\pi_1(Z)$, the same is true a fortiori for $\pi_1\bigl(h^{-1}(V)\bigr)$, which completes the proof.

\section{Proof of Theorem~1}

\paragraph{\textbf{2.1.}}
Let $C\subset \PP^2$ be a reduced plane curve, let $D$ be an irreducible component of $C$, let $C'=(C-D)$ be the union of the other components, let $\operatorname{Sing}(C)$ be the set of singular points of $C$, and let
\[
D^*=D-\operatorname{Sing}(C).
\]
Choose a base point $O\in \PP^2-C$, and let $\gamma$ be a loop in $\PP^2-C$ of the following form: $\gamma$ goes from $O$ to a point $p'$ near a point $p\in D^*$ by a path $\mathrm{ch}$, winds once positively around $D^*$, and returns to $O$ by $\mathrm{ch}^{-1}$. Since $D^*$ is connected, all loops of this type are mutually conjugate. The quotient of $\pi_1(\PP^2-C,O)$ by the normal subgroup they generate is
\[
\pi_1(\PP^2-C').
\]

\paragraph{\textbf{Proposition 2.2.}}
Assume that the singular points of $C$ lying on $D$ are ordinary double points with distinct tangents. Then $\gamma$ is central in $\pi_1(\PP^2-C,O)$.

Let $j$ be the inclusion of $\PP^2-C$ into $\PP^2$, let $g\colon \widetilde D\to D$ be the normalization of $D$, and apply Theorem~1.6 to
\[
h=j\times g\colon (\PP^2-C)\times \widetilde D \to \PP^2\times \PP^2.
\]

To describe conveniently the inverse image of certain neighborhoods of the diagonal, we choose a Riemannian structure on $\PP^2$ such that, in a neighborhood of every point $P\in D\cap \operatorname{Sing}(C)$, the surface $\PP^2$ is Euclidean and the two branches of $C$ through $P$ are orthogonal real $2$-planes.

If $(u,v)\in (\PP^2-C)\times \widetilde D$ lies in the inverse image of a sufficiently small neighborhood of the diagonal---that is, if $u$ and $v$ are sufficiently close---then $u$ lies near $D$, and one can drop from $u$ a short perpendicular to $D$. When $u$ lies near $\operatorname{Sing}(D)$ there are even two such perpendiculars, one for each branch. We choose the one whose foot $\bar u$ is close to $v$ on $\widetilde D$; then $\bar u\in D^*$.

Conversely, let $\varepsilon>0$ be sufficiently small, let
\[
q\colon U^*\to D^*
\]
be the punctured-disc subbundle of the normal bundle to $D^*$ in $\PP^2$ consisting of nonzero normal vectors of length $<\varepsilon$, and let $E$ be the set of pairs $(x,v)$ with $x\in U^*$, $v\in \widetilde D$, and
\[
d\bigl(q(x),v\bigr)<\varepsilon
\]
(the distance being computed on $\widetilde D$). The projection
\[
\operatorname{pr}_1\colon E\to U^*
\]
is a homotopy equivalence, with section given by the subspace of pairs $(x,q(x))$. The map
\[
\exp\colon (x,v)\mapsto (\exp(x),v)
\]
from $E$ to $(\PP^2-C)\times \widetilde D$ identifies $E$ with the inverse image of a neighborhood of the diagonal, and as $\varepsilon\to 0$ one obtains the inverse images of a fundamental system of neighborhoods. Applying Theorem~1.6, we find that for any $O\in U^*$ the maps $\exp$ and $q$ induce a surjection
\[
(\exp,q)_*\colon \pi_1(U^*,O)\to \pi_1(\PP^2-C,\exp(O))\times \pi_1(\widetilde D,q(O)).
\]

There is an exact homotopy sequence
\[
\ZZ\to \pi_1(U^*,O)\to \pi_1(D^*,q(O))\to 1.
\]
The image of $\ZZ$ is central; the image of $1\in \ZZ$ is represented by a small loop turning once positively around $D^*$. Since $(\exp,q)_*$, and hence $\exp_*$, is surjective, this loop determines a central element of $\pi_1(\PP^2-C,\exp(O))$; this is precisely $\gamma$.

\paragraph{\textbf{2.3. Proof of Theorem~1.}}
For every irreducible component $D$ of $C$, let $\gamma(D)$ be the corresponding central element of $\pi_1(\PP^2-C,O)$. The quotient of $\pi_1(\PP^2-C,O)$ by the central subgroup generated by the $\gamma(D)$ is
\[
\pi_1(\PP^2,O)=\{e\}.
\]
Therefore $\pi_1(\PP^2-C,O)$ is abelian. \qed
