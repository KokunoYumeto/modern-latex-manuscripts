\section{Introduction}

Let $S$ be a complex algebraic variety and $X$ a family of nonsingular projective varieties parametrized by $S$: the $X_s$ are the fibers of $f:\mathcal{X}\to S$, with $X_s$ projective and $f$ smooth over $S$. Let moreover $h\in H^{2p}(X_s,\mathbb{Q})$ be a Hodge class, i.e. of type $(p,p)$ in the Hodge decomposition of $H^{2p}(X_s,\mathbb{C})$. On the local system $R^{2p}f_*(\mathbb{Q})$, the Hodge filtration induces a filtration by holomorphic subbundles. Their projective bundle has a flat structure and the local system of the $H^{2p}(X_t,\mathbb{Z})$ modulo torsion is, on a suitable connected neighborhood $U$ of $s$, trivial on $U$, so that for $t\in U$ they can all be identified with $H^{2p}(X_s,\mathbb{Z})$. The Hodge filtration $F_t$ of $H^{2p}(X_t,\mathbb{C})$, $t\in U$, can be viewed as a variable filtration on the fixed complex vector space $H^{2p}(X_s,\mathbb{C})$. It varies holomorphically with $t$.

It follows that the locus $T\subset U$ where $h$ remains of type $(p,p)$, i.e. in $F^p$, is a complex analytic subspace of $U$.

It follows from the (rational) Hodge conjecture that the germ of $T$ at $s$ is algebraic, meaning that its irreducible components are irreducible components of germs at $s$ of algebraic subvarieties of $S$. Sketch of proof: let $T_0$ be an irreducible component of $T$ containing $s$ and assume that for all $t$ in $T_0$ some non zero multiple of $h$ is the class of an algebraic cycle in $X_t$. A Baire category argument shows that for suitable $M$, $N$, the set of $t\in T_0$ for which $h$ is the class of an algebraic cycle of degree $\leq M$ on $X_t$ with $Z^p - Z^{p-}$ effective, $Z^{\pm}$ effective cycles of degree $\leq M$ on the $X_t$, is a union of countably many closed algebraic subvarieties of $T_0$. On the other hand, because the Chow varieties of effective cycles of degree $\leq M$ on the $X_t$ form an algebraic variety over $S$, or simply that they form a limited family.

In Corollary~1.2 below, we prove unconditionally that the germ of $T$ at $s$, as above, is indeed algebraic. Our main result, Theorem~1.1, is slightly more precise and gives as corollary a positive answer to a question of A.~Weil~\cite{weil}:\ ``\dots whether imposing a certain Hodge class upon a generic member of [such family] amounts to an algebraic condition upon the parameters.''

The Hodge conjecture would also imply that if $f:\mathcal{X}\to S$ can be defined over an algebraically closed subfield of $\mathbb{C}$, then so can the germ of $T$ at $s$. About this, we are not able to say anything.

The proof will be in the setting of variations of Hodge structures, of which the local system of the $H^{2p}(X_t,\mathbb{Z})$ modulo torsion is an example. For the definition and basic properties of variations of Hodge structures on a complex variety $S$, we refer to \cite{griffiths} or \cite{schmid}.

Our notations are spelled out in 2.1. Note that in the Hodge decomposition, we do not assume $p,q\geq 0$. If $H$ is a Hodge structure of even weight $2p$, shifting the Hodge filtration, i.e., replacing $H$ by the Tate twist $H(p)$, one obtains a Hodge structure of weight $0$. Classes of type $(p,p)$ become of type $(0,0)$. This allows us to restrict our attention to variations of weight $0$ and classes of type $(0,0)$. For simplicity, we will assume the parameter space $S$ to be nonsingular.

Let $S$ be a nonsingular complex algebraic variety and $\mathbb{V}$ a variation of Hodge structures of weight $0$ on $S$ with polarization form $Q$. If $C$ is the Weil operator: multiplication by $i^{p-q}$ on $\mathbb{V}^{p,q}$, the hermitian form $h(u,v)=Q(Cu,\bar{v})$ is positive definite and makes the Hodge decomposition orthogonal. For $u$ a real element of type $(0,0)$, $h(u,u)=Q(u,u)$. Fix an integer $K$ and let $S^{(K)}$ be the space of pairs $(s,u)$ with $s\in S$, $u\in \mathbb{V}_{s\mathbb{Z}}$ integral of type $(0,0)$, and $Q(u,u)\leq K$. It projects to $S$ and arguments as above show that, locally on $S$, $S^{(K)}$ is a finite disjoint sum of closed analytic subspaces. Our main result is:

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
$S^{(K)}$ is an algebraic variety, finite over $S$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:germ}
Fix $s\in S$ and $u\in \mathbb{V}_{s\mathbb{Z}}$ integral of type $(0,0)$. The germ of analytic subvariety of $S$ where $u$ remains of type $(0,0)$, is algebraic.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The required algebraic subvarieties of $S$ are images in $S$ of irreducible components of $S^{(K)}$, for $K=Q(u,u)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:section}
Let $u$ be a section of the local system $\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}$ on a universal covering of $S$. The set of points in $S$ where some determination of $u$ is of type $(0,0)$, is an algebraic subvariety of $S$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Such set is a union of images of connected components of $S^{(K)}$, for $K=Q(u,u)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:hodgesub}
Let $\mathbb{V}$ be a polarizable variation of Hodge structures on $S$, fix $s\in S$ and let $U_{\mathbb{Q}}\subset \mathbb{V}_{s\mathbb{Q}}$ be a rational subspace. The locus where some flat translate of $U_{\mathbb{Q}}$ is a Hodge substructure is an algebraic subvariety of $S$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Let $T$ be the locus in question, assumed nonempty, and suppose first that $U_{\mathbb{Q}}$ is of dimension one. Then $\mathbb{V}$ is of even weight $2p$ so that, replacing it by $\mathbb{V}(p)$, we may and shall assume it to be of weight $0$. Let $e$ be a generator of $U_{\mathbb{Q}}\cap \mathbb{V}_{s\mathbb{Z}}$. Then $U_{\mathbb{Q}}$ is a Hodge substructure if and only if $e$ is of type $(0,0)$, and one applies \ref{cor:section}.

Consider now the general case: $U_{\mathbb{Q}}$ of dimension $n$. A Hodge structure on a rational vector space $H_{\mathbb{Q}}$ gives rise to an action of the real algebraic group $\mathbb{C}^*$ on $H_{\mathbb{R}}=H_{\mathbb{Q}}\otimes\mathbb{R}$, with $z\in\mathbb{C}^*$ acting as multiplication by $z^p\bar{z}^{-q}$ on $H^{p,q}$. A subspace $U_{\mathbb{Q}}\subset H_{\mathbb{Q}}$ is a sub-Hodge structure--i.e., $U_{\mathbb{C}}=U_{\mathbb{Q}}\otimes\mathbb{C}$ is the sum of its intersections with the $H^{p,q}$--if and only if $U_{\mathbb{R}}$ is stable under $\mathbb{C}^*$. This amounts to $\bigwedge^n U_{\mathbb{R}}\cap\bigwedge^n H_{\mathbb{R}}$ being stable under $\mathbb{C}^*$, i.e. to $\bigwedge^n U_{\mathbb{Q}}$ being a Hodge substructure of $\bigwedge^n\mathbb{V}_{s\mathbb{Q}}$, and reduces us to the one-dimensional case, proving \ref{cor:hodgesub}.
\end{proof}

Let $D^*=D-\{0\}$ be the open punctured disk. Theorem~\ref{thm:main} will be derived from the following local result.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:local}
Let $\mathbb{V}$ be a polarized variation of Hodge structures of weight $0$ on $S=(D^*)^r\times D^m$, with unipotent monodromy. Define $S^{(K)}$ as in \ref{thm:main}. Then, there is a neighborhood $U$ of $0$ in $D^{r+m}$ such that, above $U$, $S^{(K)}$ is a finite disjoint sum of traces on $S\cap U$ of closed analytic subspaces of $U$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent\textit{Proof of \ref{thm:main} $\Rightarrow$ \ref{thm:local}:} To prove \ref{thm:main} one is free to replace $S$ of \ref{thm:main} by a finite \'etale covering $S'\to S$. We may and shall assume that the monodromy mod $k$ of $\mathbb{V}$ is trivial, for some $k\geq 3$.

Let $\bar{S}$ be a smooth compactification of $S$, with $\bar{S}-S$ a divisor with normal crossings. The assumption on the monodromy ensures that the local monodromy of $\mathbb{V}$ at infinity is unipotent: in a neighborhood of any point in $\bar{S}-S$, one is in the situation considered in \ref{thm:local}. One concludes that $S^{(K)}$ can be extended to a space $\bar{S}^{(K)}$ over $\bar{S}$, which locally over $\bar{S}$ is a finite disjoint sum of closed analytic subspaces. By GAGA, $\bar{S}^{(K)}$, finite over $\bar{S}$, is algebraic, and \ref{thm:main} follows. \qed

The rest of the paper is devoted to proving \ref{thm:local}. To simplify notations, we will assume $m=0$. The general case is recovered by considering the partition of $S$, according to which of $z_{r+1},\dots,z_{r+m}$ vanish.

\section{Notations. Schema of Proof.}

\subsection{}
Let $\mathbb{V}$ be a polarized variation of Hodge structures of weight $w$ on a complex manifold $S$, with polarization form $Q$. We write $\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}$ for the underlying local system of free $\mathbb{Z}$-modules, $\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{O}_S}$ for the corresponding holomorphic vector bundle, identified with its sheaf of sections $\mathcal{O}_S\otimes\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}$, and $\mathbb{V}$ for the underlying complex vector bundle, identified with its sheaf of $C^{\infty}$ sections. The Hodge decomposition is a decomposition of this complex vector bundle
\[
\mathbb{V}=\bigoplus_{p+q=w}\mathbb{V}^{p,q}
\]
and the corresponding Hodge filtration
\[
F^p=\bigoplus_{a\geq p}\mathbb{V}^{a,b}
\]
is holomorphic. We still write $F^p$ for the corresponding filtration of $\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{O}_S}$. Griffiths transversality axiom then reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transversality}
\nabla F^p\subset \Omega^1\otimes F^{p-1}.
\end{equation}

The Weil operator $C$ is the endomorphism of $\mathbb{V}$ acting on $\mathbb{V}^{p,q}$ as multiplication by $i^{p-q}$. The polarization form $Q$ is best viewed as a morphism of Hodge structures $\mathbb{V}\otimes\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{Z}(-w)$. It is an integral bilinear form on $\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}$, $(-1)^w$-symmetric, and the form on $\mathbb{V}$
\[
h(u,v)=Q(Cu,\bar{v})
\]
is hermitian symmetric, positive definite and makes the Hodge decomposition orthogonal. This Hodge metric is, generally, not flat.

\subsection{}
We will need Schmid's theory of nilpotent orbits giving the asymptotic behavior of variations of Hodge structures \cite{schmid}. We begin with a coordinate-free description. The coordinate-bound translation given in 2.3 suffices for our needs.

Let $S$ be the complement in a smooth variety $\bar{S}$ of smooth divisors $E_i$ meeting transversally. Assume that the monodromy of $\mathbb{V}$ around the $E_i$ is unipotent. Let $\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{O}_{\bar{S}}}$ be the canonical extension \cite{deligne-edpsr} of the holomorphic vector bundle $\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{O}_S}$ to $\bar{S}$. It is characterized by the property that, in any local basis of $\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{O}_S}$, the connection matrix (an endomorphism-valued $1$-form) has logarithmic poles with nilpotent residues along the $E_i$. The first result is that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hodgeextends}
\text{The Hodge filtration of $\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{O}_S}$ extends to a filtration of $\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{O}_{\bar{S}}}$ by locally direct factors.}
\end{equation}

Let $E$ be the intersection of the $E_i$, $L_i$ the restriction to $E$ of the normal line bundle of $E_i$, and $L_i^*$ the complement in $L_i$ of the zero section. The product $L$ of the $L_i$ is the normal bundle of $E$ and the product $L^*$ of the $L_i^*$ is obtained by removing from the normal bundle $L$, the normal bundles of $E$ in the $E_i$.

The nilpotent orbit $\mathbb{V}^{un}$ (or: deformation to the normal cone) approximating $\mathbb{V}$ around $E$ lives on $L^*$. It is obtained as follows:

(a) As a filtered holomorphic vector bundle, it is the pullback of the restriction of $(\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{O}_{\bar{S}}},F)$ to $E$.

(b) The connection $\nabla^{un}$ on $\mathbb{V}^{un}$ is described as follows. Locally, let $z_i=0$ be an equation for $E_i$. It defines a trivialization of $L_i$ and we write again $z_i$ for the corresponding function on $L$. In a local basis of $\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{O}_{\bar{S}}}$, the connection $\nabla$ is $d+\Gamma$, with $\Gamma=\Gamma_0+\sum_i \nu_i\frac{dz_i}{z_i}$, $\Gamma_0$ and $\nu_i$ holomorphic. The local basis of $\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{O}_{\bar{S}}}$ gives one on $E$, which pulls back to $\mathbb{V}^{un}$; in this basis,
\[
\nabla^{un}=d+(\Gamma_0|E)+\sum_i (\nu_i|E)\frac{dz_i}{z_i}.
\]

(c) It remains to define the integral lattice. Locally, let $\varphi$ be an isomorphism from a neighborhood of the zero section of $L$ (identified with $E$) to a neighborhood of $E$ in $\bar{S}$. Assume it is the identity on $E$ and that, on $E$, $d\varphi$ induces the identity on the normal bundle of $E$. Assume further that $\varphi$ maps $L^*$ into $S$. Then, the pullback by $\varphi$ of the local system $\nabla$ of horizontal sections of $\mathbb{V}$ is canonically isomorphic to $\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}$. One defines $(\mathbb{V}^{un})_{\mathbb{Z}}$ to be the pullback of $\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}$.

The results are that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nilpotentorbit}
\text{On the trace on $L^*$ of a neighborhood $U$ of $E$, with $U$ depending only on the rank of $\mathbb{V}$, $\mathbb{V}^{un}$ is a variation of Hodge structures, polarized by a polarization form of $\mathbb{V}$.}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:close}
\text{For $\varphi$ as in (c), defined on $U$, $\mathbb{V}$ and $\varphi^*\mathbb{V}^{un}$ are close: on any compact $K\subset U$, the distance at $z\in K\cap L^*$ between the Hodge filtrations, measured with an invariant metric on the relevant period mapping domain, i.e., using the Hodge metrics, is $\leq C_0 d(z,E)\frac{1}{|\log d(z,E)|^{C_1}}$}
\end{equation}
with $C_0$ depending only on the rank, and $C_1$ only on the rank, on $K$ and on the chosen distance $d$.

\subsection{}
We now translate in coordinates. Let $D\subset\mathbb{C}$ be the open unit disk and $D^*=D-\{0\}$. Take $S=(D^*)^r\subset D^r$. Assume that the monodromy of $\mathbb{V}$ is unipotent. Let $M_i$ be the monodromy around $z_i=0$ and $N_i=\log M_i$.

Using the coordinate $z_i$, one can view $\mathbb{V}^{un}$ as living on $(\mathbb{C}^*)^r\supset S$. The underlying local system is the unique local system extending $\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}$ on $S$. We continue to denote it by $\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}$. Let $V_{\mathbb{Z}}$ be its fiber at $1$.

The Poincar\'e upper half-plane $\mathcal{H}$ is the universal covering of $D^*$, with covering map $z\mapsto s=e^{2\pi iz}$. Similarly, $\mathcal{H}^r$ is the universal covering of $S=(D^*)^r$ and $(\mathbb{C}^r,0)$ that of $(\mathbb{C}^{*r},1)$. For $z\in\mathbb{C}^r$, we write $s$ for the corresponding point in $\mathbb{C}^{*r}$.

When pulled back to $\mathcal{H}^r$, the variation can be described as a variable Hodge filtration $\Phi(z)$ on the fixed vector space $V_{\mathbb{C}}=V_{\mathbb{Z}}\otimes\mathbb{C}$, with
\[
\Phi(z+e_j)=\exp(N_j)\Phi(z)
\]
for $e_j$ the $j$th coordinate vector in $\mathbb{C}^r$. We also view $\Phi$ as a holomorphic map $\Phi:\mathcal{H}^r\to\check{D}(V_{\mathbb{Z}},Q)$ with values in the appropriate period mapping domain.

Statement \eqref{eq:hodgeextends} translates as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nilpotentform}
\Phi(z)=\exp\bigl(\sum_j z_j N_j\bigr)\Psi(s)
\end{equation}
($s=e^{2\pi iz}$) for some holomorphic map $\Psi$ from $D^r$ to the flag manifold of $V_{\mathbb{C}}$. The nilpotent orbit $\mathbb{V}^{un}$ is given by
\[
\Phi_{un}(z)=\exp\bigl(\sum_j z_j N_j\bigr)\Psi(0).
\]

Statements \eqref{eq:nilpotentorbit} and \eqref{eq:close} translate as the existence of constants $C_0,C_2,C_3$, depending only on the rank of $\mathbb{V}$, with the following properties. Let $x_i$ and $y_i$ be the real and imaginary parts of $z_i$. Then, as soon as $\inf(y_i)\geq C_2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hodgestructure}
\Phi_{un}(z)\text{ defines a Hodge structure on }V.
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:distance}
\text{The distance between $\Phi(z)$ and $\Phi_{un}(z)$, measured as in \eqref{eq:close}, is $\leq C_3 e^{-2\pi\inf_j(y_j)}\leq\inf_i(y_i)^{-C_0}$.}
\end{equation}

Fix $I\subset\{1,\dots,r\}$, with complement $J$. Let $E$ be the intersection in $D^r$ of the divisors $z_i=0$, for $i\in I$. It is identified with $D^J$. Let $q_I$ be the projection $(D^*)^r\to(D^*)^J$. The nilpotent orbit along $E$ is then given by $\mathbb{V}^{un,I}$
\[
\Phi_{un,I}(z)=\exp\bigl(\sum_j z_j N_j\bigr)\Psi(q_I(s)).
\]
Freezing the $z_j$ ($j\in J$) and applying \eqref{eq:hodgestructure}, \eqref{eq:distance}, one sees that $\Phi_{un,I}(z)$ gives a Hodge structure on $V$ as soon as $\inf_{i\in I}(y_i)\geq C_2$, and that the proximity of $\Phi_{un,I}(z)$ to $\Phi(z)$ is controlled by $\inf_{i\in I}(y_i)$ as in \eqref{eq:distance}.

\subsection{}
Any nilpotent endomorphism $N$ of $V$, $N^{k+1}=0$, has an associated filtration $W(N)$. This is an increasing filtration
\[
0=W_{-k-1}(N)\subset\dots\subset W_k(N)=V
\]
characterized by the properties that
\[
NW_{\ell}\subset W_{\ell-2}
\]
and that $N^{\ell}$ induces an isomorphism
\[
N^{\ell}:\Gr_{\ell}^W\xrightarrow{\sim}\Gr_{-\ell}^W.
\]

With the notations of 2.3, it is shown in \cite{cks} that all elements $N$ in the cone
\[
C=\Bigl\{\sum_j \lambda_j N_j,\; \lambda_j>0\Bigr\}
\]
define the same filtration $W(C)$. As $C\times X$ contains endomorphisms defined over $\mathbb{Q}$, the filtration $W:=W(C)$ is defined over $\mathbb{Q}$.

Let us call ``limiting Hodge filtration'' any filtration of the form $\Phi_{un}(z)$ ($z\in\mathbb{C}^r$). It is a consequence of the SL(2)-orbit theorem \cite{schmid} that, for any limiting Hodge filtration $F$, $F$ and $W[-w]$ define a mixed Hodge structure. Here $w$ is the weight of $\mathbb{V}$ and $W[-w]_{\ell}=W_{\ell-w}$.

The mixed Hodge structure $(W[-w],F)$ is polarized, in the sense of \cite{cks}, by the polarization form of $\mathbb{V}$ and any $N$ in $C$.

Theorem~\ref{thm:local} will be deduced from the following result, where we use the notations of 2.3, 2.4.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:finite}
Assume $\mathbb{V}$ of weight $0$. Given $K$, there is a constant $A_1$ (depending on $K$ and $\mathbb{V}$) such that
\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)] There are only finitely many $v\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}$ such that: $Q(v,v)\leq K$ and $v\in\Phi^0(z)$ at some point $z$ with $0\leq x_i\leq 1$ and $\inf(y_i)\geq A_1$.
\item[(ii)] Any such $v$ is in $W_0\cap F^0$ for some limiting Hodge filtration $F$, as well as in $F^0$.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:mixed}
If $v$ is, at $z$, in $\Phi^0$, it defines a morphism of Hodge structures from the unit Hodge structure $\mathbb{Z}(0)$ to $\mathbb{V}$ at $z$. By (ii), if $Q(v,v)\leq K$ and $\inf(y_i)\geq A_1$, it will also define a morphism of mixed Hodge structures from $\mathbb{Z}(0)$ to $\mathbb{V}^{un}$ at some point $z'$. In 2.13, we will deduce from 2.3 that $z'$ can be chosen such that
\[
|z'-z|\leq A_2 e^{-2\pi\inf(y_i)}
\]
with $A_2$ depending only on $\mathbb{V}$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{}
As a preparation to deducing \ref{thm:local} from \ref{thm:finite}, we now fix $v\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}$ which, for some $z_0$, is in $W_0\cap\Phi_{un}^0(z_0)$, and investigate the locus where $v\in\Phi^0(z)$. We make the change of variables $z\mapsto z-z_0$, $s\mapsto s/s_0$, to simplify notations by assuming $z_0=0$. This is at the cost of having $\Phi(z)$ defined, in the new coordinates, only for $y_i\geq -\Im(z_{0i})$.

Let $F$ be the filtration $\Phi_{un}(0)$. Let $V^{p,q}$ be the bigrading of $V_{\mathbb{C}}$ associated to the mixed Hodge structure $(W,F)$ (cf.\ \cite[2.13]{deligne-trdc2}). It splits the filtrations $W$ and $F$: $W_{\ell}$ (resp.\ $F^p$) is the sum of the $V^{a,b}$ for $a+b\leq\ell$ (resp.\ $a\geq p$). The assumptions on $v$ imply that $v\in V^{0,0}$.

The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{gl}(V)$ of $\GL(V)$ inherits from $V$ a mixed Hodge structure whose associated bigrading is
\[
\mathfrak{g}^{p,q}=\{X\in\mathfrak{gl}(V)\mid X(V^{r,s})\subset V^{p+r,q+s}\}.
\]
We will mainly use the corresponding $p$-grading, for which $V^p$ (resp.\ $\mathfrak{g}^p$) is the sum of the $V^{p,b}$ (resp.\ $\mathfrak{g}^{p,b}$). The isotropy subalgebra of $\mathfrak{g}$ at $F$ is $F^0(\mathfrak{g})=\bigoplus_{p\geq 0}\mathfrak{g}^p$. It admits as supplement the nilpotent subalgebra $\mathfrak{b}=\bigoplus_{p<0}\mathfrak{g}^p$. The map $X\mapsto \exp(X)\cdot F$ identifies a neighborhood of $0$ in $\mathfrak{b}$ with a neighborhood of $F$ in the flag space. As $\Psi(0)=F$, we can, for $y_i$ large enough, rewrite \eqref{eq:nilpotentform} as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phigamma}
\Phi(z)=\exp\bigl(\sum_j z_j N_j\bigr)\exp(\Gamma(s))\cdot F
\end{equation}
($s=e^{2\pi iz}$) with $\Gamma$ holomorphic at $s=0$, $\mathfrak{b}$-valued and such that $\Gamma(0)=0$.

The $N_j$ are morphisms of mixed Hodge structures $V\to V(-1)$, hence they lie in $\mathfrak{g}^{-1,-1}\subset\mathfrak{g}^{-1}\subset\mathfrak{b}$. As $v\in V^0$, the equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vinF0}
v\in\Phi^0(z)=\exp\bigl(\sum z_j N_j\bigr)\exp(\Gamma(s))\cdot F^0
\end{equation}
or, equivalently,
\[
(\exp(\sum z_j N_j)\exp(\Gamma(s)))^{-1}(v)\in F^0=\bigoplus_{p\geq 0}V^p,
\]
holds if and only if $v$ is fixed by $\exp(\sum z_j N_j)\exp(\Gamma(s))$. Let $\Gamma_p(s)$ be the component of $\Gamma(s)$ in $\mathfrak{g}^p$. Taking the component in $V^{-1}$ of the equation $\exp(\sum z_j N_j)\exp(\Gamma(s))(v)=v$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:component}
\bigl(\sum_j z_j N_j+\Gamma_{-1}(s)\bigr)(v)=0.
\end{equation}

We now use the transversality axiom to prove:

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:connected}
If at one point of the analytic space $\Sigma$ where \eqref{eq:component} holds one has $v\in\Phi^0(z)$, the same is true on the whole connected component of $\Sigma$ containing that point.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Equation \eqref{eq:component} is equivalent to
\[
(\exp(\sum z_j N_j)\exp(\Gamma(s)))^{-1}(v)\in F^0\oplus\bigoplus_{p\leq -2}V^p,
\]
i.e., to
\[
v\in\exp(\sum z_j N_j)\exp(\Gamma(s))\bigl(F^0\oplus\bigoplus_{p\leq -2}V^p\bigr),
\]
so that \ref{lem:connected} is a particular case of

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:variation}
Let $\mathbb{X}$ be a variation of Hodge structures on a complex manifold $M$. Let $U$ be a supplement to $F^{p+1}$. For $v$ a horizontal section of $\mathbb{X}$, let $\Sigma$ be the locus where $v\in F^p\oplus U$. Then, if at one point of $\Sigma$ one has $v\in F^p$, then the same holds in the whole connected component of $\Sigma$ containing that point.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By assumption, $\mathbb{X}=F^{p+1}\oplus U$. The connection $\nabla$ of $\mathbb{X}$ induces a connection $\nabla_U$ on $U$, not necessarily integrable:
\[
\nabla_U(\xi)=\text{projection of }\nabla(\xi).
\]
Let $\hat{v}$ be the projection of $v$ to $U$. Because $\nabla F^p\subset\Omega^1\otimes F^{p-1}$ on $\Sigma$, $\hat{v}$ is a horizontal section of $U$. The lemma follows.
\end{proof}
\noindent This completes the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:connected}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{}
By \ref{lem:connected}, the locus where $v\in\Phi^0(z)$ is a union of connected components of the locus $\Sigma$ where \eqref{eq:component} holds. Each $N_j v$ is in $V_{\mathbb{Q}}$. Writing \eqref{eq:component} in a basis of $V_{\mathbb{Q}}$, we obtain a system of equations
\begin{equation}\label{eq:system}
\sum_j \nu(\alpha)_j z_j+\gamma(\alpha)(s)=0\quad (\alpha=1,\dots,\dim V_{\mathbb{Q}})
\end{equation}
with $\nu(\alpha)_j$ rational, $\gamma(\alpha)(s)$, $s=e^{2\pi iz}$, holomorphic at $0$ and $\gamma(\alpha)(0)=0$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:analytic}
Near $0$ in $D^r$, there is a closed analytic subspace $\Delta$ such that, if $\Sigma_0$ is a connected component of $\Sigma$ (equation \eqref{eq:component}), its image in $(D^*)^r$ is a connected component $\Delta_0$ of $\Delta\cap (D^*)^r$. Further, the image of $\pi_1(\Delta_0)$ in $\pi_1((D^*)^r)$ fixes $v$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If we clear denominators and exponentiate, equations \eqref{eq:system} give
\[
\prod_i a_{\alpha}(s)\cdot s_i^{n(\alpha,i)}=1
\]
with $a_{\alpha}(s)$ holomorphic at $0$, $a_{\alpha}(0)=1$ and $n(\alpha,i)\in\mathbb{Z}$. That $a_{\alpha}(0)=1$ springs from the normalization $z_0=0$. As equations for $\Delta$, we take
\[
a_{\alpha}(s)\cdot\prod_i s_i^{n(\alpha,i)}-\prod_i s_i^{-\min(0,n(\alpha,i))}=0.
\]

An element $m$ of $\pi_1((D^*)^r)=\mathbb{Z}^r$ is in the image of $\pi_1(\Delta_0)$ if and only if $\Sigma_0$ contains, together with any point $z$, the point $z+m$. Subtracting the corresponding equations \eqref{eq:system}, we obtain
\[
\sum_j \nu_{\alpha j} m_j=0
\]
i.e.,
\[
\sum_i m_i N_i v=0.
\]
Since $m$ acts on $V$ as $\exp(\sum m_i N_i)$, the Lemma follows.
\end{proof}

\subsection{}
\textit{Proof of \ref{thm:finite} $\Rightarrow$ \ref{thm:local}:} As explained after \ref{thm:local}, it suffices in \ref{thm:local} to consider the case $m=0$: $S=(D^*)^r$. By \ref{thm:finite}, \ref{lem:connected}, \ref{lem:analytic}, there are, in a neighborhood of $0\in D^r$, finitely many closed analytic subspaces $Y$ such that all connected components of $S^{(K)}$ are obtained by taking a connected component of $Y\cap(D^*)^r$ and a section of $\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}$ on it. \qed

\subsection{}
\textit{Proof of \ref{rem:mixed}:} Expressed in $(D^*)^r$, \ref{rem:mixed} claims that, for $s$ close enough to $0$, if $v\in(\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{Z}})_s$ is of type $(0,0)$ and satisfies $Q(v,v)\leq K$, then at a nearby point $s'$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nearby}
|s-s'|\leq A_3|s|
\end{equation}
the same $v$ is of type $(0,0)$ for $\mathbb{V}^{un}$. ``Same'' means: horizontal translate by a path remaining in the neighborhood \eqref{eq:nearby} of $s$.

As the strip $0\leq x_i\leq 1$ covers $(D^*)^r$, working on $\mathcal{H}^r$ we may assume that $v$ is as in \ref{thm:finite}. By \ref{thm:finite} (i), we may treat those $v$ one at a time. We make the same change of variable $z\mapsto z-z_0$ as in 2.7. The equation \eqref{eq:component} gives, for a fixed norm on $V$,
\[
|\sum_j z_j N_j v|\leq A_4 e^{-2\pi\inf(y_j)}.
\]
This implies that
\[
\sum_j z'_j N_j v=0,\quad\text{with}\quad |z'_j-z_j|\leq A_5 e^{-2\pi\inf(y_j)}:
\]
take $z'\mapsto\sum z'_j N_j v$ to be the image of $\sum z_j N_j v$ by a fixed linear section of $\mathbb{C}^r\to V$; $v\mapsto\sum z_j N_j v$. The condition $\sum z'_j N_j v=0$ is equivalent to $v\in\Phi_{un}^0(z')$.

\begin{remark}
The following example shows that the constant $A_1$ in \ref{thm:finite} does depend on $\mathbb{V}$. Consider the case of a variation on $D^*$ that extends to a variation on $D$. The nilpotent orbit is then the constant variation with value $\mathbb{V}_0$. Fix a variation $\mathbb{W}$ on a bigger disk and $v\in\mathbb{W}_{\mathbb{Z}}$ of type $(0,0)$ at $0$ and nowhere else. Translating $\mathbb{W}$ by any small $\varepsilon$, we obtain on $D$ $v\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}$ of bounded norm and type $(0,0)$ at $\varepsilon$, but not at $0$, and hence nowhere for $\mathbb{V}^{un}$.
\end{remark}

Theorem~\ref{thm:finite} is really a statement about what happens when there is a sequence of points $s_{\alpha}\in(D^*)^r$ tending to $0$ in $D^r$ and of $v_{\alpha}$ integral of type $(0,0)$ and of bounded norm.

\subsection{}
\textit{Heuristics for \ref{thm:finite}.} Our method of proof forces us to prove a result more general than \ref{thm:finite}, where the assumption $v\in\Phi^0(z)$ is replaced by the assumption that $v$ is close to $\Phi^0(z)$.

In any hermitian space, given $\alpha$, a quantity $Y$, a nonzero vector $v$ and a subspace $F$, we will write
\[
v\sim_Y F
\]
if the sine of the angle between $v$ and $F$ is bounded by $\exp(-\alpha Y)$, i.e., if $v+w\in F$ with
\[
|w|\leq\exp(-\alpha Y)|v|.
\]
For $z=(z_1,\dots,z_r)$, with imaginary part $y$ we will write $\sim_z$ for $\sim_{\sup(y_i)}$. We will denote by $\|v\|_{\Phi(z)}$ (or, simply, by $\|v\|$ if no ambiguity is possible) the Hodge norm of $v\in V$ at the point $\Phi(z)$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:closeness}
Assume $\mathbb{V}$ of weight $0$. Given $K$ and $\alpha>0$, there is a constant $A_1$ (depending on $K$, $\alpha$, and $\mathbb{V}$) such that
\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)] There are only finitely many $v\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}$ such that, at some point $z$ with $0\leq x_i\leq 1$ and $\inf(y_i)\geq A_1$, $\|v\|_{\Phi(z)}^2\leq K$ and, relative to the Hodge metric at $\Phi(z)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:closeness}
v\sim_z\Phi^0(z)
\end{equation}
\item[(ii)] Any such $v$ is in $W_0$.
\item[(iii)] If a fixed $v$ satisfies (i) at a sequence of points $z$ with $0\leq x_i\leq 1$ and $\inf(y_i)\to\infty$, then $v$ is in $F^0$ for some limiting Hodge filtration $F$.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

\subsection{}
\textit{Remarks.} (i) The proof could be strengthened to show that if $\mathbb{V}$ depends continuously on a parameter $\tau$ varying in a compact space, the constant $A_1$ can be taken independent of $\tau$.

(ii) In \eqref{eq:closeness}, we use the Hodge metric at $\Phi(z)$. We could as well have used a fixed metric. Indeed, the ratio between a fixed metric and the Hodge metric is bounded by a power of $\sup(y_i)$ (see (3.8 (i))) so that for any $\alpha'<\alpha$ and for $\inf(y_i)$ large enough, $v\sim_z\Phi^0(z)$ for $\alpha$ and one metric implies $v\sim_z\Phi^0(z)$ for $\alpha'$ and the other metric.

(iii) The condition $v\sim_z\Phi^0(z)$ implies that the ratio $Q(v,v)/\|v\|_{\Phi(z)}^2$ is close to one. Instead of the condition $\|v\|_{\Phi(z)}^2\leq K$, we could as well have required $Q(v,v)\leq K$.

\subsection{}
To prove \ref{thm:closeness}, one would like to be able to replace $\Phi$ by $\Phi_{un}$. For $\inf(y_i)$ large, $\Phi(z)$ and $\Phi_{un}(z)$ are close--roughly at a distance $\exp(-2\pi\inf(y_i))$. The case of a variation extending across $D^r$ shows that one cannot hope for anything better. On the other hand, $\exp(\sum z_i N_i)$ is of size $\sup(y_i)^k$, $k$ bounded by the rank of $\mathbb{V}$. If the $y_i$ are of wildly different magnitudes, the product
\[
\exp(-2\pi\inf(y_i))\cdot\prod_i\sup(y_i)^{k_i}
\]
need not be small. This leads to difficulties which may be circumvented as follows.

Fix $I\subset[1,r]$, with complement $J$. Assume that $z$ is such that the $y_i$ ($i\in I$) are of comparable size, and much bigger than the $y_j$ ($j\in J$). Let $W^1$ be the filtration attached, as in (2.4), to the elements of the cone
\[
C_I=\Bigl\{\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_i N_i,\; \lambda_i>0\Bigr\}.
\]
If we freeze the variables $z_j$ ($j\in J$), and consider the asymptotic nilpotent orbit of the resulting variations on $(D^*)^I$, we obtain $\Phi_{un,I}(z)$ which is close to $\Phi(z)$, with a proximity controlled by $\inf_{i\in I}(y_i)$. These nilpotent orbits, for the variables $z=(z_1,\dots,z_r)$, fit into a period map $\Phi_{un,I}$. For $z$ as above, a small angle between $v$ and $\Phi^0(z)$, as in \eqref{eq:closeness}, implies a similarly small angle $v$ and $\Phi_{un,I}^0(z)$, as $\inf_{i\in I}(y_i)\sim\sup_{i\in[1,r]}(y_i)$ by assumption.

The first step will be to show that $v$ is in $W^1_0$. This cannot be viewed as a consequence of the variant 2.17(i) of \ref{thm:closeness}(ii) with parameters, for variations on $(D^*)^I$, as the required set $(D^*)^J$ of parameters is non compact. We have to rely on the SL(2)$^r$-orbit theorem \cite{cks}, which controls $\Phi(z)$ in the whole of regions of the form $y_1\geq ay_2$, $y_2\geq ay_3$, \dots, $y_{r-1}\geq ay_r$.

Next, one shows that $(\sum_{i\in I}y_i N_i)(v)$ is small, and this allows to find $z^*$, with the same $y_j$ ($j\in J$) and with $y_i^*$ comparable to $\sup_{j\in J}(y_j)$ such that $v$ is close to $\Phi_{un,I}$ at $z^*$, with a proximity controlled by $\inf_{i\in I}(y_i)\sim\sup_{i\in[1,r]}(y_i)$. Iterating this process, one eventually finds $z^{**}$, with all $y_i^{**}$ comparable to $\inf(y_i)$, such that $v$ is close to $\Phi_{un}^0(z^{**})$ (in the sense of \eqref{eq:closeness}, possibly for a new $\alpha$). The next step gives $v\in W_0$ and $v$ close to $\Phi^0(z^{***})$, with $z^{***}$ bounded and a proximity controlled by $\inf(y_i)$. From this \ref{thm:closeness} follows.

\subsection{}
To ease the handling of quantifiers and estimates, but at the cost of effectivity, we will prove \ref{thm:closeness} by contradiction. If (i) fails, we can find sequences $u(n)$, $z(n)$ with the $(u(n),z(n))$ as in \ref{thm:closeness} (i), the $u(n)$ all distinct and $\inf_i(y(n)_i)\to\infty$. If (ii) fails, we can find sequences as above with each $u(n)$ not in $W_0$. If (i) and (ii) hold, but (iii) fails we can find similar sequences with $u(n)$ constant and $u(n)$ not in $F^0$ for any limiting Hodge filtration $F$. In each case, a subsequence of the offending sequence is again offending. To prove \ref{thm:closeness} by contradiction, it hence suffices to show that given a sequence $(u(n),z(n))$ with $u(n)\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}$, $\|u(n)\|_{\Phi(z(n))}^2\leq K$ and $u(n)\sim_z\Phi^0(z(n))$ in the Hodge norm at $z(n)$, $0\leq x(n)_i\leq 1$, $\inf(y(n)_i)\to\infty$, it has a subsequence for which $u(n)$ is constant, in $W_0$, and in $F^0$ for some limiting Hodge filtration $F$.


\section{Preliminaries}

In 3.2, we comment on what it means and what it takes for a mixed Hodge structure to be close to another. We then recall results of the SL(2)$^r$-theory of \cite{cks} in a form suitable for our purposes.

\subsection{}
Let $V$ be a complex vector space. Fix subspaces $A$, $B$ of $V$. If subspaces $A'$, $B'$ are close to $A$, $B$ in their respective grassmannians, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:semicontinuous}
\dim A'\cap B'\leq\dim A\cap B
\end{equation}
(upper semicontinuity of the map $(A,B)\mapsto\dim A\cap B$). Further, on the space of pairs of subspaces $A,B$ with $A\cap B$ of fixed dimension, the map $(A,B)\mapsto A\cap B$ is continuous.

Indeed, let $A'$, $B'$ be close to $A$, $B$. Some $g\in\GL(V)$, close to the identity maps $A'$ to $A$: we may and shall assume that $A'=A$. Fix a supplement $C_1$ of $A\cap B$ in $A$ and a supplement of $C_2$ of $A+B$ in $V$: for $C=C_1\oplus C_2$, we have $V=B\oplus C$ and $A=(A\cap B)\oplus(A\cap C)$. Being close to $B$, $B'$ is the graph of a map $b':B\to C$ with $B'\mapsto b'$ continuous. An element $u+b'(u)$ of $B'$ is in $A$ if and only if both $u\in B$ and $b'(u)\in C$ are. This requires $u\in A\cap B$ and the assertion follows. If $\dim A\cap B'=\dim A\cap B$, then $A\cap B'$ is the graph of the restriction of $b'$ to $A\cap B$, continuous in $B'$.

Let now $A$, $B$ be finite filtrations of $V$. We take them decreasing. We consider filtrations $A'$, $B'$ with $\dim A^p\cap B^q=\dim A'^p\cap B'^q$ for all $p$, $q$, and show that for such filtrations, if $A'$, $B'$ are close to $A$, $B$ in the respective flag manifolds, then $(A',B')$ is the image of $(A,B)$ by some $g\in\GL(V)$ close to the identity. Arguing as before, we may and shall assume $A=A'$.

Fix a bigrading $C$ splitting the bifiltration $(A,B)$: $A^p$ (resp.\ $B^q$) is the sum of the $C^{ij}$ for $i\geq p$ (resp $j\geq q$). Fix a basis $e_{\alpha}^{pq}$ of the $C^{pq}$. As we assumed that $\dim A^p\cap B^q=\dim A'^p\cap B'^q$, $A'^p\cap B'^q$ is close to $A^p\cap B^q$ and we can find $e'^{pq}_{\alpha}\in A'^p\cap B'^q$ close to $e^{pq}_{\alpha}$. The endomorphism $g:e^{pq}_{\alpha}\mapsto e'^{pq}_{\alpha}$ is close to the identity, hence in $\GL(V)$, and maps $A^p\cap B^q$ into, hence onto $A^p\cap B'^q$. It carries $(A,B)$ to $(A',B')$.

We now apply this to mixed Hodge structures.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:mixedhodge}
Let $(W,F)$ be a mixed Hodge structure on a real vector space $V$. If a filtration $F'$ of $V_{\mathbb{C}}$ is close to $F$ and such that $(W,F')$ is also a mixed Hodge structure, then there is an automorphism $g$ of $V_{\mathbb{C}}$, close to the identity, respecting $W$ and carrying $F$ to $F'$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:semicontinuous}, we may assume that for all $w$, $p$, one has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dims}
\dim W_w\cap F'^p\leq\dim W_w\cap F^p
\end{equation}
and it suffices to prove equality.

We proceed by induction on $w$. If equality in \eqref{eq:dims} holds for $w-1$, from
\[
\dim\Gr_w^W(F^p)=\dim W_w\cap F^p-\dim W_{w-1}\cap F^{p-1},
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grdims}
\dim\Gr_w^W(F'^p)\leq\dim\Gr_w^W(F^p)
\end{equation}
and, because of the inductive hypothesis, equality holds if and only if it holds in \eqref{eq:dims}. Taking the sum of \eqref{eq:grdims} for indices $(w,p)$ and $(w,w-p-1)$, we obtain
\[
\dim\Gr_w^W(F'^p)+\dim\Gr_w^W(F'^{w-p-1})\leq\dim\Gr_w^W(F^p)+\dim\Gr_w^W(F^{w-p-1}).
\]
Both sides equal $\dim\Gr_w^W(V)$, implying equality in \eqref{eq:grdims}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{}
Let $\mathbb{V}$ be a polarized variation of Hodge structures on $(D^*)^r$, with unipotent monodromy, corresponding to a period mapping $\Phi$ on $\mathcal{H}^r$, with values in the filtrations of $V_{\mathbb{C}}$.

Fix $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_d$ in $\mathbb{R}^r$, with $0\leq\theta_{ij}\leq\cdots\leq\theta_{ij}$ and all $\theta_{id}>0$. We want to control $\Phi(z)$ when $z$ tends to infinity in the following way: the real part $x$ is bounded, the imaginary part can be written as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:imexpand}
y=\tau_1\theta_1+\cdots+\tau_d\theta_d+b
\end{equation}
with $b$ bounded, and where, for
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tau}
t_i=\tau_i/\tau_{i+1}\;(1\leq i<d),\quad t_d=\tau_d,
\end{equation}
one has $t_i\to\infty$.

For this, we approximate $\Phi$ by the nilpotent orbit $\Phi_{un}$ and apply \cite[(4.20)]{cks} to the nilpotent orbit
\[
\Phi^*(u_1,\dots,u_d)=\Phi_{un}(\sum u_j\theta_j).
\]
Define $T_j=\theta_j N=\sum_i\theta_{ij}N_i$. Adding $1$ to $u_j$ transforms $\Phi^*$ by $\exp(T_j)$. The $T_j$ are not, in general, rational. In the context of \cite{cks}, where real variations are considered, this does not matter.

\subsection{}
The SL(2,$\mathbb{R}$)$^d$-theory of \cite{cks} approximates period mappings, in suitable sectors, by simpler ones which we begin by describing.

On $D^*$, the family of elliptic curves $\mathbb{C}^*/q^{\mathbb{Z}}$ gives rise, by taking $H^1$, to a variation of Hodge structures of rank $2$ and type $\{(0,1),(1,0)\}$. In a suitable basis of $V_{\mathbb{Z}}$, the corresponding period mapping
\[
\Phi:\mathcal{H}\to\{\text{lines in }\mathbb{R}^2\otimes\mathbb{C}\}
\]
assigns to $z\in\mathcal{H}$ the line spanned by $\begin{pmatrix} z \\ 1\end{pmatrix}$, with
\[
\Phi(z+1)=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\Phi(z).
\]
On $(D^*)^d$, one can then consider real variations direct sum of variations of the following kind:
\[
\pr_j^*\Sym^{n_j}(\mathbb{V}_1)\otimes H
\]
for $H$ a fixed Hodge structure. These are the simpler variations announced. Here is an alternative description of them.

The Hodge structure $\Phi(i)$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ induces a Hodge structure of weight $0$ on $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})\subset\End(\mathbb{R}^2)$. Let $\rho_j$ be the representation of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})^d$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$ via its $j$-th factor. If $A$ is a real Hodge structure, whose underlying real vector space is a representation of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})^d$, and if the representation map
\[
\rho:\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})^d\to\End(A)
\]
is a morphism of Hodge structures, where each $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})$ factor is given the Hodge structure induced by $\Phi(i)$, then, $(A,\rho)$ is isomorphic to a sum of tensor products
\[
\Sym^{n_j}(\rho_j)\otimes H
\]
where $\mathbb{R}^2$--the representation space of $\rho_j$--is given the Hodge structure $\Phi(i)$ and where $H$ is a Hodge structure with trivial action. Indeed, the isomorphism of representations of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})^d$:
\[
\Sym^{n_j}(\rho_j)\otimes_{\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})^d}\Hom_{\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})^d}(\Sym^{n_j}(\rho_j),A)\xrightarrow{\sim} A
\]
is compatible with mixed Hodge structures.

In view of our later applications, we write $\hat{T}_j$ for the image under the representation $\rho$ of the element $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ in the $j$-th factor of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})^d$. For $F$ the Hodge filtration of $A$, the variations we are considering correspond to period maps of the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sl2period}
\Phi(z)=\exp\bigl(\sum_j z_j\hat{T}_j\bigr)F.
\end{equation}
Denoting by $Y_j$ the image of the element $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix}$ in the $j$-th factor of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})^d$, one has for $z$ purely imaginary
\begin{equation}\label{eq:imaginary}
\Phi(iy)=\exp\bigl(-\sum_j\log(y_j)Y_j/2\bigr)F_{\sharp}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fsharp}
F_{\sharp}:=\Phi(i):=\Phi(i,\dots,i).
\end{equation}

\subsection{}
Given a nilpotent orbit $\Phi$ on $\mathcal{H}^d$ and an ordering of the variables, the SL(2,$\mathbb{R}$)$^d$-orbit theorem provides a period mapping $\Phi_{\SL}$ of the type 3.4 which approximates it. Let $T_j$ be the monodromies for $\Phi$:
\[
\Phi(z+e_j)=T_j\Phi(z)
\]
and define $C(j)$ to be the cone
\[
\Bigl\{\sum_{i=1}^j\lambda_i T_i,\; \lambda_i>0\Bigr\}
\]
and $W^j:=W(C(j))$ as in (2.4). Define $t_j=y_j/y_{j+1}$ for $j<d$, and $t_d=y_d$. One has \cite{cks}:

(3.5.1) For $x$ bounded and $t_i\to\infty$, the invariant distance between $\Phi(z)$ and $\Phi_{\SL}(z)$ tends to zero.

(3.5.2) The $Y_j$ define a $\mathbb{Z}^d$ grading of $V$, with $Y_j$ acting as multiplication by $\ell_j$ on $V_{\ell}$, $\ell=(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_d)$. One has
\[
W^j_w=\bigoplus_{\ell_1+\cdots+\ell_j\leq w} V_{\ell}.
\]

(3.5.3) The construction is compatible with tensor products. In particular, $\SL(2,\mathbb{R})^d$ respects the polarization form.

One should beware that $\Phi$ and $\Phi_{\SL}$ don't have the same transformation law for $z_i\mapsto z_i+1$: $\Phi$ is transformed by $\exp(T_i)$, while $\Phi_{\SL}$ is transformed by $\exp(\hat{T}_i)$. The two are related as follows: $\hat{T}_i$ is the degree zero component, for the $Y_j$, $j<i$, of $T_i$. In particular
\begin{equation}\label{eq:t1hat}
T_1=\hat{T}_1.
\end{equation}
In addition, (3.5.2) tells that the monodromy weight filtration $W^j$ is also the monodromy weight filtration for $\sum_j\lambda_j\hat{T}_j$ when all $\lambda_i>0$.

\subsection{}
We now apply this to a period mapping $\Phi$ on $\mathcal{H}^r$, to approximate $\Phi(z)$ where $z$ is as in 3.3. With the notations of 3.3, the distance between the following pairs of Hodge structures tends to zero, when $t_i\to\infty$:
\begin{itemize}
\item[(a)] $\Phi(z)$ and $\Phi_{un}(z)$: by \eqref{eq:distance},
\item[(b)] $\Phi_{un}(z)$ and $\Phi^*(i\tau)$: one has $\Phi^*(i\tau)=\Phi_{un}(\sum_j i\tau_j\theta_j)$; the hyperbolic distance between $z$ and $\sum_j i\tau_j\theta_j$ is in $O(1/\inf(y_i))$ and one uses the distance decreasing property of period maps,
\item[(c)] $\Phi^*(i\tau)$ and its SL(2,$\mathbb{R}$)$^d$-orbit approximation $\Phi_{\SL}$: by (3.5.1).
\end{itemize}
One concludes that the hyperbolic distance between $\Phi(z)$ and $\Phi_{\SL}(i\tau)$ tends to zero.

With the notations of 3.5, we set, as in \eqref{eq:fsharp}, $F_{\sharp}=\Phi_{\SL}(i)$ and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:etau}
e(\tau)=\exp\bigl(\sum_j\log(\tau_j)Y_j/2\bigr).
\end{equation}
It acts by multiplication by $\tau_j^{\ell_j/2}$ on $V_{\ell}$ (notation of (3.5.2)). It respects the polarization form, hence induces an isometry of the period mapping domain. Because of \eqref{eq:imaginary},
\[
e(\tau)\Phi^*_{\SL}(i\tau)=\Phi^*_{\SL}(i)=F_{\sharp}
\]
and, consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:etauconverge}
e(\tau)\Phi(z)\to F_{\sharp}
\end{equation}
for $z$, $\tau$ as in 3.3, $t_i\to\infty$.

\subsection{}
Let $I(j)\subset[1,r]$ be the set of $i$ for which $\theta_{ij}\neq 0$. It is increasing with $j$. Let $C(j)$ be the cone
\[
C(j):=\Bigl\{\sum_{i\in I(j)}\lambda_i N_i,\; \lambda_i>0\Bigr\}
\]
and $W^j$ be the corresponding filtration $W(C(j))$. Those filtrations $W^j$ coincide with those of 3.5, for $\Phi^*(u)$ and the monodromies $T_j=\theta_j N$.

Some of the above results can be expressed just in terms of the $W^j$.

(3.7.1) There is a $\mathbb{Z}^d$-grading $A$, splitting all the $W^j$: $W^j_w$ is the sum of $A_{\ell}$ with $\ell_1+\cdots+\ell_j\leq w$. $A$ may be chosen rational and compatible with the polarization form.

Indeed, (3.5.2) gives one such grading. The statement (3.7.1) is equivalent to the statement that the $W^j$ generate a distributive lattice of subspaces. As the filtrations $W^j_w$ are rational, there exists a rational $\mathbb{Z}^d$-grading $A$ as in (3.7.1). The grading (3.5.2) is in addition compatible with the polarization form: $V_{\ell}$ orthogonal to $V_m$ for $\ell+m=0$.

If $A$, $B$ are two $\mathbb{Z}^d$-gradings as in (3.7.1), $A_{\ell}$ and $B_{\ell}$ are both canonically isomorphic to an iterated graded of $V$ by the $\Gr^{W^j}_{w_j}$, $w_j=\ell_1+\cdots+\ell_j$. Order irrelevant, because the $W^j_w$ generate a distributive lattice. If $g$ is the direct sum of the resulting isomorphisms $A_{\ell}\xrightarrow{\sim}B_{\ell}$, one has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gproperty}
(g-1)(A_{\ell})\subset\bigoplus_m A_m\text{ with }m\neq\ell,\; m_1+\cdots+m_j\leq\ell_1+\cdots+\ell_j.
\end{equation}

If we transport the grading $A$ by the polarization form, viewed as an isomorphism from $V$ to $V^*$, and dualize, we get another grading $A'$, equal to $A$ if and only if $A$ is compatible with the polarization. We have $A'=gA$ where $g$ obeys \eqref{eq:gproperty}. The grading $g^{1/2}A$ is then compatible with the polarization. This shows the existence of rational gradings of the $W^j$, as in (3.7.1), compatible with the polarization form.

Fix any grading $A$ as in (3.7.1) and define $e_A(\tau)$ to be the multiplication by $\tau_j^{\ell_j/2}$ on $A_{\ell}$. If $A_{\ell}=gV_{\ell}$, with $g$ as above, $e_A(\tau)=ge(\tau)g^{-1}$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:etau}
Notations being as above,
\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)] $e_A(\tau)\Phi(z)$ tends to $gF_{\sharp}$.
\item[(ii)] $e_A(\tau)[\text{Hodge metric at }\Phi(z)]$ tends to the transform by $g$ of the Hodge metric at $F_{\sharp}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $A$ the decomposition by the $V_{\ell}$, $g$ is the identity, (i) is \eqref{eq:etauconverge} and (ii) follows as $e(\tau)$ respects the polarization form. In general,
\[
e_A(\tau)=ge(\tau)g^{-1}=g(e(\tau)g^{-1}e(\tau)^{-1})e(\tau)
\]
and, because of \eqref{eq:gproperty}, $e(\tau)g^{-1}e(\tau)^{-1}$ tends to the identity, on each $A_{\ell}$. The proposition follows.
\end{proof}

\subsection{}
\textit{Remark.} (i) Fix a metric $\|\cdot\|_a$. By \ref{prop:etau}(ii), the Hodge metric at $\Phi(z)$ is comparable to the orthogonal direct sum of those metrics, multiplied by $\tau^{\ell/2}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hodgemetric}
\|v\|_{\Phi(z)}^2\sim\sum_{\ell}\tau^{\ell}\|v_{\ell}\|_a^2
\end{equation}
i.e., the ratios of both members of \eqref{eq:hodgemetric} are bounded.

(ii) Suppose $v\in V$ is in $W^1_w$. Let $v_1$ be its image in $\Gr_w^{W^1}$. The Hodge filtration $\Phi_{un}(z)$ induces a Hodge filtration on $\Gr_w^{W^1}$. We want to compare the Hodge norm of $v$ and $v_1$ at $z$. Decompose $V$ as in (3.7.1), the $A_{\ell}$ with $\ell_1=w$ then project to a similar decomposition $B$ of $\Gr_w^{W^1}$. Numbering: $B_{\ell}=0$ if $\ell_1\neq w$, $B_{0,\ell_2,\dots,\ell_d}=B_{w,\ell_2,\dots,\ell_d}$. Applying \eqref{eq:hodgemetric} both to $V$ and $\Gr_w^{W^1}$, we obtain
\[
\|v_1\|_k^2\leq c\tau_1^{-\ell_1/2}\|v\|_k^2
\]
for an appropriate constant $c$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:sl2}
Let $A$ be a real Hodge structure of weight $0$ and a representation of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})$. One assumes that
\[
\rho:\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})\to\End(A)
\]
is a morphism of Hodge structures, with $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})$ being given the Hodge structure of 3.4. Let $W$ be the monodromy weight filtration for $\rho\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$. Then
\[
A_{\mathbb{R}}\cap W_0\cap F^0\subset A_{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$A$ can be decomposed as a direct sum
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decomp}
\bigoplus_n\Sym^n(\mathbb{R}^2)\otimes H_n,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{R}^2$ is the standard representation of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})$, with the Hodge structure of 3.4, and where $H_n$ is a Hodge structure of weight $-n$. For $e,f$ the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$, $\Sym^n(\mathbb{R}^2)$ is the space of homogeneous polynomials of degree $n$ in $e,f$, $P(e,f)$ is in $W_0$ if it is divisible by $f^m$, $m=[(n+1)/2]$ and in $F^p$ if divisible by $(e+if)^p$.

We may and shall assume $A$ reduced to one of the summands \eqref{eq:decomp}. Take $x$ real in $W_0\cap F^0$. Choose a basis $h_{\alpha}$ of $H_{\mathbb{C}}$, compatible with the Hodge decomposition. Write $x=\sum x_{\alpha} h_{\alpha}$. If $h_{\alpha}$ is of type $(p,q)$, $x_{\alpha}$ must be of type $(p,q)$, i.e. a multiple of $(e+if)^p(e\otimes-if_{\alpha})^q$. It can be in $W_0$ only for $x_{\alpha}=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:sl2d}
Let $A$ be a real Hodge structure of weight $0$ and a representation of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})^d$. One assumes that
\[
\rho:\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})^d\to\End(A)
\]
is a morphism of Hodge structures, with $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})$ being given the Hodge structure of 3.4. Let $W^j$ be the monodromy weight filtration for $\rho\circ\Delta_j$, where $\Delta_j$ is the diagonal embedding of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})$ in the first $j$ factors of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})^d$. Then, the intersection
\[
A_{\mathbb{R}}\cap\bigcap_j W^j_0\cap F^0
\]
is contained in the $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})^d$-invariants.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
We proceed by induction on $d$. By \ref{prop:sl2}, the intersection is contained in the invariants of the first factor $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})$. This space $A'$ is a sub Hodge structure of $A$ acted on by $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})^{d-1}$, and \ref{cor:sl2d} follows from the induction assumption applied to $A'$.
\end{proof}


\section{Proof of 2.16}

\subsection{}
In this section we prove \ref{thm:closeness}, and hence \ref{thm:finite}, reasoning by contradiction as explained in 2.19. We fix a sequence $(z(n),u(n))$ with the following properties: $0\leq x(n)_i\leq 1$, $\inf_i y(n)_i\to\infty$, $u(n)\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}$, the Hodge norm $\|u(n)\|$ of $u(n)$ at $z(n)$ is bounded and, for some fixed $\alpha>0$, $u(n)\sim_{z(n)}\Phi^0(z(n))$.

We have to show that for a suitable subsequence,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:const}
u(n)\text{ is constant, and}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:limit}
\text{its constant value is in }W_0\text{ as well as in }F^0\text{ for some limiting Hodge filtration }F.
\end{equation}

Taking a subsequence, we may and shall assume that for suitable $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_d$ in $\mathbb{R}^r$, one has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:yexpand}
y(n)=\tau_1(n)\theta_1+\cdots+\tau_d(n)\theta_d+b(n)
\end{equation}
with $t_j(n):=\frac{\tau_j(n)}{\tau_{j+1}(n)}\to\infty$, $t_d(n)=\tau_d(n)\to\infty$ and $b(n)$ bounded. Then, in addition to \eqref{eq:const} and \eqref{eq:limit}, we will show that, with $v:=u(n)$ and $T_j:=\theta_j N=\sum_i\theta_{ij}N_i$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tjv}
T_j v=0.
\end{equation}
The proof is by induction on $d\geq 1$.

\subsection{}
If $\theta_j$ is a linear combination of preceding $\theta_i$ (this includes the case $j=1$, $\theta_1=0$), \eqref{eq:yexpand} can be replaced by a similar expansion with $\theta_j$ omitted. This lowers $d$ and the claims \eqref{eq:const}, \eqref{eq:limit}, \eqref{eq:yexpand} are unaffected: by induction, we may and shall assume that the $\theta_j$ are linearly independent.

We assumed that $\inf_i y(n)_i$ tends to $\infty$. This means that for each $i$, one of $\theta_{1i},\dots,\theta_{di}$ is nonzero and that the first to be nonzero is positive. The $\theta$'s are not uniquely determined by $y(n)$--only the flag is.

Adding $\theta_1$ to a large enough multiple of $\theta_2$, then to $\theta_3$, \dots, a large enough multiple of $\theta_2$, \dots, we may and shall assume that $\theta_1\geq\theta_2\geq\cdots\geq\theta_d$.

For simplicity of notation, we will reorder the coordinates $z_i$ so that the $i$ for which the $i$th coordinate of $\theta_j$ is not zero form an initial segment $1\leq i\leq a(j)$. We let $W^j$ be the monodromy weight filtration $W(C(j))$, for the cone
\[
C(j)=\Bigl\{\sum_{i=1}^{a(j)}\lambda_i N_i:\lambda_i>0\Bigr\}.
\]
Let $\Phi':=\Phi_{un,[1,a(1)]}$ be the nilpotent orbit in the $z_i$, $i\leq a(1)$, approximating $\Phi$. By 2.3, and the fact that $\inf_{i\leq a(1)}(y(n)_i)$ is comparable to $\sup(y(n)_i)$, we still have $u(n)$ bounded in the $\Phi'$-Hodge norm at $z(n)$, and $u(n)\sim_{z(n)}\Phi'^0(z(n))$. The monodromies, as well as the limiting Hodge filtrations being the same for $\Phi$ and $\Phi'$, we may replace $\Phi$ by $\Phi'$: we may and shall assume that $\Phi$ is a nilpotent orbit in $z_1,\dots,z_{a(1)}$. It follows that $(W^1,\Phi)$ is a mixed Hodge structure.

For each $w$, $\Gr_w^{W^1}$ is a variation of Hodge structures of weight $w$. It is independent of $z_1,\dots,z_{a(1)}$: if $a(1)=r$, it is a constant Hodge structure. If $a(1)<r$, it corresponds to a period map $\Phi_1$ defined on $\mathcal{H}^{[a(1)+1,r]}$. Let $z(n)_1$ be the projection of $z(n)$ on $\mathcal{H}^{[a(1)+1,r]}$. The projection of $\theta_1$ being zero, \eqref{eq:yexpand} projects to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:yproj}
y(n)_1=\tau_2(n)\bar{\theta}_2+\cdots+\tau_d(n)\bar{\theta}_d+\bar{b}(n)
\end{equation}
with $\bar{\cdot}$ denoting projection. This is an expansion like \eqref{eq:yexpand}, but with $d-1$ $\theta$'s. This is one of two mechanisms by which induction will proceed.

We polarize $\Gr_w^{W^1}(V)$ using any rational element $N$ in $C(1)$: it polarizes the mixed Hodge structure, inducing a polarization of the graded.

Our first task is to prove:

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:inW}
For $n$ large enough, $u(n)$ is in $W^1_0$.
\end{proposition}

We say that $u\in V$ is in the position $\ell=(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_d)$ relative to the filtrations $W^j$ if, for $A$ a $\mathbb{Z}^d$-grading splitting the $W^j$, $\ell$ is the largest multi-index, in the lexicographic order, for which the $\ell$-component of $u$ does not vanish. By \eqref{eq:gproperty}, this does not depend on the choice of $A$.

If $u$ is in position $(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_d)$, then it is in $W^1_{\ell_1}$, with a nonzero image $u_1$ in $\Gr_{\ell_1}^{W^1}(V)$, and $u_1$ is in the position $(\ell_2,\dots,\ell_d)$ relative to the filtrations induced by $W^2[\ell_1],\dots,W^d[\ell_1]$, where $W[m]_w=W_{m+w}$.

Those induced filtrations are also the monodromy weight filtrations for the action of any $N\in C(j)$ on $\Gr^{W^1}(V)$: this expresses that $W^j$ is the relative monodromy weight filtration of $W^1$ and any $N\in C(j)$ ([1, (3.3)]).

Taking a subsequence, we may and shall assume that, for a suitable $(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_d)$, all $u(n)$ are in the position $(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_d)$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:nonnegative}
For each $j$, one has $\ell_1+\cdots+\ell_j\geq 0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We will prove by induction on $d$ the following more general statement: one takes $\Phi$ to be a variation of weight $w\geq 0$; $z$, $u$, $\theta$, $\tau$ are as before (except that the $\theta$ are not assumed linearly independent); the $u(n)$ are in position $(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_d)$; one assumes $u(n)\sim_z\Phi^0$ and
\[
\|u(n)\|\leq c\tau_1(n)^{-w/2}.
\]
One claims
\[
w+\ell_1+\cdots+\ell_j\geq 0\text{ for each }j.
\]

If $\theta_j$ is a linear combination of previous $\theta$, we have $W^j=W^{j-1}$ (resp.\ trivial if $j=1$, $=0$) and $\ell_j=0$. Reasoning as in 4.2, we may, using the induction hypothesis, assume that the $\theta$ are linearly independent.

As in 4.2, we may and shall assume that $\Phi$ is a nilpotent orbit in the variables $z_1,\dots,z_{a(1)}$. Let $\Phi_1$ be the period mapping on $\mathcal{H}^{[a(1)+1,r]}$ corresponding to $\Gr_w^{W^1}(V)$; let $u(n)_1$ (weight $w+\ell_1$) be the image of $u(n)$ in this graded.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:induction}
Assumptions and notations being as above,
\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)] $w+\ell_1\geq 0$
\item[(ii)] $\|u(n)_1\|\leq c\tau_1(n)^{-(w+\ell_1)/2}$ (Hodge norm)
\item[(iii)] $u(n)_1\sim_{z_1}\Phi_1^0(z(n)_1)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let us transform $u(n)\sim_z\Phi^0(z(n))$ by $e(\tau(n))$ (notations of 3.6). In the Hodge norm for $e(\tau(n))\Phi(z(n))$, we continue to have
\[
e(\tau(n))u(n)\sim_z e(\tau(n))\Phi^0(z(n)).
\]
The subspace on the right tends to $F_{\sharp}^0$. Any limiting value of the ray spanned by the real vector $e(\tau(n))u(n)$ is hence in
\[
W^1_{w+\ell_1}\cap F_{\sharp}^0\cap\bar{F}_{\sharp}^0.
\]
As $(W^1[w],F_{\sharp})$ is a mixed Hodge structure, this intersection can be non zero only for $w+\ell_1\geq 0$. This proves (i).

Comparing the asymptotics of the Hodge norm for $V$ and for $\Gr^{W^1}$, one finds (cf.\ 3.9) that for any $v\in W^1_{\ell}$, with image $v_1$ in the graded, one has at $z(n)$
\[
\|v_1\|_k^2\leq c\tau_1(n)^{-\ell_1}\|v\|_k^2,
\]
proving (ii).

For (iii), we will consider angles in a fixed metric. As explained in 2.17(ii), we still have $u(n)\sim_z\Phi^0$, and it suffices to prove $u(n)_1\sim_{z_1}\Phi_1^0$, in this new sense.

Fix $z_0$ with big enough imaginary part so that $(W^1,\Phi_{un}(z_0))$ is a mixed Hodge structure. As $\exp(\sum z(n)_i N_i)$ is bounded by some $\sup(y(n)_i)^k$, we have
\[
\exp(\sum(z(n)-z_0)N_i)u(n)\sim_z\exp(\sum(z(n)-z_0)N_i)\Phi^0(z(n)).
\]
The filtration $\exp(\sum(z(n)-z_0)N_i)\Phi(z(n))$ tends to $\Phi_{un}(z_0)$. Together with $W^1$, both filtrations define a mixed Hodge structure. It follows that some complex endomorphism $\gamma_n$ tending to $1$, respects $W^1$, transforms the latter into the former, and satisfies
\[
v(n):=\gamma_n^{-1}\exp(\sum(z(n)-z_0)N_i)u(n)\sim_z\Phi_{un}^0(z_0).
\]
As $v(n)\in W^1_{\ell_1}$, one also has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vn}
v(n)\sim_z W^1_{\ell_1}\cap\Phi_{un}^0(z_0).
\end{equation}
The projection $v(n)_1$ of $v(n)$ in $\Gr_{\ell_1}^{W^1}$ is the transform by $\gamma_n^{-1}\exp(\sum(z(n)-z_0)N_i)$ of a nonzero element of $\Gr_{\ell_1}^{W^1}(V_{\mathbb{Z}})$. Its size is hence at least like $c\sup(y(n)_i)^{-k}$, while that of $v(n)$ is at most like $c\sup(y(n)_i)^k$. It follows that the exponential closeness \eqref{eq:vn} continues to hold mod $W^1_{\ell_1-1}$:
\[
v(n)_1\sim_{z_1}\Phi_{un}^0(z_0)\text{ in }\Gr_{\ell_1}^{W^1}(V).
\]
Applying $\exp(\sum(z(n)-z_0)N_i)\gamma_n$, we get 4.5(iii).
\end{proof}

\noindent\textit{Proof of \ref{lem:nonnegative}:} If $a(1)=r$, one has $W^j=W^1$ for all $j$, $\ell_j=0$ for $j\geq 2$ and \ref{lem:nonnegative} follows from \ref{lem:induction}(i). If $a(1)<r$, we apply the induction hypothesis to $\Phi_1$, of weight $w+\ell_1\geq 0$ (cf.\ \ref{lem:induction}(i)), to $u(n)_1$ and to $z(n)_1$ which has an expansion like \eqref{eq:yexpand}, with $d-1$ $\theta$'s. By \ref{lem:induction} (ii), (iii), the required estimates hold.

\textit{Proof of \ref{prop:inW}:} Fix a rational decomposition $V_{\mathbb{Z}}=\bigoplus A_{\ell}$ as in (3.7.1). The projection of $V_{\mathbb{Z}}$ in $A_{\ell}$ is a lattice. By \eqref{eq:hodgemetric}, it follows that if $u(n)$ has a nonzero projection in $A_a$, then
\[
\|v(n)\|^2\gg\tau_1(n)^{a_1}\tau_2(n)^{a_2}\cdots\tau_d(n)^{a_d}.
\]
Take $a=(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_d)$. By definition, the projection of $v(n)$ is nonzero. One has
\[
\tau_1(n)^{\ell_1}\cdots\tau_d(n)^{\ell_d}=\frac{\tau_1(n)^{\ell_1}}{\tau_2(n)^{\ell_1}}\cdot\frac{(\tau_1(n)\tau_2(n))^{\ell_1+\ell_2}}{\tau_3(n)^{\ell_1+\ell_2}}\cdots\tau_d(n)^{\ell_1+\cdots+\ell_d}.
\]
As $\|v(n)\|$ is bounded, we conclude that
\[
\ell_1=\ell_2=\cdots=\ell_d=0.
\]
In particular, $\ell_1=0$, proving \ref{prop:inW}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{}
We now apply the induction hypothesis to $\Gr_0^{W^1}(V)$, corresponding to a period mapping $\Phi_1$, and to the projection $u(n)_1$ of $u(n)$ in $\Gr_0^{W^1}(V)$.

If $a(1)=r$, the Hodge structure $\Gr_0^{W^1}(V)$ is constant. By \ref{lem:induction}(ii), or directly by 3.9(ii), $u(n)_1$ is bounded. Being in a lattice, it can take only a finite number of values. We may and shall assume it is constant and define $u^1:=u(n)_1$. As $u^1\sim_{z_1}\Phi^0$, one has $u^1\in F^0$.

If $a(1)<r$, the induction hypothesis applies by \eqref{eq:yproj} and \ref{lem:induction}. Again, we find that $u(n)_1$ may and shall be assumed to have a constant value $u^1$. By \eqref{eq:tjv}, $u^1$ is killed by $T_j$ ($j\geq 2$) and in particular sits in the $W^j_0$ ($j\geq 2$). It is also in $F^0$ for some limiting Hodge filtration $F$ (all this in $\Gr_0^{W^1}(V)$).

$T_1$ maps $W^1_0$ to $W^1_{-2}$, inducing a morphism of Hodge structures
\begin{equation}\label{eq:t1morphism}
\Gr_0^{W^1}(V)\to\Gr_{-2}^{W^1}(V)(-1).
\end{equation}
We next prove

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:kernel}
$u^1$ is in the kernel of the morphism \eqref{eq:t1morphism}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If we transform the assumption
\[
u(n)\sim_z\Phi^0(z(n))
\]
by $e(\tau(n))$ (notations of 3.6.1), we obtain
\[
e(\tau(n))u(n)\sim_z e(\tau(n))\Phi^0(z(n))
\]
where angles are now taken in the Hodge metric for $e(\tau(n))\Phi(z(n))$. The Hodge norm of $e(\tau(n))u(n)$ in this metric --equal to that of $u(n)$ in the Hodge metric for $\Phi(z(n))$-- is bounded by assumption. The $e(\tau(n))\Phi(z(n))$ tend to the Hodge filtration $F_{\sharp}$ (cf.\ 3.6). The Hodge metric for $e(\tau(n))\Phi(z(n))$ tends to that for $F_{\sharp}$. It follows that $e(\tau(n))u(n)$ remains bounded and, taking a subsequence, we may and shall assume that it has a limit $u_0$. We have
\[
u_0\in F_{\sharp}^0.
\]
Consider the decomposition $V=\bigoplus A_{\ell}$: $e(\tau)$ acts on $A_{\ell}$ as multiplication by $\tau^{\ell/2}$. By \ref{prop:sl2}, $u_0$ is in the sum of the $A_{\ell}$ with $\ell_1=0$ and is in the kernel of $T_1$.

The components $u(n)(\ell)$ of $u(n)$, for $\ell_1=0$, depend only on $u^1$: they are independent of $n$. That $u^1$ is killed by the $T_j$ ($j\geq 2$) implies it is in the $W^j_0$ and that, for $\ell_1=0$, $u(n)(\ell)$ can be nonzero only for $\ell_2+\cdots+\ell_j\leq 0$.

The $V_{\ell}$ with $\ell_1=2$ project to a decomposition of $\Gr_{-2}^{W^1}(V)$. By \eqref{eq:t1hat}, \ref{prop:kernel} and the fact that $u^1$ is in $W^j_0$, $T_1 u^1$ has a nonzero component in $V_{\ell}$ ($\ell_1=2$) only when $(\ell_2,\dots,\ell_d)=(0,\dots,0)$, $\ell_1+\cdots+\ell_j\leq 0$.

The $e(\tau(n))\Phi(z(n))$ belong to a compact family of Hodge filtrations $F$, for which $(W^1,F)$ is mixed Hodge. By 3.2, $e(\tau(n))u(n)$ is a sum $\chi+\varepsilon$, $\chi$ in $F$, $\|\varepsilon\|\leq\exp(-\alpha\sup_i y(n)_i)$. By compacity, or 3.2, $\chi$ can be taken in $W^1_0$. Apply $T_1$; by \eqref{eq:t1hat},
\[
e(\tau(n))T_1 u(n)=\tau_1(n)^{-1}T_1(e(\tau(n))u(n))=\tau_1(n)^{-1}(T_1\chi+T_1\varepsilon)
\]
and we find that $T_1 u^1$ is sum of an element in $\Phi^{-1}(z(n))$ plus an exponentially small term. Passing to the limit, we obtain
\[
T_1 u^1\in F_{\sharp}^{-1},
\]
where $F_{\sharp}^{-1}$ is the filtration in $\Gr_{-2}^{W^1}(V)$ induced by $F_{\sharp}^{-1}$. If we go to $\Gr_{-2}^{W^1}(V)(-2)$, to have a Hodge structure of weight zero, this reads
\[
T_1 u^1\in F_{\sharp}^1(-2)^0.
\]
The action of the individual factors of SL(2,$\mathbb{R}$)$^d$ of index $\neq 1$, and the filtration $F_{\sharp}(-2)$ on $\Gr_{-2}^{W^1}(V)(-2)$, are of the type considered in \ref{cor:sl2d}. By \ref{cor:sl2d}, $T_1 u^1$ is in $V^{-2,0,\dots,0}$, hence it is zero--as the $(-2,0,\dots,0)$ component has been shown to vanish. This proves \ref{prop:kernel}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:small}
$T_1 u(n)$ is exponentially small: of Hodge norm $<<\exp(-\beta\sup_i(y(n)_i))$ for suitable $\beta$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \ref{prop:kernel}, $T_1 u(n)$ is in $W^1_{-3}$. It is also the sum of $\chi$ in $\Phi^{-1}(z(n))$ and of an exponentially small $\varepsilon$. The same holds after applying $e(\tau(n))$. We gain that the $e(\tau(n))\Phi(z(n))$ belong to a compact family of filtrations $F$, for which
\[
W_{-3}^1\cap F^{-1}\cap\bar{F}^{-1}=0,
\]
so that $e(\tau(n))T_1 u(n)$ must be exponentially small. Hence so is $T_1 u(n)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{}
We now complete the proof of \ref{thm:closeness}. With angles measured using a fixed metric, we have
\[
u(n)\sim_z\Phi^0(z(n)).
\]
Applying $\exp(-\sum_i\tau_1(n)T_i)$, of polynomial size in $\sup(y(n)_i)$:
\[
\exp(-\sum_i\tau_1(n)T_i)u(n)\sim_z\Phi^0(z(n)-i\tau_1(n)\theta_1)
\]
and, by \ref{prop:small},
\[
u(n)\sim_z\Phi^0(z(n)-i\tau_1(n)\theta_1).
\]
If $d=1$, we choose the expansion \eqref{eq:yexpand} (subtracting a constant to $\tau_1(n)$) so that $y_j(n)-\tau_1(n)\theta_{1j}\geq A>0$. As $u(n)$ is close to $\Phi^0$ at $z(n)$ and at $z(n)-i\tau_1(n)\theta_1$, its Hodge norm at both places is close to $Q(u(n),u(n))^{1/2}$ and hence is bounded. Moreover, $z(n)-i\tau_1(n)\theta_1$ remains bounded and the corresponding Hodge filtrations remain in a compact set. Being bounded and integral, $u(n)$ can take only finitely many values. Taking a subsequence for which $z(n)-i\tau_1(n)\theta_1$ tends to a limit, we find that $u$ is in the corresponding $\Phi^0$. It is in $W_0$ by \ref{prop:inW}. This proves \eqref{eq:limit}, while \eqref{eq:tjv} results from \ref{prop:kernel}.

Assume now $d>1$. The imaginary part of $z(n)-i\tau_1(n)\theta_1$ is
\[
\tau_2(n)\theta_2+\cdots+\tau_d(n)\theta_d+b(n),
\]
an expansion as in \eqref{eq:yexpand}, but with only $d-1$ $\theta$'s. Applying the induction assumption, we see that, on a subsequence, $u(n)$ satisfies \eqref{eq:const} and \eqref{eq:limit} and is killed by the $T_j$ ($j>1$). Being constant, it is also killed by $T_1$ (cf.\ \ref{prop:small}). This finishes the proof. \qed
