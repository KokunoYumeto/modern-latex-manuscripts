\section*{\S0 Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{\S0 Introduction}

The purpose of this paper is to interpolate $p$-adically the values at negative integers of abelian $L$-functions for totally real fields, by using the method of Hilbert modular forms. We recall that the $p$-adic study of $L$-values by means of $p$-adic modular forms was initiated by Serre in his Antwerp paper [33] and was suggested by Siegel's use of Eisenstein series to prove the rationality of $L$-values [38]. Serre used modular forms of one variable in his theory, and constructed $p$-adic zeta functions, as well as $p$-adic $L$-functions for powers of the Teichm\"uller character, over totally real fields. Soon after, Katz (unpublished) and Queen [27] observed that Serre's theory applied more generally to the construction of $p$-adic $L$-functions attached to abelian characters over totally real fields. Here the point was to introduce $p$-adic modular forms with level, i.e., on $\Gamma_0(N)$. Already in Serre's paper, it was clear that the use of forms of one variable was insufficient for a complete theory. For example, one could not rule out the possibility of a pole at $s=1$ for the $p$-adic $L$-functions attached to certain non-trivial powers of the Teichm\"uller character. At the close of [33], Serre suggested that one develop a theory of $p$-adic Hilbert modular forms to overcome these difficulties.

This problem was taken up by the first author of this paper, who described in letters to Serre [5] how the required congruences among $L$-values would follow from a (conjectural) theory of $p$-adic Hilbert forms. This theory was in turn seen as a consequence of a construction over $\Z$ of certain Hilbert-Blumenthal moduli schemes, whose fibres in characteristic $p$ were further conjectured to be geometrically irreducible. The geometric irreducibility amounted to the surjectivity of a certain Galois representation, and was already known in the case of one-variable modular forms (i.e., with the totally real field taken to be $\Q$) [12].

The second author entered the picture by proving (in 1974) the conjectured irreducibility from the Galois-representation point of view [29]. Strictly speaking, the schemes being proved irreducible did not yet exist.

In 1976, however, Rapoport constructed in his thesis [28] the schemes in question. The work of Rapoport contains the foundational material necessary to the theory of $p$-adic Hilbert forms, and in particular the all-important $q$-expansion principle. Thus, among other things, Rapoport gave algebraic meaning to the $q$-expansion of a Hilbert modular form, so that one could define the $q$-expansion of a Hilbert modular form over an arbitrary ring $R$, at an arbitrary unramified cusp over $R$. The $q$-expansion principle then states that each $q$-expansion of a Hilbert modular form $F$ over a ring $R$ completely determines that form, and furthermore that $F$ is a form over a subring $R_0$ of $R$ if and only if the $q$-expansion coefficients of $F$ lie in $R_0$. Rapoport's thesis completed the program begun by Serre and Deligne and thus removed the last tangible obstacle to the writing of the present paper.

Before summarizing the contents of this paper, we wish to mention related work by other authors. First of all, the ``moduli'' approach to modular forms and its application to the construction of $p$-adic $L$-functions has been developed in a series of papers by Katz [15--19]. Especially, in [19], Katz uses the theory of $p$-adic Hilbert modular forms in a construction of ``$2n$-variable'' $p$-adic $L$-functions for CM fields. His paper begins with an excellent summary of the theory, on which our \S5 is modeled.

Secondly, Barsky [1] and Cassou-Nogu\`es [2] have given alternate approaches to $p$-adic $L$-functions over totally real fields, based on the explicit formulas of Shintani [37]. The results of Barsky/Cassou-Nogu\`es and those of the present paper were compared in [30]. The two sets of results are nearly identical; the only difference between them is that we obtain certain ``extra'' 2-adic divisibilities in the presence of functions with parity, which have not yet been proven by the method which uses Shintani's formulas.

As we have already suggested, our method for studying $L$-values is based on a general theorem about $p$-adic Hilbert modular forms. It states, roughly speaking, that such a form which at one cusp has a $p$-adically integral $q$-expansion will have a $p$-adically integral $q$-expansion at every cusp. We apply this theorem to certain linear combinations of Eisenstein series and thus obtain integrality statements for corresponding linear combinations of $L$-values. These integrality statements include, for example, the ``axioms'' introduced by Coates in [3] and therefore lead in particular to the construction of $p$-adic $L$-functions over totally real fields.

For the convenience of the reader, we now briefly state our main results concerning modular forms and integrality of $L$-values. We then explain how these relate to $p$-adic $L$-functions.

\subsection*{$q$-Expansions of Hilbert Modular Forms}

Let $K$ be a totally real field, which for simplicity we choose different from the rational field. Let $r=[K:\Q]$ be its degree. Let
\[
\mathfrak{H}=\{\tau\in K\otimes\C\mid\Im\tau\gg0\}
\]
be the usual Hilbert upper half plane associated to $K$. Let $k$ be a non-negative integer. We define an action $|_k$ of $\SL(2,K\otimes\R)$ on the space of $\C$-valued functions $F(\tau)$ on $\mathfrak{H}$ by the usual formula
\[
(F|_k\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix})(\tau)=\mathcal{N}(c\tau+d)^{-k}F\Big(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\Big),
\]
in which $\mathcal{N}:K\otimes\C\to\C$ denotes the norm.

We now let $N$ denote a positive integer. Let $\Gamma_0(N)$ denote the subgroup of $\SL(2,K)$ consisting of those matrices $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ which satisfy the conditions:
\[
a,d\in1+N\OO,\quad b\in\DD^{-1},\quad c\in N\DD,
\]
where $\OO$ and $\DD$ denote respectively the integer ring and the different of $K$. A (Hilbert) modular form of weight $k$ on $\Gamma_0(N)$ is a holomorphic function $F:\mathfrak{H}\to\C$ such that
\[
F|_kM=F
\]
for each $M\in\Gamma_0(N)$.

The condition $K\neq\Q$ implies that $F$ is ``holomorphic at infinity,'' as is well known. Since $F$ is invariant under translations $\tau\mapsto\tau+b$ ($b\in\DD^{-1}$), we may expand $F$ as a Fourier series
\[
c(0)+\sum_{\substack{\mu\in\DD^{-1}\\\mu\gg0}}c(\mu)q^{\mu},
\]
where
\[
q^{\mu}=e^{2\pi i\cdot\tr(\mu\cdot\tau)}.
\]
This series is the standard $q$-expansion of $F$. To obtain the others, it is convenient to introduce the action on $F$ of the group $\SL(2,\A)$, where $\A$ is the ring of finite adeles of $K$. For this, let $\bar{\Gamma}$ be the closure in $\SL(2,\A)$ of the group $\Gamma=\Gamma_0(N)$. Using the strong approximation theorem for $\SL(2)$, we write
\[
\SL(2,\A)=\bar{\Gamma}\cdot\SL(2,K).
\]
Given $M\in\SL(2,\A)$, we write $M=M_1M_2$, with $M_1\in\bar{\Gamma}$ and $M_2\in\SL(2,K)$. We then define $F|_kM$ to be $F|_kM_2$. For each invertible element $\alpha$ of $\A$ (i.e., for each ``finite idele'') we let
\[
F_{\alpha}=F\Big|_k\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&0\\0&\alpha^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.
\]
We may write $F_{\alpha}$ as a Fourier series
\[
c(0,\alpha)+\sum_{\substack{\mu\text{ totally positive}\\\mu\in(\alpha)^2\DD^{-1}}}c(\mu,\alpha)q^{\mu},
\]
where the sum is restricted to totally positive elements of $K$ which lie in the square of the ideal $(\alpha)$ of $K$ ``generated'' by $\alpha$\footnote{We recall that for $x$ an invertible element of $K$, we denote by $(x)$ the ideal ``generated'' by $x$.}. We call this series the $q$-expansion of $F$ at the cusp determined by $\alpha$. We are interested in comparing the various $q$-expansions of a form, or of a collection of forms. For example, we recall the following result of Rapoport [28]:

\begin{theorem}[(0.1)]
Suppose that the $q$-expansion coefficients of $F$ at one cusp are rational numbers, i.e., that there is an $\alpha\in K^*$ such that $c(\mu,\alpha)\in\Q$ for all $\mu$. Then the $q$-expansion coefficients of $F$ are rational for each $\alpha\in K^*$.
\end{theorem}

We now consider forms $F_k$ ($k\ge0$) on $\Gamma_0(N)$ of respective weights $0,1,2,\dots$, all but finitely many of which are zero. We assume that each form satisfies the rationality condition of (0.1). For each $k$ and each $\alpha\in K^*$, let $F_{k,\alpha}$ be the $q$-expansion of $F_k$ at the cusp determined by $\alpha$. We view these $q$-expansions as formal power series over $\Q$, in the variables $q^{\mu}$ (with $\mu=0$ or $\mu$ totally positive). Let $p$ be a prime. For $\alpha\in K^*$, the $p$-th component $\alpha_p$ of $\alpha$ is the image of $\alpha$ in $K\otimes\Q_p$; its norm $\mathcal{N}\alpha_p$ is an element of $\Q_p$. When $\alpha\in K^*$, the sum
\[
S(\alpha)=\sum_{k\ge0}\mathcal{N}\alpha_p^{k}\cdot F_{k,\alpha}
\]
is thus a formal power series with coefficients in $\Q_p$.

\begin{theorem}[(0.2)]
If $S(\alpha)$ has coefficients in $\Z_p$ for one $\alpha$, then $S(\alpha)$ has coefficients in $\Z_p$ for each $\alpha$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[(0.3)]
Let $\alpha$ be such that $S(\alpha)$ has $\Z_p$ coefficients, except possibly for its constant coefficient. Then for all $\beta\in K^*$, $S(\beta)$ again has non-constant coefficients in $\Z_p$, and the difference between the constant coefficients of $S(\alpha)$ and $S(\beta)$ lies in $\Z_p$.
\end{corollary}

These assertions are special cases of (5.13), (5.14) as explained in (5.15).

\subsection*{Kummer Congruences}

When the $F_k$ are Eisenstein series, (0.3) yields congruences among $L$-values. We now state the principal such congruence (cf.~(8.2)). Let $K$ and $p$ be as above, and let $\ff$ be a non-zero integral ideal of $K$. Let $G_{\ff}$ be the strict ray class group of $K$ mod $\ff$. Let $G$ be the ray class group of conductor $\ff p^{\infty}$, corresponding via class field theory to the largest abelian extension of $K$ which is unramified at all finite places of $K$ prime to $\ff p$. When $\varepsilon$ is a complex-valued function on $G_{\ff}$, we define as usual
\[
L(s,\varepsilon)=\sum_{\mathfrak{a}}\varepsilon(\mathfrak{a})\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{a}^{-s}\qquad(\Res>1),
\]
taking the sum over prime-to-$\ff$ integral ideals of $K$. One knows that $L(s,\varepsilon)$ may be continued to a meromorphic function on $\C$, holomorphic except for a possible simple pole at $s=1$. In particular, for each integer $k\ge1$, values $L(1-k,\varepsilon)$ are defined.

According to a fundamental theorem of Siegel [38], the association $\varepsilon\mapsto L(1-k,\varepsilon)$ is rational in the sense that $L(1-k,\varepsilon)$ is a rational number whenever $\varepsilon$ is $\Q$-valued. This being the case, we define by linearity values $L(1-k,\varepsilon)\in V$ for each function $\varepsilon$ on $G_{\ff}$ with values in a $\Q$-vector space $V$. This construction applies especially when $V$ is a field of characteristic zero, for example $\Q_p$.

We now let $\mathcal{N}:G\to\Z_p^*$ be that continuous character whose value on the image in $G$ of a prime-to-$\ff p$ ideal is its norm. For $c\in G$, $k\ge1$, and $\varepsilon$ a $\Q_p$-valued function on $G_{\ff}$, we define
\[
\Delta_c(1-k,\varepsilon)=L(1-k,\varepsilon)-\mathcal{N}c^k\cdot L(1-k,\varepsilon_c)\in\Q_p,
\]
where $\varepsilon_c$ is the function $g\mapsto\varepsilon(cg)$ and the product $cg$ is computed in $G_{\ff}$. Let $\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,\dots$ be a sequence of such functions, only finitely many of which are non-zero. For $\mathfrak{x}$ a prime-to-$\ff$ ideal, set
\[
\varphi(\mathfrak{x})=\sum_{k\ge1}\varepsilon_k(\mathfrak{x})\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{x}^{k-1}\in\Q_p.
\]

\begin{theorem}[(0.4)]
Suppose that $\varphi(\mathfrak{x})\in\Z_p$ for each prime-to-$\ff$ ideal $\mathfrak{x}$. Then for all $c\in G$, we have $\Delta\in\Z_p$, where
\[
\Delta=\sum_{k\ge1}\Delta_c(1-k,\varepsilon_k).
\]
\end{theorem}

Our main theorem (8.2) is equivalent to (0.4). We prove also some further assertions concerning the case where $p=2$ and the $\varepsilon_k$ satisfy certain parity conditions. Briefly, if $p=2$ and if $\varepsilon_k$ is an odd (resp.~even) function when $k$ is odd (resp.~even), then $\Delta\in2\Z_2$. We may furthermore give necessary and sufficient conditions for the divisibility of $\Delta$ by $2^r$. (See (8.11), (8.12).)

\subsection*{$p$-adic $L$-functions}

It is now well known that the integrality theorem (0.4) may be used to construct $p$-adic $L$-functions [3, 30, 34]. In the above situation, we take $\ff$ to be divisible by each prime of $K$ lying over $p$. Whenever $\ff'$ is an ideal divisible by $\ff$ and dividing $(\ff p)^n$ for some $n\ge1$, the ideals of $K$ prime to $\ff'$ are just those prime to $\ff$. Hence for $\varepsilon$ a locally constant function on $G$ with values in a $\Q$-vector space $V$, we may unambiguously define values $L(1-k,\varepsilon)\in V$ by selecting an $\ff'$ such that $\varepsilon$ factors through $G\to G_{\ff'}$, and applying the above procedure, with $\ff$ replaced by $\ff'$. We may thus view the functions
\[
\varepsilon\mapsto L(1-k,\varepsilon)\qquad(k\ge1)
\]
as $\Q$-valued distributions on $G$. For $k\ge1$ and $c\in G$, the map
\[
\mu_{c,k}:\varepsilon\mapsto\Delta_c(1-k,\varepsilon)
\]
is then a $\Q_p$-valued distribution on $G$.

From (0.4), we immediately obtain (cf.~[30, 4.1])

\begin{theorem}[(0.5)]
The distribution $\mu_{c,k}$ is in fact a measure with values in $\Z_p$. We have the formula\footnote{In this formula, the right-hand member represents the product of the function $\mathcal{N}^{k-1}$ and the measure $\mu_{c,1}$. Such products are to be distinguished from the (convolution) product of two measures.}
\[
\mu_{c,k}=\mathcal{N}^{k-1}\cdot\mu_{c,1}.
\]
\end{theorem}

Following a normalization of Serre, we put $\Delta_c=\mathcal{N}^{-1}\mu_{c,1}$ for $c\in G$. For $k\ge1$ and for $\varepsilon$ locally constant, we have
\[
\int\varepsilon\mathcal{N}^{k-1}d\Delta_c=\Delta_c(1-k,\varepsilon).
\]
As in [34], we regard the various measures $\Delta_c$ as elements of the ring $\Lambda=\Lambda_G$ of $\Z_p$-valued measures on $G$; this ring may alternately be described as a sort of completed group ring $\Z_p[[G]]$. Choose $c$ so that $1-c$ is not a zero-divisor in $\Lambda$ and let
\[
\lambda_c=(1-c)^{-1}\Delta_c
\]
in the total quotient ring of $\Lambda$. One sees easily that $(1-c')\lambda_c=\Delta_{c'}$ for $c'\in G$, so that $\lambda_c$ is a pseudo-measure in the sense of [34]. With $k$ and $\varepsilon$ as above, the integral against $\lambda_c$ of $\varepsilon\mathcal{N}^{k-1}$ is $L(1-k,\varepsilon)$. We thus obtain a result cited by Serre in his discussion of $L$-values [34, 3.5]. As Serre notes, one now obtains the usual $p$-adic $L$-functions of $K$ (attached to characters whose conductors divide powers of $\ff$) by integrating suitable characters against $\lambda_c$. (See also [30, \S4].)

The plan of this paper is as follows. \S1 begins by recalling the Kummer congruences in the case $K=\Q$. Although we do not prove these congruences here, they may easily be derived from explicit expressions for values $L(1-k,\varepsilon)$ in terms of Bernoulli polynomials. Our aim is to motivate what follows by reinterpreting the congruences in terms of measures on $\Z$ and on $\Q$. In \S2 and \S3, we study the analogues $A$ and $I$ of these two spaces for the case of an arbitrary totally real field $K\neq\Q$. Especially, we prove a functional equation for $L$-functions which involves a somewhat curious Fourier transform for functions on $I$.

In the next two \S\S, we prove an irreducibility theorem for the Hilbert-Blumenthal moduli problem and give applications to $p$-adic modular forms. We obtain (0.2), in particular. Then in \S6 and \S7 we construct Eisenstein series and theta series for the Hilbert group and compute their $q$-expansions. From (0.3) we can then obtain information about $L$-values. In the final \S\ we make explicit this information and use it to prove (0.4) and its 2-adic refinements. For this, we work with measures on the space $I$.

\section{Review of Kummer Congruences over $\Q$}

Let $\varepsilon$ be a complex valued function defined on the set $\Z/f\Z$ of integers mod $f$. Viewing $\varepsilon$ as a function on $\Z$, periodic mod $f$, we set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L-def}
L(s,\varepsilon)=\sum_{n\ge1}\varepsilon(n)n^{-s}
\end{equation}
for $s\in\C$ of real part $\Res>1$. As is well known, this $L$-function may be continued to a meromorphic function of $s$, analytic except for a possible simple pole at $s=1$. We are interested in its values at the non-negative integral points $s=1-k$ with $k\ge1$.

Define Bernoulli polynomials $B_k(x)\in\Q[x]$, $k\ge0$, by the formal expansion
\[
\frac{ze^{xz}}{e^z-1}=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{B_k(x)}{k!}z^k.
\]

\begin{theorem}[(1.2)]\label{thm:bernoulli}
For $k\ge1$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L-bernoulli}
L(1-k,\varepsilon)=-\frac{f^{k-1}}{k}\sum_{t\in\Z/f\Z}\varepsilon(t)B_k\Big(\frac{t}{f}\Big).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{example}
If $k=1$, the theorem gives
\[
L(0,\varepsilon)=-\sum_{t\in\Z/f\Z}\varepsilon(t)\Big(\frac{t}{f}\Big).
\]
In the special case where $\sum\varepsilon(t)=0$, the value $L(0,\varepsilon)$ is thus the Cesaro sum of the series $\sum\varepsilon(t)$: we have
\[
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\varepsilon(t)=-\sum_{t=1}^{f}\varepsilon(t)\Big(\frac{t}{f}\Big)=\frac{1}{f}\sum_{t=1}^{f}t\varepsilon(t).
\]
The general formula for $L(0,\varepsilon)$ may be recovered from this special one after one knows that $\zeta(0)=-1/2$, where $\zeta$ is the Riemann zeta function. This latter fact is just Euler's formula $\sum_{n\ge1}1=0$, as we see by rewriting the sum as $1+2\sum_{n\ge1}1=1+2\zeta(0)$.
\end{example}

Discussions of the analytic continuation of $L(s,\varepsilon)$ and its evaluation at negative integers may be found for example in [11; I, pp.72--78], [13, 23, 42].

Since the numbers $B_k(\frac{t}{f})$ are rational, \eqref{eq:L-bernoulli} may be used as the definition of values $L(1-k,\varepsilon)$ for $\varepsilon$ now a function mod $f$ with values in an arbitrary $\Q$-vector space $V$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L-rational}
L(1-k,\varepsilon)=-\frac{f^{k-1}}{k}\sum_{t\in\Z/f\Z}\varepsilon(t)B_k\Big(\frac{t}{f}\Big).
\end{equation}
Furthermore, we can unambiguously define such values for $\varepsilon$ a periodic function $\Z\to V$ (i.e.~a function defined mod $f$ for some $f$) whose modulus of periodicity $f$ is not specified along with $\varepsilon$. [To verify this it suffices to examine the case $V=\Q$ and thus \textit{a fortiori} it is sufficient to treat the case $V=\C$; then one simply observes that the number $f$ does not intervene in \eqref{eq:L-def}.] In other terms, \eqref{eq:L-rational} defines for a given $k\ge1$ a $\Q$-linear map
\[
\varepsilon\mapsto L(1-k,\varepsilon)
\]
from (periodic functions $\Z\to\Q$) to $\Q$, and by tensoring with a $\Q$-vector space $V$ we obtain
\[
\text{(periodic functions $\Z\to V$)}\to V.
\]

Since restriction from $\Z$ to $\Z$ identifies the locally constant functions on $\Z$ with the periodic functions on $\Z$, the map $\varepsilon\mapsto L(1-k,\varepsilon)$ may be viewed as a distribution $T_k$ on $\Z$ with values in $\Q$. Such a distribution may alternately be regarded as a function on the compact open subsets $U$ of $\Z$, here explicitly given by the formula
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Tk}
T_k(U)=\sum_{\substack{n\in U\\n\ge1}}n^{k-1}\cdot n^{s}\Big|_{s=1-k}=\sum_{\substack{n\in U\\n\ge1}}n^{k-1}.
\end{equation}
(The summation is extended over the positive integers $n\in U$.) From \eqref{eq:Tk} we may deduce immediately the ``invariance''
\begin{equation}\label{eq:invariance-Z}
T_k(nU)=n^{k-1}T_k(U)
\end{equation}
for positive integers $n$. This invariance implies that $T_k$ has a unique extension to a distribution $T_k$ on $\Q=\Z\otimes\Q$ which satisfies the invariance
\begin{equation}\label{eq:invariance-Q}
T_k(nU)=n^{k-1}T_k(U)
\end{equation}
for $n$ a positive rational number and $U$ a compact-open subset of $\Q$. This distribution is again defined by \eqref{eq:Tk}, with the summation now over all positive $n\in\Q$.

Another way to view distributions on $\Z$ is to regard them as collections of functions $\alpha_f$ on the groups $\Z/f\Z$ ($f\ge1$), linked by a suitable compatibility relation (cf.~[26]). The value of $\alpha_f(x)$ on the class of $a$ modulo $f$ is the value of the distribution on the open subset $a+f\Z$ of $\Z$. For $T_k$, if we take $a$ in the range $1\le a\le f$ then \eqref{eq:L-rational} states that $\alpha_f$ maps $(a\bmod f)$ to the rational number $-\frac{f^{k-1}}{k}B_k(\frac{a}{f})$. This formula, or else the invariance \eqref{eq:invariance-Z}, implies that the maps $\alpha_f$ associated to $T_1$ are each obtained by composing the natural maps $\Z/f\Z\to\Q/\Z$ with a single map $\alpha:\Q/\Z\to\Q$. In the language of Kubert-Lang ([21]), $T_1$ is thus an ordinary distribution.

\subsection*{Twists}

We now introduce ``twists.'' If $c$ is a positive integer and $\varepsilon$ a periodic function on $\Z$ with values in a $\Q$-vector space, we write $\varepsilon_c$ for the function $x\mapsto\varepsilon(cx)$. If we are regarding $\varepsilon$ as a function modulo $f$, then $c$ will typically be taken prime to $f$. Set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:twist}
\Delta_c(1-k,\varepsilon)=L(1-k,\varepsilon)-c^kL(1-k,\varepsilon_c).
\end{equation}

Now let $\varepsilon_1,\dots,\varepsilon_t$ be functions mod $f$ with values in a $p$-adic field $\Q_p$, and let $k_1,\dots,k_t$ be positive integers. The following result summarizes the ``generalized Kummer congruences'' as considered by Mazur and others (cf.~[20, 24, 25]).

\begin{theorem}[(1.9)]\label{thm:kummer}
Suppose for all $n\ge1$ that we have
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{t}\varepsilon_i(n)n^{k_i-1}\in\Z_p.
\]
Then for each $c\ge1$, prime to $pf$, we have
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{t}\Delta_c(1-k_i,\varepsilon_i)\in\Z_p.
\]
\end{theorem}

This elementary theorem may be proved directly from \eqref{eq:L-rational}. It is the analogue for $K=\Q$ of (0.4). As mentioned in the Introduction, we now wish to rephrase (1.9) in terms of measures.

First, taking a more ``distribution-theoretic'' point of view, we eliminate the modulus $f$ from the statement of the theorem. For this, it is desirable to allow twisting by $c$ which are simultaneously prime to all moduli $f$: these will be elements of the group $\Z^*\subset\hat{\Z}$.

So let $\varepsilon$ be a locally constant function on $\hat{\Z}$ with values in a $\hat{\Q}$-module $V$ ($\hat{\Q}=\Q\otimes\hat{\Z}$ is the ring of finite adeles for $\Q$). Then for $c\in\hat{\Z}^*$ we again define $\varepsilon_c$ and elements $\Delta_c(1-k,\varepsilon)\in V$. We regard $V=\Q_p$ as a $\hat{\Q}$-module via the projection $\hat{\Q}\to\Q_p$.

The hypothesis of (1.9) may be rephrased as follows: the locally constant functions $\varepsilon_i:\hat{\Z}\to\Q_p$ and positive integers $k_i$ ($i=1,\dots,t$) are such that for all $n\in\Z$ we have $\varphi(n)\in\Z_p$ where
\[
\varphi(n)=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\varepsilon_i(n)n^{k_i-1}.
\]
Indeed, $\varphi$ is a continuous function $\hat{\Z}\to\Q_p$ and is thus $\Z_p$-valued if and only if its values on positive integers lie in $\Z_p$. The conclusion may be rephrased as follows: for all $c\in\hat{\Z}^*$, we have
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{t}\Delta_c(1-k_i,\varepsilon_i)\in\Z_p.
\]
In fact, one sees immediately that this statement for a given $c\in\hat{\Z}^*$ and the conclusion of (1.9) for a given $d\ge1$ are equivalent if $c\equiv d\pmod{fp^n}$, where $f$ is a modulus for all the $\varepsilon_i$ and where $p^n$ is a common denominator for the (finitely many) numbers
\[
L(1-k_i,\varepsilon_{ic})\qquad(i=1,\dots,t;\;c\bmod f).
\]

Having eliminated the $f$, we may also eliminate the $p$. Namely, the following is trivially equivalent to (1.9).

\begin{theorem}[(1.9a)]\label{thm:kummer-a}
Let $\varepsilon_1,\dots,\varepsilon_t$ be locally constant functions on $\hat{\Z}$ with values in $\Q$. Let $k_1,\dots,k_t$ be positive integers. Suppose that
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{t}\varepsilon_i(x)x^{k_i-1}\in\Z
\]
for all $x\in\hat{\Z}$. Then for each $c\in\hat{\Z}^*$ we have
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{t}\Delta_c(1-k_i,\varepsilon_i)\in\Z.
\]
\end{theorem}

We now rephrase this result in terms of measures on $\hat{\Z}$. Let $c\in\hat{\Z}^*$. For each locally constant function $\varepsilon:\hat{\Z}\to\Q$, the quantity $\Delta_c(0,\varepsilon)$ lies in $\Z$ by (1.9a). The map $\varepsilon\mapsto\Delta_c(0,\varepsilon)$ is thus a measure $\mu_c$ on $\hat{\Z}$ with values in $\Z$, so that a quantity $\int\varepsilon\,d\mu_c\in\Z$ is defined for all continuous functions $\varepsilon:\hat{\Z}\to\Z$.

In particular, let $x$ be the identity function ($a\mapsto a$) on $\hat{\Z}$, and let $\varepsilon$ be locally constant. Then (1.9a) implies that we have
\[
\int\varepsilon x^{k-1}\,d\mu_c=\Delta_c(1-k,\varepsilon)
\]
for all $k\ge1$. Indeed, to verify this it suffices to check that the difference between the two sides is congruent to $0\bmod N$, for each $N\ge1$. Let $N$ be given, and let $\eta:\hat{\Z}\to\Z$ be a locally constant function which is congruent to $x^{k-1}\bmod N$. Because $\mu_c$ is a measure we have
\[
\int\varepsilon x^{k-1}\,d\mu_c\equiv\int\varepsilon\eta\,d\mu_c\pmod{N\cdot\Z}.
\]
Thus it suffices to check that $\Delta_c(0,\varepsilon\eta)\equiv\Delta_c(1-k,\varepsilon)\pmod{N}$. This follows from (1.9a) and the congruence $\varepsilon(x)\eta(x)\equiv\varepsilon(x)x^{k-1}$ for $x\in\hat{\Z}$.

Summarizing, we have obtained from the Kummer congruences the following result.

\begin{theorem}[(1.9b)]\label{thm:kummer-b}
For each $c\in\hat{\Z}^*$, the map $\varepsilon\mapsto\Delta_c(0,\varepsilon)$ is a measure $\mu_c$ on $\hat{\Z}$ with values in $\Z$. For all $k\ge1$, the product $x^{k-1}\cdot\mu_c$ is the distribution $\varepsilon\mapsto\Delta_c(1-k,\varepsilon)$ (which is consequently a measure as well).
\end{theorem}

Conversely, this theorem immediately implies (1.9a). For suppose that we are given $\varepsilon_i$, $k_i$ and $c$ as in the hypothesis of (1.9a). Then we have, assuming (1.9b),
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{t}\Delta_c(1-k_i,\varepsilon_i)=\int\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{t}\varepsilon_i x^{k_i-1}\Big)d\mu_c\in\Z.
\]

In yet another variant, we consider locally constant functions $\varepsilon$ with compact support on $\Q$. For these, we define values $L(1-k,\varepsilon)$ as the integrals of $\varepsilon$ against the distributions $T_k$ introduced above. Formally,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L-compact}
L(1-k,\varepsilon)=\sum_{\substack{n>0\\n\in\Q}}\varepsilon(n)n^{k-1}.
\end{equation}
Introducing twisted values $\Delta_c(1-k,\varepsilon)$ as in \eqref{eq:twist}, we obtain from the invariance \eqref{eq:invariance-Q} the following integrality properties:

\begin{itemize}
\item[(1.11)] For $c\in\hat{\Z}^*$, the map $\mu_c:\varepsilon\mapsto\Delta_c(0,\varepsilon)$ is a $\Z$-valued measure on $\Q$.
\item[(1.12)] For $k\ge1$, the distribution $x^{k-1}\,d\mu_c$ on $\Q$ is the map $\varepsilon\mapsto\Delta_c(1-k,\varepsilon)$.
\end{itemize}

Note that the map $x^{k-1}$ is not bounded when $k>1$. Hence $x^{k-1}\,d\mu_c$ will not be a measure on $\Q$ for $k>1$.

\subsection*{Extra divisibility at 2}

Finally, we discuss an ``extra divisibility'' at $2$ which arises formally from parity considerations. For simplicity, we discuss only functions $\varepsilon$ supported on $\Z$.

Let $\delta$ be the Dirac measure at $0$ on $\hat{\Z}$:
\[
\delta:\varepsilon\mapsto\varepsilon(0).
\]

\begin{lemma}[(1.13)]\label{lem:parity}
$\mu_{-1}=-\delta$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We must verify that $L(0,\varepsilon)+L(0,\varepsilon_{-1})=-\varepsilon(0)$. This follows immediately from the formula
\[
L(0,\varepsilon)=-\sum_{t=1}^{f}\varepsilon(t)\Big(\frac{t}{f}-\frac{1}{2}\Big),
\]
which is a special case of \eqref{eq:L-bernoulli}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[(1.14)]\label{cor:parity}
For $k>1$, we have $x^{k-1}\cdot\mu_{-1}=0$, i.e.,
\[
L(1-k,\varepsilon)=(-1)^kL(1-k,\varepsilon_{-1}).
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
We have $x^{k-1}\cdot(-\delta)=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark0}
(1.13) and (1.14) summarize the presence of trivial zeros of $L$-functions for complex valued characters $\varepsilon$.
\end{remark0}

\begin{definition}
A function $\varphi$ on $\hat{\Z}$ with values in a $\Q$-vector space $V$ is \textit{odd} (resp.~\textit{even}) if it satisfies $\varphi(-x)=-\varphi(x)$ (resp.~$\varphi(-x)=\varphi(x)$) for $x\in\hat{\Z}$.
\end{definition}

From (1.14) we see that if $k$ is even then $L(1-k,\varepsilon)=0$ if a locally constant $\varepsilon$ is odd, whereas $L(1-k,\varepsilon)=0$ for even functions if $k>1$ is odd. For $k=1$, we have $L(1-k,\varepsilon)=0$ for even $\varepsilon$ which vanish at $0$ (1.13).

\begin{theorem}[(1.15)]\label{thm:odd}
Let $\varphi:\hat{\Z}\to\Z$ be an odd function. Then we have
\[
\int\varphi\,d\mu_c\in2\Z
\]
for all $c\in\hat{\Z}^*$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\alpha$ be a locally constant function which is congruent to $\varphi\bmod4$ and which vanishes at $0$. Set
\[
\alpha_+(x)=\frac{\alpha(x)+\alpha(-x)}{2},\qquad\alpha_-(x)=\frac{\alpha(x)-\alpha(-x)}{2}.
\]
Since $\alpha_+$ is even and vanishes at $0$, we have $\int\alpha_+\,d\mu_c\equiv0\pmod{2\Z}$, from the above discussion. Moreover, we have $\varphi\equiv\alpha_-\pmod{2}$, since
\[
2\varphi(x)=\varphi(x)-\varphi(-x)\equiv\alpha(x)-\alpha(-x)=2\alpha_-(x)\pmod{4}.
\]
Hence we have $\int\varphi\,d\mu_c\equiv\int\alpha_-\,d\mu_c\pmod{2\Z}$.

These computations show that we may assume that $\varphi$ is in fact locally constant, say mod $f$. We make this assumption.

Since $\varphi$ is odd, we may find a function $g:\Z/f\Z\to\Z$, vanishing at $0$, such that $\varphi(x)=g(x)-g(-x)$. We find
\[
\int\varphi(x)\,d\mu_c=\Delta_c(0,g)-\Delta_c(0,g_{-1})=2\Delta_c(0,g)\in2\Z.\qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[(1.16)]\label{cor:parity-2}
Let $\varepsilon_1,\dots,\varepsilon_t$ and $k_1,\dots,k_t$ be as in (1.9a). Suppose further for each $i=1,\dots,t$ that $\varepsilon_i$ and $k_i$ have the same parity (namely: $\varepsilon_i$ is even if $k_i$ is even and odd if $k_i$ is odd). Then for all $c\in\hat{\Z}^*$ we have
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{t}\Delta_c(1-k_i,\varepsilon_i)\in2\Z.
\]
\end{corollary}

\section{The Measure Spaces $A$ and $I$}

Let $K$ be a totally real field, which will be fixed for the remainder of this paper. In order to avoid certain technical difficulties later on, we will assume right away that $K$ is different from the rational field $\Q$. We introduce the following notation and conventions, which will be in force for the remainder of this paper:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item $r$ is the degree of $K$ over $\Q$.
\item $\mathcal{N}$ is the norm map $K\to\Q$ or any norm map derived from it, such as the norm map on ideals of $K$ or a map of the type $K\otimes R\to R$, where $R$ is a $\Q$-algebra.
\item An ideal of $K$ is tacitly understood to be non-zero and fractional (as opposed to integral), unless otherwise described. A conductor is a (non-zero) integral ideal. The group of ideals is denoted by $I_0$ and the sub-monoid of integral ideals by $A_0$.
\item We denote the ring of integers of $K$ by $\OO$ and its different by $\DD$. The unit ideal $(1)$ is sometimes abbreviated $1$.
\item For $\alpha\in K$, the symbol $\alpha\gg0$ means that $\alpha$ is totally positive. For $\alpha\in K\otimes\R$, we write $\alpha>0$ if the image of $\alpha$ under each $\R$-algebra homomorphism $K\otimes\R\to\R$ is positive. Thus, if we number the embeddings $K\hookrightarrow\R$, so that $K\otimes\R$ may be written $\R^r$, then $\alpha>0$ means that $\alpha$ is an $r$-tuple of positive numbers.
\end{itemize}

We let $\A$ denote the ring of adeles of $K$ and write either $\hat{K}$ or $\A_f$ for the ring of finite adeles of $K$. If $x\in\hat{K}^*$ is a ``finite idele'' we write $(x)$ for the ideal ``generated'' by $x$, namely
\[
(x)=\prod_{\pp\text{ prime}}\pp^{\ord_{\pp}(x_{\pp})},
\]
the product being extended over the prime ideals $\pp$ of $K$. Also, when $\mathfrak{a}$ is an ideal, we write $\hat{\mathfrak{a}}$ for the completion of $\mathfrak{a}$, i.e., the closure of $\mathfrak{a}$ in $\hat{K}$.

\begin{definition}[(2.1)]\label{def:equiv}
Let $\mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{b}$ be ideals, and let $\ff$ be a conductor. The ideals $\mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{b}$ are \textit{equivalent} (or \textit{congruent}) mod $\ff$ if the ideal $\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}^{-1}$ may be written as the principal ideal $(\alpha)$ for some totally positive $\alpha$ in the set $1+\ff\mathfrak{b}^{-1}$.
\end{definition}

We write $\sim_{\ff}$ for the equivalence relation introduced above. If $\mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{b}$ are equivalent mod $\ff$, we write $\mathfrak{a}\sim_{\ff}\mathfrak{b}$ and sometimes say that $\mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{b}$ are equivalent mod $\ff$. The following results follow easily from the definition of $\sim_{\ff}$.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item[(2.2)] Suppose that $\mathfrak{a}\sim_{\ff}\mathfrak{b}$ and that $\mathfrak{b}$ is an ideal containing $\ff$. Then $\mathfrak{b}$ contains $\mathfrak{a}$ if and only if $\mathfrak{b}$ contains $\mathfrak{a}$.
\item[(2.3)] \textbf{Corollary.} Any ideal equivalent mod $\ff$ to an integral ideal is again integral.
\item[(2.4)] If $\mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{b}$ are integral ideals which are equivalent mod $\ff$, then the greatest common divisors $(\mathfrak{a},\ff)$ and $(\mathfrak{b},\ff)$ are equal.
\item[(2.5)] Let $\mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{b}$ be ideals and let $\mathfrak{b}$ be an integral ideal. We have $\mathfrak{a}\sim_{\ff}\mathfrak{b}$ if and only if $\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}\sim_{\ff\mathfrak{b}}\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{b}$.
\item[(2.6)] The set $A_{\ff}$ of mod $\ff$ classes of integral ideals is a monoid under a multiplication $A_{\ff}\times A_{\ff}\to A_{\ff}$ induced by multiplication of ideals. The invertible elements are those classes represented by ideals which are relatively prime to $\ff$. Further, the group $G_{\ff}$ of invertible elements of $A_{\ff}$ is precisely the group of strict ray classes of $K$ modulo $\ff$: when $\mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{b}$ are prime-to-$\ff$ integral ideals, we have $\mathfrak{a}\sim_{\ff}\mathfrak{b}$ if and only if $\mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{b}$ represent the same strict ray class mod $\ff$.
\end{itemize}

Using the above results, and especially (2.5), (2.6), we obtain the following description of $A_{\ff}$. It is a disjoint union of copies of the groups $G_{\ff/\mathfrak{b}}$, $\mathfrak{b}$ running over the divisors of $\ff$. The image of $G_{\ff/\mathfrak{b}}$ in $A_{\ff}$ consists of those classes whose greatest common divisor with $\ff$ is $\ff\mathfrak{b}^{-1}$. This decomposition generalizes the fact that, for $\ff\mid f$, $\Z/f\Z$ is a disjoint union of copies of $(\Z/d\Z)^*$, with $d$ running over the divisors of $f$.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item[(2.7)] Suppose that $\mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{b}$ are integral ideals divisible by $\ff$. Then $\mathfrak{a}$ is equivalent to $\mathfrak{b}$ mod $\ff$ if and only if $\mathfrak{a}$ is equivalent to $\mathfrak{b}$ mod $(1)$.
\end{itemize}

We have already introduced the notation $A_{\ff}$ for the (finite) set of classes of integral ideals mod $\ff$. Now let $I_{\ff}$ be the set of equivalence classes of all ideals mod $\ff$. For each ideal $\mathfrak{b}$ containing $\ff$, let $I_{\ff}^{\mathfrak{b}}$ be the set of classes mod $\ff$ of ideals contained in $\mathfrak{b}$, cf.~(2.2).

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item[(2.8)] Each set $I_{\ff}^{\mathfrak{b}}$ is finite. Indeed, if $\mathfrak{a}$ is the inverse of an integral ideal $\mathfrak{a}'$, the map on ideals $\mathfrak{x}\mapsto\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{x}$ induces a bijection $I_{\ff}^{\mathfrak{b}}\xrightarrow{\sim}A_{\ff/\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{a}}$.
\end{itemize}

We now allow $\ff$ to vary. Clearly, if $\ff'\mid\ff$ and if $\mathfrak{a}\sim_{\ff'}\mathfrak{b}$, then $\mathfrak{a}\sim_{\ff}\mathfrak{b}$. There are thus natural maps $I_{\ff'}\to I_{\ff}$, inducing maps $A_{\ff'}\to A_{\ff}$ and $G_{\ff'}\to G_{\ff}$. We define
\[
I=\varprojlim I_{\ff},\qquad A=\varprojlim A_{\ff},\qquad G=\varprojlim G_{\ff}.
\]
(We note that $G$ no longer has the meaning given it in the Introduction.) In the prohibited case $K=\Q$, these limits become respectively $\Q$, $\hat{\Z}$, and $\hat{\Z}^*$. Also, we recognize $G$ as the Galois group of the maximal abelian extension $K^{\ab}$ of $K$. In what follows, we will gradually establish parallels between $A$ and $\Z$ and between $I$ and $\Q$. A typical element of $I$ will be denoted $(\mathfrak{a}_{\ff})=\mathfrak{a}$, indicating that $\mathfrak{a}_{\ff}$ is the $\ff$-component of $\mathfrak{a}$.

For $\mathfrak{b}\in I_0$, we let
\[
I^{\mathfrak{b}}=\{(\mathfrak{a}_{\ff})\in I\mid\mathfrak{a}_{\ff}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}\text{ for all }\ff\in\DD\}
\]
\[
=\{(\mathfrak{a}_{\ff})\in I\mid\mathfrak{a}_{\ff}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}\text{ for some }\ff\subseteq\mathfrak{b}\};
\]
the equivalence of the two definitions resulting from (2.2). It is clear that we have
\[
I^{\mathfrak{b}_1\mathfrak{b}_2}\subseteq I^{\mathfrak{b}_1}\cap I^{\mathfrak{b}_2}.
\]

\begin{proposition}[(2.9)]\label{prop:mult}
Let $\mathfrak{b}_1$ and $\mathfrak{b}_2$ be ideals. Suppose that $\ff_1\subseteq\mathfrak{b}_1$ and $\ff_2\subseteq\mathfrak{b}_2$ are conductors such that
\[
\ff_1\mathfrak{b}_2,\;\ff_2\mathfrak{b}_1\subseteq\ff.
\]
Then multiplication of ideals induces a map
\[
I_{\ff_1}^{\mathfrak{b}_1}\times I_{\ff_2}^{\mathfrak{b}_2}\to I_{\ff}^{\mathfrak{b}_1\mathfrak{b}_2+\ff\mathfrak{b}_1\mathfrak{b}_2}.
\]
\end{proposition}

Using (2.9), we find a multiplication
\[
I\times I\to I.
\]
Specifically, to calculate a product $\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}$ mod $\ff$, suppose that $(\mathfrak{a}_{\ff})\in I^{\mathfrak{b}_1}$, $(\mathfrak{b}_{\ff})\in I^{\mathfrak{b}_2}$. If $\ff_1\subseteq\mathfrak{b}_1$ and $\ff_2\subseteq\mathfrak{b}_2$ are such that $\ff_1\mathfrak{b}_2,\ff_2\mathfrak{b}_1\subseteq\ff$, then $\mathfrak{a}_{\ff_1}\cdot\mathfrak{b}_{\ff_2}$ gives $\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}$ mod $\ff$. It is clear that $\mathfrak{b}_1\cdot I^{\mathfrak{b}_2}=I^{\mathfrak{b}_1\mathfrak{b}_2}$ for ideals $\mathfrak{b}_1$ and $\mathfrak{b}_2$. (We write again $\mathfrak{b}$ for the image of an ideal $\mathfrak{b}$ in $I$.) Namely, $\mathfrak{b}_1\subseteq\mathfrak{b}_2$ for all $\ff$ sufficiently small if and only if $\mathfrak{b}_1\subseteq\mathfrak{b}_2$ for all $\ff$ sufficiently small.

We observe, incidentally, that the natural map $I_0\to I$ is injective. In fact, if $\mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{b}$ are ideals which are equivalent modulo a conductor $\ff\subseteq\mathfrak{a}\cap\mathfrak{b}$, then by (2.2) we have $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}$ and $\mathfrak{b}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}$.

\begin{proposition}[(2.10)]\label{prop:invertible}
Let $I^*$ be the set of invertible elements of $I$ under the multiplication. Then $I^*=I_0\times G$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
It is immediate from (2.6) that if we have $\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}=1$ with $\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\in A$, then both $\mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{b}$ belong to $G$. In general, if $\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}=1$ with $\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\in I$, multiply $\mathfrak{a}$ by an ideal (and $\mathfrak{b}$ by its inverse) to have $\mathfrak{a}\in A$. We need not have $\mathfrak{b}\in A$; let $\mathfrak{b}'\in I_0$ be the denominator of $\mathfrak{b}$. Then $\mathfrak{b}'\in I^{*1}$. Calculating $\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}'$ mod $1$, we find $1\sim\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{b}'$, giving that $\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}'$ is divisible by $1$. Hence $\mathfrak{a}\in I^{\mathfrak{b}'}$, so that in the expression
\[
(\mathfrak{b}'^{-1}\mathfrak{a})\cdot(\mathfrak{b}'\mathfrak{b})=1,
\]
both terms in the product lie in $A$. Thus $\mathfrak{b}'^{-1}\mathfrak{a}\in G$, so $\mathfrak{a}\in I_0\times G$.

[It is obvious that the intersection $I_0\cap G$ is trivial because an element $(\mathfrak{a}_{\ff})$ of $G$ is such that $\mathfrak{a}_{\ff}\in I_{\ff}$, $(\mathfrak{a}_{\ff},\ff)=1$ for each $\ff$.]
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[(2.11)]\label{prop:norm}
Let $\ff$ be a positive integer, and suppose that $\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\in I_0$ are congruent mod $\sim_{\ff}$ (i.e., mod $\sim_{\ff\OO}$). Let $\mathfrak{b}$ be a denominator for $\mathfrak{a}$ (and hence for $\mathfrak{b}$). Then
\[
\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{a}\equiv\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{b}\pmod{(\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\ff},
\]
i.e.,
\[
\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{a}-\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{b}\in(\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\ff\cdot\Z.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}[(2.12)]\label{cor:norm}
The norm map $I_0\to\Q_{>0}$ extends to a continuous map $\mathcal{N}:I\to\Q$, such that $\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b})=\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\cdot\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{b})$. We have $\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\ge1$ for $\mathfrak{a}\in A$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}[Proof of (2.11)]
We may assume that $\mathfrak{b}=1$. Indeed, under the hypothesis that $\mathfrak{b}$ is a denominator, the ideals $\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}$ and $\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{b}$ are integral and congruent mod $\ff$ (in fact, mod $\ff\DD$). The assertion for $\mathfrak{b}=1$ gives $\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b})\equiv\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{b})\pmod{\ff}$, as desired.

Now with $\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\in A_0$, set $\mathfrak{d}=(\ff,\mathfrak{a})=(\ff,\mathfrak{b})$. We have $\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}\sim\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}$ mod $\ff\mathfrak{d}^{-1}$; let $f'$ be the largest integer dividing the ideal $\ff\mathfrak{d}^{-1}$. Then $\ff$ divides $f'\cdot\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{b}$, so that the assertion
\[
\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}^{-1})\equiv\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{b}^{-1})\pmod{f'}
\]
implies the proposition. We are thus reduced to the case where $(\ff,\mathfrak{a})=(\ff,\mathfrak{b})=1$. Then, finally, let $\alpha$ be as in the definition of $\sim_{\ff}$ for $\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}$. We have $\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{a}=\mathcal{N}\alpha\cdot\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{b}$, with $\alpha\equiv1\pmod{\ff}$. We see immediately that $\mathcal{N}\alpha\equiv1\pmod{\ff}$, giving $\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{a}\equiv\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{b}\pmod{\ff}$.
\end{proof}

We now wish to compare the space $I$ with the ring $\hat{K}$ of finite adeles of $K$. For this, we regard $\hat{K}$ as the inverse limit $\varprojlim K/\ff$ and consider an element $\alpha$ of $\hat{K}$ as a sequence $(\alpha_{\ff})$ of totally positive numbers in $K$, compatible with the transition maps $K/\ff'\to K/\ff$ for $\ff'\subseteq\ff$. Let $i(\alpha)$ be the sequence of principal ideals $((\alpha_{\ff}))$.

\begin{proposition}[(2.13)]\label{prop:i}
$i(\alpha)$ is an element of $I$ which depends only on $\alpha$, and not on the choice of the $\alpha_{\ff}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
This follows immediately from the following lemma, which in turn is an immediate consequence of the definition of $\sim_{\ff}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[(2.14)]\label{lem:unit}
Let $\alpha,\beta\in\hat{K}$ with $\alpha_{\ff},\beta_{\ff}\gg0$. Then $(\alpha_{\ff})\sim(\beta_{\ff})$ mod $\ff$ if and only if there is a totally positive unit $u\in K$ such that $\alpha_{\ff}\equiv u\beta_{\ff}\pmod{\ff}$.
\end{lemma}

Now let $U^+$ be the closure in $\hat{\OO}^*$ of the group of totally positive units of $K$.

\begin{proposition}[(2.15)]\label{prop:kernel}
Let $\alpha,\beta\in\hat{K}$. Then $i(\alpha)=i(\beta)$ if and only if $\alpha=\beta u$ for some $u\in U^+$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The lemma shows that if $\alpha=\beta u$ with $u\in U^+$, then $(\alpha_{\ff})\sim(\beta_{\ff})$ mod $\ff$ for all $\ff$, so $i(\alpha)=i(\beta)$. Conversely, suppose $i(\alpha)=i(\beta)$, and let us prove that $\alpha=\beta u$ for some $u$. We do have $\alpha_{\ff}\equiv u_{\ff}\beta_{\ff}\pmod{\ff}$ for each $\ff$, where $u_{\ff}$ is a totally positive unit. However, the sequence $(u_{\ff}\bmod\ff)$ may not be compatible because the units $u_{\ff}$ may not be unique mod $\ff$.

To deal with this problem, we multiply $\alpha$ and $\beta$ by a positive integer so as to have $\alpha_{\ff},\beta_{\ff}\in\OO$. (This changes neither the hypothesis nor the conclusion of the proposition.) For each $\ff$, let $\mathfrak{d}_{\ff}=\gcd(\ff,\alpha_{\ff}^{-1})=\gcd(\ff,\beta_{\ff}^{-1})$. Then the unit $u_{\ff}$ is determined modulo $\ff\mathfrak{d}_{\ff}^{-1}=:\mathfrak{g}_{\ff}$. For $\ff'\subseteq\ff$, we have $\mathfrak{g}_{\ff'}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\ff}$. Thus we need only to know that $U^+$ maps into $\varprojlim C_{\mathfrak{g}}$, where $C_{\mathfrak{g}}$ is the group of totally positive units taken mod $\mathfrak{g}$. This is obvious, for example because $U^+$ is compact and maps onto each $C_{\mathfrak{g}}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[(2.16)]\label{prop:norm-idele}
$\mathcal{N}(i(\alpha))=\mathcal{N}\alpha$ for all $\alpha\in\hat{K}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Indeed, $\mathcal{N}((\alpha_{\ff}))=\mathcal{N}\alpha_{\ff}$ because $\alpha_{\ff}\gg0$.
\end{proof}

\textbf{Convention.} Let $\mathfrak{a}\in I$, $\alpha\in\hat{K}$. We write $(\mathfrak{a}\cdot\alpha)$, or simply $\mathfrak{a}\cdot\alpha$, for the product $\mathfrak{a}\cdot i(\alpha)$ in $I$.

Note that for $\alpha>0$ in $K$ we have $(\alpha)=i(\alpha)$ in $I$. Thus $(\mathfrak{a}\cdot\alpha)\in I$.

\begin{lemma}[(2.17)]\label{lem:class}
For $\mathfrak{a}\in I$, the quantity $(\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathcal{O})$ depends only on the strict ideal class of $\mathfrak{a}_1$: for $\mathfrak{b}\in I$, we have $(\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathcal{O})=(\mathfrak{b}\cdot\mathcal{O})$ if and only if $\mathfrak{a}_1$ and $\mathfrak{b}_1$ belong to the same strict ideal class of $K$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $t>0$, we have $(t\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathcal{O})=(\mathfrak{a}\cdot t\mathcal{O})=(\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathcal{O})$. Hence we can assume that $\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\in A$, in which case the assertion to be proved is: $(\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathcal{O})=(\mathfrak{b}\cdot\mathcal{O})$ if and only if $\mathfrak{a}_1\sim\mathfrak{b}_1$ mod $1$. For each $\ff$, let $\alpha_{\ff}$ be a totally positive number divisible by $\ff$. Modulo $\ff$, $(\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathcal{O})$ is $\mathfrak{a}_{\ff}\alpha_{\ff}$ and $(\mathfrak{b}\cdot\mathcal{O})$ is $\mathfrak{b}_{\ff}\alpha_{\ff}$. By (2.7) we see that $\mathfrak{a}_{\ff}\alpha_{\ff}\sim\mathfrak{b}_{\ff}\alpha_{\ff}$ mod $\ff$ if and only if $\mathfrak{a}_{\ff}\alpha_{\ff}\sim\mathfrak{b}_{\ff}\alpha_{\ff}$ mod $1$, which is true if and only if $\mathfrak{a}_1$ and $\mathfrak{b}_1$ are in the same strict ideal class.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[(2.18)]\label{prop:unit-class}
Let $\mathfrak{a}\in I$. Suppose that $(\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathcal{O})=(1\cdot\mathcal{O})$. Then $\mathfrak{a}=(1\cdot\alpha)$ for some $\alpha\in\hat{K}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
As usual, we can multiply $\mathfrak{a}$ by some $t>0$ to assume that $\mathfrak{a}\in A$. Then by hypothesis we have, for each $\ff$, $\mathfrak{a}_{\ff}=(\alpha_{\ff})$, where $\alpha_{\ff}\in K$ is a totally positive integer, which by (2.14) is well defined, mod $\ff$, modulo the action of the group of totally positive units. Let $C_{\ff}$ be the set of integers mod $\ff$, taken modulo this action. Then the compact $\hat{\OO}$ maps onto each $C_{\ff}$ and hence onto the limit $\varprojlim C_{\ff}$. This gives what is needed.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[(2.19)]\label{cor:representative}
Let $\mathfrak{a}=(\mathfrak{a}_{\ff})\in I$, and let $\mathfrak{b}\in I_0$ be an ideal in the same ideal class as $\mathfrak{a}_1$. Then $\mathfrak{a}=(\mathfrak{b}\cdot\alpha)$ for some $\alpha\in\hat{K}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By (2.17), $(\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}^{-1}\cdot\mathcal{O})=(1\cdot\mathcal{O})$. Hence $\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}^{-1}$ may be written $(1\cdot\alpha)$ for some $\alpha\in\hat{K}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[(2.20)]\label{prop:j}
For each $x\in\hat{K}^*$, we have $(x)\cdot x^{-1}\in G$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
It suffices to prove that $(x)\cdot x^{-1}\in A$, using the symmetry and the fact that an element of $A$ with an inverse in $A$ must in fact belong to $G$. This follows from the following result, whose proof we omit.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[(2.21)]\label{lem:closure}
Let $\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\in I_0$, and $\alpha\in\hat{K}$. Then $(\mathfrak{a}\cdot\alpha)\in I^{\mathfrak{b}}$ if and only if $\alpha$ belongs to the completion $\hat{\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\mathfrak{b}}$ of the ideal $\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\mathfrak{b}$.
\end{lemma}

As a complement to (2.21), we mention the following fact:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item[(2.22)] Let $\mathfrak{a}$ be an ideal and let $\alpha,\beta\in\hat{K}$. We have $(\mathfrak{a}\cdot\alpha)\sim(\mathfrak{a}\cdot\beta)$ mod $\ff$ if and only if there is a totally positive unit $u$ such that we have
\[
\alpha\equiv u\beta\pmod{\hat{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\ff}.
\]
\end{itemize}

Referring now to (2.20), we let $j(x)=(x)\cdot x^{-1}$ for $x\in\hat{K}^*$. Then $j$ may be viewed as a homomorphism
\[
j:\hat{K}^*\to G
\]
which is trivial on the set $\hat{K}_{>0}$ of totally positive elements of $\hat{K}$. On the other hand, for each conductor $\ff$ there is a natural map
\[
\psi_{\ff}:\hat{K}^*\to G_{\ff},
\]
trivial on $\hat{K}_{>0}$, which maps each $x\in\hat{K}^*$ such that $x\equiv1\pmod{\ff}$ to the class in $G_{\ff}$ of the ideal $(x)$ generated by $x$, cf.~[22, pp.~146--147]. (Here we should recall that $G_{\ff}$ is the ray class group of $K$ mod $\ff$, so that each prime-to-$\ff$ ideal of $K$, integral or not, has a well defined image in $G_{\ff}$. From the point of view of the equivalence $\sim_{\ff}$, only the integral prime-to-$\ff$ ideals map to $G_{\ff}$.) The resulting map
\[
\psi:\hat{K}^*\to G=\varprojlim G_{\ff}
\]
is surjective. By abuse of language, we may refer to it as an Artin symbol.

\begin{proposition}[(2.23)]\label{prop:psi-j}
We have $\psi=j$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since both $\psi$ and $j$ vanish on $\hat{K}_{>0}$, it suffices to check that $\psi_{\ff}$ and the composition of $j$ and the quotient $G\to G_{\ff}$ agree on elements of the set $1+\hat{\ff}$. Now, mod $\ff$, $j(x)$ is represented by any ideal of the form $(\alpha)(x)$, where $\alpha$ is a totally positive element of $K$ with $\alpha\equiv x^{-1}\pmod{\ff}$. We must verify that
\[
(\alpha)(x)\sim(x),
\]
and it suffices to see that
\[
\alpha\equiv1+(x)^{-1}\ff.
\]
This, finally, follows from the defining property of $\alpha$, together with the fact that $1-x^{-1}=x^{-1}(x-1)\in x^{-1}\ff$.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Frobenius Elements (2.24)}

Let $\ff$ be a conductor, and let $v$ be a real place of $K$. Let $\alpha\in1+\ff$ be a number which is negative at $v$ and positive at each real place of $K$ different from $v$. The class $\sigma_v$ of $(\alpha)$ in $G_{\ff}$ is independent of $\alpha$ and has order $1$ or $2$. It is trivial if and only if $\alpha$ may be chosen to be a unit. If we interpret $G_{\ff}$ as the Galois group over $K$ of the ray class field $K_{\ff}$ of $K$ of conductor $\ff$, then $\sigma_v$ becomes complex conjugation after we choose an embedding $K_{\ff}\hookrightarrow\C$ which induces $v$ on $K$.

For fixed $v$ and varying $\ff$, the elements $\sigma_v$ piece together to give a Frobenius element in $G$, which we again call $\sigma_v$. Each $\sigma_v$ now has order $2$, and in fact the subgroup $\Sigma$ of $G$ generated by the $\sigma_v$ is an elementary $2$-group whose order is $2^r$. Each $\sigma_v$ has norm $-1$. If we let $\Sigma_0$ be the kernel of the norm map
\[
\mathcal{N}:\Sigma\to\{\pm1\},
\]
then $\Sigma_0$ has order $2^{r-1}$ and is generated by products $\sigma_v\sigma_w$, with $v$ and $w$ running over the real places of $K$. For $\ff$ a conductor, we let $\Sigma_{\ff}^*$ (resp.~$\Sigma_{\ff}$) be the image of $\Sigma^*$ (resp.~$\Sigma$) in $G_{\ff}$. We will be especially interested in the case $\ff=(1)$.

In a natural way, the $\sigma_v$ lead to the notion of functions with parity. Let $\varphi$ be a function on $G$ with values in an abelian group $\mathcal{W}$. For each real place $v$, let $a_v$ be one of the two integers $0,1$. We say that $\varphi$ has parity $(a_v)$ if we have
\[
\varphi(\sigma_vg)=(-1)^{a_v}\varphi(g)
\]
for all $g\in G$. When the $a_v$ are all equal to $0$ (resp.~$1$) we say that $\varphi$ is even (resp.~odd). The phrase ``$\varphi$ has parity $(-1)^k$'' means that $\varphi$ is odd if $k$ is odd and even if $k$ is even.

\subsection*{Invertible Modules (2.25)}

As is well known, the group of ideal classes of $K$ may be interpreted as the group of isomorphism classes of invertible $\OO$-modules. We shall now similarly interpret the constructions of this \S. For this, we must introduce the notion of a signed $\OO$-module.

Let $\mathscr{E}$ be an invertible $\OO$-module. For each real place $v$ of $K$, let $\mathscr{E}_v$ be the tensor product $\mathscr{E}\otimes_{\OO}\R$, with $\R$ viewed as an $\OO$-module via $v$. Then $\mathscr{E}_v$ is a free $\R$-module of rank $1$. A positivity for $\mathscr{E}$ is the choice, for each $v$, of an element of
\[
\Isom(\mathscr{E}_v,\R)\cong\R^*,
\]
taken modulo the action of the group of positive real numbers. There are precisely $2^r$ possible positivities for $\mathscr{E}$.

A pair consisting of an invertible $\OO$-module $\mathscr{E}$, together with a positivity $+$ for $\mathscr{E}$, is a signed $\OO$-module. Two such modules $(\mathscr{E},+)$, $(\mathscr{E}',+')$ are isomorphic if there is an $\OO$-isomorphism $\mathscr{E}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathscr{E}'$ compatible with $+$, $+'$. It is clear that the isomorphism classes of signed $\OO$-modules are the strict ideal classes of $K$. More precisely, this works as follows. Each ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ of $K$ has a canonical positivity $+_{\can}$ arising from the canonical isomorphism $\mathfrak{a}\otimes_{\OO}K=K$. With $\mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{b}$ ideals, we have $(\mathfrak{a},+_{\can})\cong(\mathfrak{b},+_{\can})$ if and only if $\mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{b}$ are in the same strict class. Finally, any pair $(\mathscr{E},+)$ is isomorphic to $(\mathfrak{a},+_{\can})$ for some ideal $\mathfrak{a}$.

Now let $\ff$ be a conductor. We consider triples $(\mathscr{E},+,\varphi)$, where $\varphi$ is an $\OO$-linear map $\mathscr{E}\otimes\hat{\OO}\to\hat{K}/\ff$. Two such triples $(\mathscr{E},+,\varphi)$, $(\mathscr{E}',+',\varphi')$ are said to be isomorphic if there is an isomorphism $(\mathscr{E},+)\xrightarrow{\sim}(\mathscr{E}',+')$ so that the diagram
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
\mathscr{E}\otimes\hat{\OO}\arrow[r]\arrow[d] & \hat{K}/\ff\\
\mathscr{E}'\otimes\hat{\OO}\arrow[ur, "\varphi'"'] &
\end{tikzcd}
\]
is commutative. For $\mathfrak{a}\in I_0$, we let $\varphi_{\can}$ be the map $\mathfrak{a}\otimes\hat{\OO}\to\hat{K}/\ff$. A computation shows that $(\mathfrak{a},+_{\can},\varphi_{\can})$ and $(\mathfrak{b},+_{\can},\varphi_{\can})$ are isomorphic if and only if $\mathfrak{a}\sim_{\ff}\mathfrak{b}$.

Furthermore, let $(\mathscr{E},+,\varphi)$ be a given triple, and let $\mathfrak{a}$ be an ideal such that $(\mathscr{E},+)$ and $(\mathfrak{a},+_{\can})$ are isomorphic. Choosing an isomorphism we find a map $\mathfrak{a}\to\hat{K}/\ff$, necessarily given by a multiplication $\gamma:\mathfrak{a}\to\gamma\mathfrak{a}$ (with $\gamma\in K$), followed by the reduction $\hat{K}\to\hat{K}/\ff$. We may choose $\gamma$ to be totally positive. Then the isomorphism $\mathfrak{a}\gamma^{-1}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathfrak{b}\cdot\ff^{-1}$ shows that we have $(\mathscr{E},+,\varphi)\cong(\mathfrak{a}\gamma,+_{\can},\varphi_{\can})$. Hence $I_{\ff}$ is just the set of isomorphism classes of triples $(\mathscr{E},+,\varphi)$.

To describe $I$, we consider triples $(\mathscr{E},+,\varphi)$, where $\varphi$ is now an $\OO$-linear map $\mathscr{E}\otimes\hat{\OO}\to\hat{K}$. Such triples give rise to elements of $I$ as follows. Choosing an isomorphism $(\mathscr{E},+)\cong(\mathfrak{a},+_{\can})$, we find an $\OO$-linear $\mathfrak{a}\to\hat{K}$, which is necessarily of the form ``multiplication by $\gamma$'' for some $\gamma\in K$. The product $(\mathfrak{a}\cdot\gamma)$ in $I$ is easily seen to depend only on $(\mathscr{E},+,\varphi)$. Note that the image mod $\ff$ of this product is represented by $(\mathscr{E},+)$ together with the map ``$\varphi$ mod $\ff$'':
\[
\mathscr{E}\to\mathscr{E}\otimes\hat{\OO}\xrightarrow{\varphi}\hat{K}\to\hat{K}/\ff=K/\ff.
\]
In either situation, the action of a real Frobenius $\sigma_v$ is as follows: we have $\sigma_v\cdot(\mathscr{E},+,\varphi)=(\mathscr{E},+',\varphi)$, where $+'$ is deduced from $+$ by changing $+$ at $v$.

\section{A Functional Equation}

In this \S\ we define the $L$-series attached to a (complex valued) Schwartz function on the measure space $I$. We then prove a functional equation for such $L$-series, involving a Fourier transform on the space $I$. Defining this Fourier transform is the first order of business.

We shall view the ring $\A$ of adeles of $K$ as the product $\A_f\times K_{\infty}$, where $\A_f=\hat{K}$ is the ring of finite adeles of $K$ and $K_{\infty}=K\otimes\R$ is the product of the real completions of $K$. For computing, we number these real places of $K$, so that $K_{\infty}=\R^r$. Also, for $x\in\A$, we write $x_f$ (resp.~$x_{\infty}$) for the image of $x$ in $\A_f$ (resp.~$K_{\infty}$).

Let $|\cdot|:K_{\infty}\to\R$ be the standard absolute value, namely
\[
(x_1,\dots,x_r)\mapsto\prod_{i=1}^r|x_i|.
\]
We write instead $\|\cdot\|$ for the standard absolute value on $\A_f$, defined for example by the equation
\[
d(\gamma x)=\|\gamma\|\cdot dx,
\]
where $dx$ is a Haar measure on $\A_f$. For $y\in K^*$, we have $\|y\|=\mathcal{N}(y)^{-1}$, where $(y)$ is the ideal of $K$ generated by $y$.

Let $\psi:\A\to\C^*$ be the standard additive character, trivial on $K$, whose restriction to a completion $K_v$ of $K$ is given as follows:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item For $v$ real, $\psi:x\mapsto e^{2\pi ix}$;
\item For $v$ $p$-adic, $\psi:x\mapsto e^{-2\pi i\tr(x)}$,
\end{itemize}
where $\tr:K_v\to\Q_p$ is the trace, and the exponential $e^{2\pi it}$ for $t\in\R$ is defined in the usual way. The restriction of $\psi$ to $\A_f$ (resp.~$K_{\infty}$) is denoted $\psi_f$ (resp.~$\psi_{\infty}$), or simply $\psi$, according to the context. For $\varepsilon$ a Schwartz function on $\A_f$ (resp.~$K_{\infty}$, resp.~$\A$), we define the Fourier transform $\hat{\varepsilon}$ of $\varepsilon$ by the formula
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fourier}
\hat{\varepsilon}(y)=\int\varepsilon(x)\psi(xy)\,dx.
\end{equation}
Here $dx$ denotes Haar measure, normalized so that the formula $\hat{\hat{\varepsilon}}(x)=\varepsilon(-x)$ holds, cf.~\S3.3 and \S4.1 of [39].

We now turn our attention to Schwartz functions on $\hat{K}$ and on $I$. We recall the following ``variance'' formula for $\hat{K}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:variance}
\text{If $\eta(x)=\varepsilon(\gamma x)$ with $\gamma\in\hat{K}^*$, then $\hat{\eta}(y)=\hat{\varepsilon}(\gamma^{-1}y)\|\gamma\|^{-1}$.}
\end{equation}

Now let $\varepsilon$ be a locally constant function with compact support on $I$. Specifically, assume that $\varepsilon$ is supported on $I^{\mathfrak{b}}$ and defined modulo $\sim_{\ff}$. If $\mathfrak{a}\in I_0$, let $\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{a}}:\hat{K}\to\C$ be the function $x\mapsto\varepsilon(\mathfrak{a}\cdot x)$. By (2.21) and (2.22), $\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{a}}$ is supported on $\hat{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\mathfrak{b}$ and defined modulo $\hat{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\ff$. Let $\hat{\varepsilon}^{\mathfrak{a}}$ denote the Fourier transform of $\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{a}}$. For $v\in K$ totally positive, one has the formula
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eps-v}
\hat{\varepsilon}^{\mathfrak{a}v}(x)=\hat{\varepsilon}^{\mathfrak{a}}(v^{-1}x)\cdot\|v\|^{-1},
\end{equation}
as follows easily from \eqref{eq:variance}.

\textbf{Definition.} We define the Fourier transform $T\varepsilon:I\to\C$ of $\varepsilon$ by the formula
\begin{equation}\label{eq:T-transform}
(T\varepsilon)(\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\cdot x)=\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{a}\cdot\hat{\varepsilon}^{\mathfrak{a}}(x),
\end{equation}
for $\mathfrak{a}\in I_0$, $x\in\hat{K}$. [We observe that each element of $I$ is a product $\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\cdot x$, by (2.19)] To check that $T\varepsilon$ is well defined, suppose that $\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\cdot\alpha=\mathfrak{b}^{-1}\cdot\beta$; we then wish to verify that
\[
\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{a}\cdot\hat{\varepsilon}^{\mathfrak{a}}(\alpha)=\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{b}\cdot\hat{\varepsilon}^{\mathfrak{b}}(\beta).
\]
The equation $\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\cdot\alpha=\mathfrak{b}^{-1}\cdot\beta$ gives $(\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\cdot\alpha)=(\mathfrak{b}^{-1}\cdot\beta)$, so that $\alpha=v\beta$ for some $v\gg0$ by (2.17).

We find that $(\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\cdot\alpha)=(\mathfrak{b}^{-1}\cdot v^{-1}\beta)$, so that $\alpha=v^{-1}\beta t$ for some $t\in U^+$ (2.15). By \eqref{eq:eps-v}, we have
\[
\hat{\varepsilon}^{\mathfrak{a}}(\alpha)=\hat{\varepsilon}^{\mathfrak{a}}(v^{-1}\beta t)=\hat{\varepsilon}^{\mathfrak{a}v}(\beta t)\cdot\mathcal{N}v^{-1}=\hat{\varepsilon}^{\mathfrak{b}}(\beta t)\cdot\mathcal{N}v^{-1}.
\]
The required formula then follows from \eqref{eq:variance}, since $\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{a}}$ is invariant under $U^+$, and $\|t\|=1$.

We now give some formal properties of $T$ that follow directly from the definition.

\begin{proposition}[(3.5)]\label{prop:T2}
$T(T\varepsilon)(x)=\varepsilon(x\cdot(-1))$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $\eta=T\varepsilon$. For $(\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\cdot\alpha)\in I$, we have
\begin{align*}
(T\eta)(\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\cdot\alpha)&=\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{a}\cdot\hat{\eta}^{\mathfrak{a}}(\alpha)\\
&=\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{a}\int\eta(\mathfrak{a}\cdot x)\psi(x\alpha)\,dx\\
&=\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{a}\int\hat{\varepsilon}^{\mathfrak{a}}(x)\psi(x\alpha)\,dx\\
&=\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{a}}(-\alpha)=\varepsilon(\mathfrak{a}\cdot(-\alpha))\\
&=\varepsilon((\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\cdot\alpha)\cdot(-1)).\qedhere
\end{align*}
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[(3.6)]\label{prop:T-shift}
If $\eta(x)=\varepsilon(\mathfrak{b}x)$ for some $\mathfrak{b}\in I_0$, then
\[
(T\eta)(\mathfrak{a})=(T\varepsilon)(\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}^{-1})\cdot\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{b}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We have
\begin{align*}
\hat{\eta}^{\mathfrak{a}}(\alpha)&=\int\eta(\mathfrak{a}\cdot x)\psi(x\alpha)\,dx\\
&=\int\varepsilon(\mathfrak{b}\cdot(\mathfrak{a}\cdot x))\psi(x\alpha)\,dx\\
&=(T\varepsilon)((\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\cdot\alpha)\cdot\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\cdot\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{a}\\
&=(T\varepsilon)(\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\cdot\alpha\cdot\mathfrak{b}^{-1})\cdot\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{b}^{-1}.\qedhere
\end{align*}
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[(3.7)]\label{cor:T-G}
Suppose that $\eta(x)=\varepsilon(cx)$ with $c\in G$. Then $(T\eta)(y)=(T\varepsilon)(c^{-1}y)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
As noted in \S2, each $c\in G$ may be written $(\gamma)\gamma^{-1}$ for some $\gamma\in\hat{K}^*$. Since $\|\gamma^{-1}\|\cdot\mathcal{N}(\gamma)=1$, the result follows from (3.6), (3.3).
\end{proof}

Using the above properties, we will now verify that $T\varepsilon$ is locally constant and compactly supported. For the application that we ultimately have in mind, the ideal $\mathfrak{b}$ governing the support of $\varepsilon$ is $(1)$: that is, $\varepsilon$ is supported on $A$. Let us assume that this is the case, for simplicity. [If $\mathfrak{b}\neq1=(1)$, one may use (3.6) to reduce to the case $\mathfrak{b}=(1)$.] We suppose (as above) that $\varepsilon$ is defined mod $\sim_{\ff}$.

Let $\eta$ be defined by the formula
\[
\eta(\mathfrak{a})=(T\varepsilon)(\mathfrak{a}\DD^{-1}).
\]

\begin{theorem}[(3.8)]\label{thm:support}
The function $\eta$ is supported on $I^{\DD^{-1}\ff}$ and defined mod $\sim_{1}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We first verify the periodicity. If $x,y\in I$ are congruent mod $1$, we may write $x=(\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\cdot\alpha)$, $y=(\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\cdot\beta)$ for some $\mathfrak{a}\in I_0$ and elements $\alpha,\beta\in\hat{\OO}$ with $\alpha-\beta\in\hat{\OO}$. Thus the periodicity property asserted for $T\eta$ reduces to the statement that $\hat{\varepsilon}^{\mathfrak{a}}$ is periodic mod $\hat{\OO}$. As already noted, $\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{a}}$ is supported on $\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\DD^{-1}$. Looking at the formula defining $\hat{\varepsilon}^{\mathfrak{a}}$, we see that the required periodicity follows from the fact that the additive character $\psi$ is trivial on $\hat{\DD}^{-1}$.

For the question of support, it is now enough to check that $\eta$ vanishes on each ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ with $\mathfrak{a}\DD\not\subseteq\ff^{-1}\DD^{-1}$. (This remark follows from the periodicity and the fact that for $x\sim y$ mod $1$ we have $x\in I^{\DD^{-1}\ff}\iff y\in I^{\DD^{-1}\ff}$.) So we must check that $(T\varepsilon)(\mathfrak{a})=0$ provided that $\mathfrak{a}\not\supseteq\ff^{-1}\DD^{-1}$. The vanishing of $(T\varepsilon)(\mathfrak{a})$ amounts to the vanishing of the integral
\[
\int\varepsilon(\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\cdot x)\psi(x)\,dx.
\]
But the hypothesis on $\mathfrak{a}$ insures the existence of an $\alpha\in\hat{\OO}\ff$ with $\psi(\alpha)\neq1$. The integral thus vanishes because of the formula
\[
\varepsilon(\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\cdot(x+\alpha))=\varepsilon(\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\cdot(x)),
\]
cf.~(2.22).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[(3.9)]\label{cor:support}
The function $T\varepsilon$ is supported on $I^{\DD^{-1}\ff\DD^{-1}}$. It is defined modulo $\DD^{-1}$ in the sense that $(T\varepsilon)(x)=(T\varepsilon)(y)$ whenever $\DD x\sim\DD y$ mod $1$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark0}[(3.10)]\label{rem:modD}
The introduction of $\eta$ is a consequence of our failure to have defined $\sim_{\ff}$ for $\ff$ a fractional ideal. For later use we now define two elements $x,y$ of $I$ to be congruent mod $\DD^{-1}$ if $\DD x$ and $\DD y$ are congruent mod $1$.
\end{remark0}

We now begin our study of $L$-functions. Let $\varepsilon:I\to\C$ be locally constant and compactly supported. We set
\[
L(s,\varepsilon)=\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in I_0}\varepsilon(\mathfrak{a})\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{a}^{-s}
\]
for $s$ with large real part, where the sum runs over all (fractional) ideals of $K$. It is easy to rewrite $L(s,\varepsilon)$ as a linear combination of functions of the form $\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{b}^{-s}\zeta_f(s,c)$, where $\mathfrak{b}$ is some ideal of $K$ and $\zeta_f(s,c)$ is the partial zeta function of a class $c$ mod $\ff$ in the sense of Siegel. Using results of Hecke, we may thus admit \textit{a priori} that $L(s,\varepsilon)$ may be continued to a meromorphic function on $\C$, with at worst a simple pole at $s=1$ and with no other poles. We are interested in obtaining a functional equation linking $L(s,\varepsilon)$ and $L(s,T\varepsilon)$.

For this, we shall now assume that $\varepsilon$ has parity $(a_v)$ for some collection of integers $a_v=0$ or $1$, cf.~(2.24). By (3.7), $T\varepsilon$ again has parity $(a_v)$. Let $\Gamma_{\R}(s)=\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})$, and set
\begin{align}
\gamma(s)&=\prod_v\Gamma_{\R}(s+a_v),\label{eq:gamma}\\
\Lambda(s,\varepsilon)&=\gamma(s)L(s,\varepsilon),\label{eq:Lambda}
\end{align}
with the product taken over the real places of $K$.

\begin{theorem}[(3.12)]\label{thm:functional}
We have
\[
\Lambda(s,\varepsilon)=i^{a}\cdot2^{ra/2}\cdot\Lambda(1-s,T\varepsilon),
\]
where $a=\sum a_v$.
\end{theorem}

In fact, the case of primary interest for us is that where all $a_v$ are either $0$ ($\varepsilon$ even) or $1$ ($\varepsilon$ odd) and $s$ is an integer $k\ge1$ with the same parity as $\varepsilon$. In this case we will obtain the following

\begin{corollary}[(3.13)]\label{cor:L-values}
If $\varepsilon$ has parity $(-1)^k$ and $k\ge1$, then
\[
L(k,\varepsilon)=2^{r}\cdot c_k\cdot L(1-k,T\varepsilon),
\]
where
\[
c_k=\frac{(2\pi i)^{rk}}{(k-1)!^r}.
\]
\end{corollary}

Before beginning the proof of (3.12), (3.13), we remark that these equations are essentially those discussed by Siegel [38].

\begin{proof}[Proof of (3.12)]
We will in fact establish an identity between the partial $L$-function obtained by summing $\varepsilon(\mathfrak{a})\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{a}^{-s}$ over a given wide ideal class of $K$ and that made by summing $(T\varepsilon)(\mathfrak{a})\mathcal{N}\mathfrak{a}^{s-1}$ over the inverse of the class. Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be an ideal. We will prove that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:partial}
\sum_{\beta\in K^*/U}\varepsilon(\beta\mathfrak{g})|\beta|^{-s}=h(s)\sum_{\beta\in K^*/U}(T\varepsilon)(\beta\mathfrak{g}^{-1})|\beta|^{s-1},
\end{equation}
where the notation is as follows:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item $U=$ unit group of $K$
\item $h(s)=i^{a}2^{ra/2}\pi^{r(s-1)/2}\Gamma_{\R}(1-s+a)^r\Gamma_{\R}(s+a)^{-1}$
\item $|\beta|$ is the archimedean absolute value of $\beta$, i.e., $|\beta|=|\mathcal{N}\beta|$.
\end{itemize}

Let $\rho$ be the function on $(K\otimes\R)^*$ given by $\rho(\beta)=\prod_v(\sgn_v\beta)^{a_v}$. We have, by the parity hypothesis,
\begin{align}
\varepsilon(\beta\mathfrak{g})&=\varepsilon(\mathfrak{g}\cdot\beta)\rho(\beta)=\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{g}}(\beta)\rho(\beta),\label{eq:eps-rho}\\
(T\varepsilon)(\beta\mathfrak{g}^{-1})&=(T\varepsilon)(\mathfrak{g}^{-1}\cdot\beta)\rho(\beta)=(T\varepsilon)_{\mathfrak{g}^{-1}}(\beta)\rho(\beta).\label{eq:Te-rho}
\end{align}
Thus the formula to be verified reads:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:verify}
\sum_{\beta\in K^*/U^+}\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{g}}(\beta)|\beta|^{-s}\rho(\beta)=h(s)\sum_{\beta\in K^*/U^+}(T\varepsilon)_{\mathfrak{g}^{-1}}(\beta)|\beta|^{s-1}\rho(\beta),
\end{equation}
where $U^+$ is the group of totally positive units. (We prefer to sum over this latter unit group because $\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{g}}$, $\beta\mathfrak{g}$ and $\rho$ (as well as $|\cdot|$) are invariant by it, whereas only the products $\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{g}}\cdot\rho$, $\hat{\varepsilon}_{\mathfrak{g}}\rho$ need be invariant by $U$. In \eqref{eq:partial}, we have the right to replace $U$ by any subgroup of finite index in it.)

Let $\chi$ be a quasicharacter $K_{\infty}^*\to\C^*$, and let $\varphi_{\infty}$ be a function such that both it and its Fourier transform are rapidly decreasing at infinity (cf.~[39], \S2.4). For $\Res>0$, the integral
\begin{equation}\label{eq:integral}
\int_{K_{\infty}^*}\chi(x)|x|^s\varphi_{\infty}(x)\,d^*x
\end{equation}
converges to an analytic function of $s$ which has a meromorphic continuation to all of $\C$. Furthermore, there is a non-zero meromorphic function $\alpha(\chi,s)$ of $s$, independent of $\varphi_{\infty}$, such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fe-local}
\alpha(\chi,s)\int_{K_{\infty}^*}\chi^{-1}(x)|x|^{1-s}\hat{\varphi}_{\infty}(x)\,d^*x=\int_{K_{\infty}^*}\chi(x)|x|^s\varphi_{\infty}(x)\,d^*x.
\end{equation}
This formula, which is just a semi-local variant of the functional equation of [39, loc.~cit], expresses the fact that the Fourier transform of the distribution $\chi|\cdot|^{-s}=\chi_s$ on $K_{\infty}$ is proportional to $\chi^{-1}|\cdot|^{1-s}$.

\[
\rho(x)=\prod_{i=1}^r\sgn(x_i)^{a_i}.
\]
Following Tate, we compute $\alpha(\rho,s)$ by choosing $\varphi_{\infty}(x)=\prod_v(x_v^{a_v}e^{-\pi x_v^2})$. With this choice for $\varphi_{\infty}$, each integral in \eqref{eq:fe-local} may be rewritten as a product of $r$ different elementary integrals of the type computed on p.~317 of [39]. We find that
\[
\alpha(\rho,s)=h(s),
\]
with $h$ as in \eqref{eq:partial}. Thus \eqref{eq:partial} is a special case of

\begin{theorem}[(3.19)]\label{thm:general-fe}
Let $\chi:K_{\infty}^*\to\C^*$ be a quasicharacter invariant under a subgroup $U_0$ of finite index in $U$. Let $\varepsilon$ be a locally constant compactly supported function on $\hat{K}$ which is also invariant under $U_0$. Then we have the equality of meromorphic functions
\[
\sum_{\beta\in K^*/U_0}\varepsilon(\beta)\chi(\beta)|\beta|^{-s}=\alpha(\chi^{-1},1-s)\sum_{\beta\in K^*/U_0}\hat{\varepsilon}(\beta)\chi(\beta)|\beta|^{s-1}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\textbf{Remarks.} 1.~More generally, let $K$ be a global field, and let $S$ be a finite set of primes of $K$ which contains the archimedean primes. Let $\chi$ be a quasicharacter of $\prod_{v\in S}K_v^*$, invariant by a subgroup $U_0$ of finite index in the group of $S$-units of $K$. The Fourier transform $\hat{\chi}_S$ of $\chi$ is a multiple $\alpha(\chi)|\cdot|^{-1}\chi^{-1}$ of $|\cdot|^{-1}\chi^{-1}$. Let $\varepsilon$ be a $U_0$-invariant locally constant function with compact support on $\prod_{v\in S}K_v^*$, and let $\hat{\varepsilon}$ be its Fourier transform. The proof given below yields the identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:general-global}
\sum_{x\in K^*/U_0}\chi(x)\varepsilon(x)=\sum_{x\in K^*/U_0}\hat{\chi}_S(x)\hat{\varepsilon}(x),
\end{equation}
where both sides are defined by analytic continuation in the family $\chi|\cdot|^s$.

Suppose that we (formally) allow $S$ to be the set of all places of $K$, and take $U_0=K^*$. Then \eqref{eq:general-global} yields the identity $\alpha(\chi)=1$ for $\chi$ a grossencharacter. This identity is Tate's global functional equation, as rewritten by Weil [40].

2.~Our method of proof is similar to that given in Sato-Shintani [31]. A common generalization should be possible.

\begin{proof}[Proof of (3.19)]
For simplicity, we will write $U$ instead of $U_0$ for the unit group appearing in (3.19). Furthermore, let us continue to write $\chi_s=\chi\cdot|\cdot|^s$.

\textbf{Step I.} Let $\varphi_{\infty}$ be a function on $K_{\infty}$ as in \eqref{eq:integral}, chosen so that
\[
\int_{K_{\infty}^*}\chi_s(x)\varphi_{\infty}(x)\,d^*x
\]
is not identically zero. Let $\varphi$ be the function $\varepsilon\otimes\varphi_{\infty}$ on $\A$:
\[
\varphi(x)=\varphi_{\infty}(x_{\infty})\varepsilon(x_f).
\]
For each $x\in\A_f^*$ we consider the function on $\A$
\[
a\mapsto\varphi(xa)\qquad(a\in\A),
\]
whose Fourier transform is
\[
a\mapsto|x|^{-1}\hat{\varphi}(x^{-1}a).
\]
We apply the Poisson summation formula to this function and obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poisson}
\sum_{\beta\in K}\varphi(x\beta)=\sum_{\beta\in K}|x|^{-1}\hat{\varphi}(x^{-1}\beta),
\end{equation}
cf.~[39, p.~333].

\textbf{Step II.} We have an equality of meromorphic functions
\begin{equation}\label{eq:step2}
\int_{K_{\infty}^*/U}\chi_s(x)\sum_{\beta\in K^*}\varphi(x\beta)\,d^*x=\int_{K_{\infty}^*/U}\chi_s(x^{-1})|x|\sum_{\beta\in K^*}\hat{\varphi}(x\beta)\,d^*x.
\end{equation}
[Note that the integrands are $U$-invariant. E.g., if $\gamma\in U$ and $x'=\gamma x$, then $\varphi(x'\beta)$ is by definition $\varphi(x\gamma^{-1}\beta)$. Since $\varepsilon$ is invariant under $U$, we may rewrite this as $\varphi(x\beta)$.]

Let $K_{\infty,+}^*$ (resp.~$K_{\infty,-}^*$) be the subset of $K_{\infty}^*$ consisting of elements with $|x|>1$ (resp.~$|x|<1$). Over $K_{\infty,+}^*$, both integrals converge to analytic functions of $s$ because of the rapid decrease of $\varphi$, $\hat{\varphi}$ at $\infty$. Over $K_{\infty,-}^*$, the left-hand integral is well behaved for $\Res<0$ and the right hand integral for $\Res>0$. Let $L$ and $R$, respectively, denote the integrands of these integrals.

Suppose that we multiply \eqref{eq:poisson} by $\chi_s(x)$, integrate over $K_{\infty,-}^*/U$, change variables $x\mapsto x^{-1}$ on the right-hand side, and isolate the terms corresponding to $\beta=0$. We obtain
\[
\int_{K_{\infty,-}^*/U}L=\int_{K_{\infty,-}^*/U}R-\varphi(0)\int_{K_{\infty,-}^*/U}\chi_s(x)\,d^*x-\hat{\varphi}(0)\int_{K_{\infty,-}^*/U}\chi_s(x^{-1})|x|\,d^*x,
\]
for $\Res>0$. Similarly, for $\Res<0$ we have
\[
\int_{K_{\infty,-}^*/U}R=\int_{K_{\infty,-}^*/U}L+\varphi(0)\int_{K_{\infty,-}^*/U}\chi_s(x)\,d^*x-\hat{\varphi}(0)\int_{K_{\infty,-}^*/U}\chi_s(x^{-1})|x|\,d^*x.
\]
Now the point is that each integral multiplying $\varphi(0)$ and $\hat{\varphi}(0)$ is meromorphic on $\C$, so that $\int_{K_{\infty}^*/U}L$, $\int_{K_{\infty}^*/U}R$ are continuable to meromorphic functions on $\C$. Furthermore, when we take the difference of the two equations just written, the $\varphi(0)$ and the $\hat{\varphi}(0)$ terms cancel, giving the desired equality \eqref{eq:step2}.

For example, let us show that $\int_{K_{\infty}^*/U}\chi_s(x)\,d^*x$ and $\int_{K_{\infty}^*/U}\chi_s(x)\,d^*x$ are meromorphic in $s$, and that each is the negative of the other. We let $\pi:K_{\infty}^*/U\to(0,\infty)$ be the surjection $x\mapsto|x|$. By Dirichlet's theorem, the fibres of $\pi$ are compact. Hence for each $t\in(0,\infty)$, the integral
\[
\int_{\pi^{-1}(t)}\chi_s(x)\,d^*x
\]
is defined for all $s$. Because $\pi$ and $\chi_s$ are homomorphisms, the integral vanishes if $\chi$ (and hence $\chi_s$) is non-trivial on the kernel of $\pi$. In that case, by Fubini's theorem, the two integrals to be calculated are identically zero. So we can assume that $\chi$ is trivial on $\Ker\pi$, giving that $\chi(x)=|x|^{s_0}$ for some $s_0\in\C$. We then find:
\[
\int_{K_{\infty,+}^*/U}\chi_s(x)\,d^*x=\frac{V}{s-s_0}
\]
and
\[
\int_{K_{\infty,-}^*/U}\chi_s(x)\,d^*x=-\frac{V}{s-s_0},
\]
where $V$ is the volume of $\pi^{-1}(1)$. Hence our claim, and thus \eqref{eq:step2}, is verified.

\textbf{Step III.} We apply Fubini's theorem to the left hand side of \eqref{eq:step2}, obtaining
\[
\sum_{\beta\in K^*/U}\int_{K_{\infty}^*}\chi_s(x)\varphi(x\beta)\,d^*x.
\]
We replace $x$ by $\beta^{-1}x$, i.e., fix $\beta$, in the integral. It becomes
\[
\varepsilon(\beta)\chi_s(\beta)^{-1}\int_{K_{\infty}^*}\chi_s(x)\varphi_{\infty}(x)\,d^*x,
\]
so that the left-hand side of \eqref{eq:step2} may be rewritten
\[
\Big[\sum_{\beta\in K^*/U}\varepsilon(\beta)\chi_s(\beta)^{-1}\Big]\cdot\int_{K_{\infty}^*}\chi_s(x)\varphi_{\infty}(x)\,d^*x.
\]
This decomposition gives a meromorphic continuation to the first factor, i.e., the sum: the product of the two factors is meromorphic by the above, whereas the integral is meromorphic (and non-zero by the choice of $\varphi_{\infty}$). We similarly write the right-hand side of \eqref{eq:step2} as
\[
\Big[\sum_{\beta\in K^*/U}\hat{\varepsilon}(\beta)\chi_s(\beta)|\beta|^{-1}\Big]\cdot\int_{K_{\infty}^*}\chi(x^{-1})|x|^{1-s}\varphi_{\infty}(x)\,d^*x.
\]
This gives, because of \eqref{eq:fe-local}, the equality
\[
\sum_{\beta\in K^*/U}\varepsilon(\beta)\chi^{-1}(\beta)|\beta|^{-s}=\alpha(\chi^{-1},1-s)\sum_{\beta\in K^*/U}\hat{\varepsilon}(\beta)\chi(\beta)|\beta|^{s-1},
\]
which is just (3.19) with $\chi^{-1}$ and $-s$ instead of $\chi$ and $s$.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Trivial Zeros}

\begin{proposition}[(3.23)]\label{prop:trivial}
The function $\Lambda(s,\varepsilon)$ of \eqref{eq:Lambda} is holomorphic on $\C$ except in the case where $\varepsilon$ is even, i.e., where all $a_v$ vanish. In this case, $\Lambda(s,\varepsilon)$ is holomorphic for all $s\neq0,1$ and has at worst simple poles at these points.
\end{proposition}

As is well known, by considering the poles of $\Gamma$ one deduces the existence of ``trivial zeros'' for $L(s,\varepsilon)$:

\begin{corollary}[(3.24)]\label{cor:trivial}
We have $L(1-k,\varepsilon)=0$ for all even integers $k\ge1$ except when $\varepsilon$ is an even function. Similarly, if $\varepsilon$ is not an odd function, we have $L(1-k,\varepsilon)=0$ for all odd integers $k\ge1$.
\end{corollary}

[The assertion concerning $k=1$ would be false in the excluded case $K=\Q$. Note that in (3.24) the function $\varepsilon$ is still assumed to have some parity, as in (3.12)]

\begin{proof}[Proof of (3.23)]
We review the proof of (3.19), making the normalization $\chi=\rho$. The proof of the equality \eqref{eq:step2} shows that the functions in \eqref{eq:step2} are everywhere holomorphic, except for the case where $\chi$ is trivial, in which case there may be simple poles only at the points $s=0,1$. We now make in Step III of the proof of (3.19) the choice
\[
\varphi_{\infty}=\prod_v|x_v|^{a_v}e^{-\pi x_v^2}.
\]
Then we may explicitly calculate the integrals appearing in Step III, obtaining in particular the equation
\[
\int_{K_{\infty}^*}\chi_s(x)\varphi_{\infty}(x)\,d^*x=\prod_v\Gamma_{\R}(a_v-s).
\]
Except for the change of sign in $s$, the product is the $\Gamma$-factor $\gamma(s)$ used to define $\Lambda(s,\varepsilon)$. \section{An Irreducibility Theorem}

This \S\ concerns the ``Hilbert-Blumenthal'' moduli problem of classifying abelian varieties with real multiplication. We wish to complement the work of Rapoport [28] with an irreducibility theorem in characteristic $p$.

We recall that a Hilbert-Blumenthal abelian variety (or: HBAV) relative to the integer ring $\OO$ of $K$ over a base $S$ is an abelian scheme $X/S$, furnished with a homomorphism $m:\OO\hookrightarrow\End X$ making $\Lie(X/S)$ into a locally free $\OO\otimes\mathcal{O}_S$-module of rank $1$. It follows in particular from this definition that the relative dimension of $X$ over $S$ is the degree $r$ of $K$ over $\Q$. Conversely, if $S$ is of characteristic $0$ and $X/S$ is an abelian scheme of relative dimension $r$ furnished with an $m:\OO\hookrightarrow\End X$, then the condition on $\Lie(X/S)$ is automatically satisfied. Also, if $S$ is of characteristic $p>0$, then the condition is satisfied at least whenever $X$ is ordinary.

Let $X$ be a HBAV over an algebraically closed field $k$. If $N$ is prime to the characteristic of $k$, the group scheme $X_N$ of $N$-division points of $X$ is \'etale, and so may be identified with the group of its points: a free $\OO/N\OO$-module of rank $2$. Also, we attach to $X$ its polarization module $\mathscr{P}(X)$; this is the invertible $\OO$-module consisting of the symmetric $\OO$-linear homomorphisms $X\to X^*$, where $X^*$ is the dual of $X$. This module has a natural positivity, in which we declare positive those homomorphisms defined by a polarization [28, \S1].

In the special case $k=\C$, we may interpret $X_N$ and $\mathscr{P}(X)$ in terms of the homology group $T(X)=H_1(X(\C),\Z)$. For $X_N$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:XN}
X_N=\frac{1}{N}T(X)/T(X)\xrightarrow{\sim}T(X)/NT(X).
\end{equation}
For $\mathscr{P}(X)$, we regard $T(X)$ and $T(X^*)$ as being paired over $\Z$ into $\Z(1)=2\pi i\Z$. We have
\[
T(X^*)=\Hom_{\OO}(T(X),\DD^{-1}(1)),
\]
which leads to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:polarization}
\mathscr{P}(X)=\Hom_{\OO}(\wedge^2_{\OO}T(X),\DD^{-1}(1)).
\end{equation}
(The exterior power is taken over $\OO$.) The notion of positivity on $\mathscr{P}(X)$ results from the fact that the real vector space $T(X)\otimes_{\Z}\R$ has a natural complex structure [28, 1.26].

If we combine \eqref{eq:XN} and \eqref{eq:polarization}, we obtain an isomorphism
\begin{equation}\label{eq:combine}
\wedge^2_{\OO}X_N\xrightarrow{\sim}\Hom_{\OO}(\mathscr{P}(X),\DD^{-1}(1))\otimes\Z/N\Z.
\end{equation}

Over an arbitrary base $S$, the groups $(X_{\bar{s}})_N$ (with $N$ invertible on $S$) and $\mathscr{P}(X_{\bar{s}})$ fit together to give local systems $X_N$, $\mathscr{P}(X)$ (for the \'etale topology). From the $e_m$-pairings of Weil, we may construct an analogue of \eqref{eq:combine}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weil}
\wedge^2_{\OO}X_N\xrightarrow{\sim}\Hom_{\OO}(\mathscr{P}(X),\DD^{-1}(1))\otimes\mu_N.
\end{equation}

Let $N\ge3$ be an integer, and let $\mathscr{G}$ be an invertible $\OO$-module of rank $1$, given with a positivity. For each base $S$, we consider triples $(X,\lambda,\alpha)$ over $S$ consisting of: a HBAV $X/S$, a positive isomorphism $\lambda:\mathscr{P}(X)\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathscr{G}$, and a level-$N$ structure $\alpha:(\OO/N\OO)^2\xrightarrow{\sim}X_N$. Let $F[\mathscr{G},N]$ be the functor
\[
S\mapsto\{\text{isomorphism classes of triples }(X,\lambda,\alpha)\text{ over }S\}.
\]
According to [28], $F[\mathscr{G},N]$ is represented by an algebraic space $\mathscr{M}=\mathscr{M}_N[\mathscr{G}]$, smooth over $\Z[\frac{1}{N}]$. One can find a compactification $\bar{\mathscr{M}}$ of $\mathscr{M}$, proper and smooth over $\Z[\frac{1}{N}]$, so that the complement of $\mathscr{M}$ in $\bar{\mathscr{M}}$ is a relative divisor with normal crossings. (We note in passing that the level-$N$ structure is imposed in order to eliminate automorphisms.) It follows that $\mathscr{M}$ has the ``same'' geometric connected components in characteristic $p\nmid N$ as over $\C$. These components are parameterized by the set of ``invertible'' elements of the free $\OO/N\OO$-module
\[
H=(\Hom_{\OO}(\mathscr{G},\DD^{-1})\otimes\Z/N\Z)(1).
\]
Explicitly, given a triple $(X,\lambda,\alpha)$, we obtain an element of $H$ according to the following recipe. From $\alpha$ and \eqref{eq:weil} we obtain an isomorphism
\[
\OO/N\OO=\wedge^2_{\OO}(\OO/N\OO)^2\xrightarrow{\sim}\wedge^2_{\OO}X_N\xrightarrow{\sim}\Hom_{\OO}(\mathscr{P}(X),\DD^{-1})\otimes\mu_N=H',
\]
and using $\lambda$ we have also an isomorphism $H\xrightarrow{\sim}H'$. Composing the two, we obtain an isomorphism $\OO/N\OO\xrightarrow{\sim}H$, and hence a canonically given ``basis vector'' of $H$.

Let $p\nmid N$ be a prime, and let $p^n>1$ be a power of $p$. Let $\mathscr{M}^0=\mathscr{M}^0_N[\mathscr{G},p]$ be the open subset of the reduction of $\mathscr{M}$ modulo $p$ which corresponds to ordinary abelian varieties. (We recall that $X$ is ordinary if $X_p$ is an extension of an \'etale group scheme by a group scheme of multiplicative type.) We shall see below that $\mathscr{M}^0$ meets each connected component of $\mathscr{M}$ mod $p$, so that it is dense in $\mathscr{M}$ mod $p$.

If $X/S$ is an ordinary Hilbert abelian variety in characteristic $p$, then $X_{p^n}$ is locally (over $S$ for the \'etale topology) an extension of an \'etale group scheme, $\OO$-isomorphic to $\OO/p^n\OO$, by a group scheme of multiplicative type, isomorphic to $\OO\otimes\mu_{p^n}$:
\[
(X_{p^n})^{\et}\sim\OO/p^n\OO,\qquad(X_{p^n})^0\sim\OO\otimes\mu_{p^n}.
\]
Let $J=J[\mathscr{G},N,p^n]$ be the covering of $\mathscr{M}^0$ defined by:
\[
J=\Isom_{\mathscr{M}^0}(\OO/p^n\OO,(X_{p^n})^{\et}).
\]
$X/\mathscr{M}^0$ being the universal Hilbert-Blumenthal abelian variety. It is a principal homogeneous space (``torsor'') over $\mathscr{M}^0$, with structural group $(\OO/p^n\OO)^*$. It may alternately be obtained as the reduction (mod $p$) of the space $J'$ associated to the moduli problem of classifying quadruples $(X,\lambda,\alpha,\beta)$, with $X$, $\lambda$, $\alpha$ as above, and $\beta$ an ($\OO$-linear) immersion
\[
\OO\otimes\mu_{p^n}\hookrightarrow X.
\]
The space $J'$ is \'etale and quasi-finite over $\mathscr{M}$, but not finite.

\begin{theorem}[(4.5)]\label{thm:irreducible}
The covering $J[\mathscr{G},N,p^n]$ of $\mathscr{M}^0_N[\mathscr{G},p]$ is geometrically irreducible. I.e., if $\bar{\F}$ is an algebraic closure of $\F_p$, then $J$ defines an irreducible covering of each connected component of $\mathscr{M}^0\otimes_{\F_p}\bar{\F}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $q$ be large enough so that all the geometric connected components of $\mathscr{M}^0$ are defined over $\F_q$. (By the above discussion, it suffices in fact to have $\F_q$ containing the $N$-th roots of $1$.) Let $\mathscr{M}^0_i\subseteq\mathscr{M}^0\otimes_{\F_p}\bar{\F}_q$ be one of the components, and let $m\in\mathscr{M}^0_i(\F_q)$ be a base point of $\mathscr{M}^0_i$. The covering of $\mathscr{M}^0_i$ induced by $J$ is determined (up to unique isomorphism) by its fibre $J_m$ and the monodromy action of $\pi_1(\mathscr{M}^0_i,m)$ on $J_m$. This action is given by a character
\[
\rho:\pi_1(\mathscr{M}^0_i,m)\to(\OO/p^n\OO)^*.
\]
Irreducibility of the covering, after extension from $\F_q$ to $\bar{\F}_q$, amounts to the surjectivity of $\rho|_{\pi_1(\mathscr{M}^0_i\otimes\bar{\F}_q)}$. Geometric irreducibility amounts to surjectivity for all $a\ge1$: any geometric connected component of $J$ is defined over some $\F_{q^a}$.

Each closed point $x$ of $\mathscr{M}^0\otimes\F_{q^a}$ defines a Frobenius element $F_x$ in $\pi_1(\mathscr{M}^0_i\otimes\F_{q^a})$. (Although $F$ is in fact not well-defined, its image under $\rho$ is well defined.) We will prove the required surjectivity by using these Frobenius elements and their powers. (The Cebotarev density theorem tells us that we will be able to do this if the theorem is true.) The powers come in because of the following ``base change'' formula: Let $b$ be a multiple of $a$, and let $x\in\mathscr{M}^0_i(\F_{q^b})$. Then $x$ is a closed point of $\mathscr{M}^0\otimes\F_{q^a}$ but defines as well a closed point $x'$ of $\mathscr{M}^0\otimes\F_{q^b}$. Let $\F_{q^a}$ be the residue field of $x'$, so that $a\mid b$. Then we have $F_x^{b/a}=F_{x'}$ in $\pi_1(\mathscr{M}^0_i\otimes\F_{q^b})$.

Suppose that $x\in\mathscr{M}^0_i(\F_{q^b})$ corresponds to a triple $(X,\lambda,\alpha)$ over $\F_{q^b}$. Then $\rho(F_x)\in(\OO/p^n\OO)^*$ is the number giving the action of Frobenius on the \'etale group scheme $\Hom(\OO\otimes\mu_{p^n},(X_{\bar{x}})^0)$. Equivalently, we write the Frobenius endomorphism of the formal group $\hat{X}$ of $X$ as a product $q^b u$, where $u\in(\OO\otimes\Z_p)^*$. Then $\rho(F_x)$ is the image of $u$ in $(\OO/p^n\OO)^*$, cf.~[15, (42.1)]. (Depending on conventions chosen, one may prefer to take $u^{r-1}$ instead of $u$ at this point; this problem is irrelevant for our purposes.)

Notice that the recipe just given for $\rho(F_x)$ does not involve either the level $N$-structure $\alpha$ or the isomorphism $\lambda$. On the other hand, the action of $\GL(2,\OO/N\OO)$ on $\mathscr{M}^0$ deduced from
\[
g:(X,\lambda,\alpha)\mapsto(X,\lambda,\alpha\circ g^{-1})
\]
permutes the geometric connected components of $\mathscr{M}^0$. This enables us to ignore the distinction between $\mathscr{M}^0(\F_{q^a})$ and $\mathscr{M}^0(\F_{q^b})$ in finding a triple $(X,\lambda,\alpha)$ such that the associated $u$ maps mod $p^n$ to a desired element $\bar{u}$ of $(\OO/p^n\OO)^*$; we are required only to find an ordinary $X/\F_{q^b}$, for some multiple $b$ of $a$, with the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $X$ admits a level-$N$ structure $\alpha$ over $\F_{q^b}$;
\item The number $u\in(\OO\otimes\Z_p)^*$ computed for $X$ maps to the given $\bar{u}$ in $(\OO/p^n\OO)^*$;
\item The polarization module $\mathscr{P}(X)$ is isomorphic to the given $\mathscr{G}$ (as an invertible $\OO$-module with positivity).
\end{enumerate}
Carrying out this construction will prove the surjectivity, and thus the theorem.

We eliminate consideration of requirement (iii) by the following trick. Let $M$ be a positive integer, prime to $pN$, such that the ideal $M\OO$ is divisible by some ideal of $\OO$ in each strict ideal class of $K$. Assume that $X/\F_{q^b}$ satisfies (ii) and admits a level $NM$ structure over $\F_{q^b}$, but does not necessarily satisfy (iii). Choose a level $NM$ structure for $X$, and view it as a level $M$ structure plus a level $N$ structure. If $\mathfrak{c}$ is an ideal with $M\OO\subseteq\mathfrak{c}\OO$, we may divide $X$ by the image in $X$ of the group $(\OO/\mathfrak{c}\OO)\mod M$ under the immersion $(\OO/M\OO)^2\hookrightarrow X_M$. We obtain a variety $X_{\mathscr{G}}$, which satisfies (i) and (ii). It is easily seen (and at least guessed from \eqref{eq:polarization}) that the polarization module $\mathscr{P}(X')$ is isomorphic to $\mathscr{P}(X)\otimes\mathfrak{c}^{-1}$. Since we may choose $\mathfrak{c}$ in any strict ideal class of $K$, we may arrange $\mathscr{P}(X')$ to be isomorphic to the given $\mathscr{G}$. We may thus forget about condition (iii), after replacing $N$ by a suitable multiple.

To construct varieties $X$ satisfying (i) and (ii), we use the description given in [4] of the category of ordinary abelian varieties over a finite field. The equivalence of categories furnished by [4] identifies ordinary Hilbert-Blumenthal abelian varieties with pairs $(\mathscr{F},F)$, where $\mathscr{F}$ is a locally free $\OO$-module of rank $2$, and $F$ is a ``Frobenius'' endomorphism. One requires that $F$ satisfy:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\det F=q^b$, and $(\tr F)^2-4\det F<0$;
\item the completion $\mathscr{F}\otimes\Z_p$ of $\mathscr{F}$ decomposes as the sum $\mathscr{F}'\oplus\mathscr{F}''$ of two $F$-stable free $(\OO\otimes\Z_p)$-modules of rank $1$, with $F$ acting as $q^{1/b}u$ on $\mathscr{F}'$ and as $u^{-1}$ on $\mathscr{F}''$ for some $u\in(\OO\otimes\Z_p)^*$.
\end{enumerate}
[The number $u$ appearing in (b) is the number $u$ associated above to the abelian variety corresponding to $(\mathscr{F},F)$.] Varieties which admit level-$N$ structures correspond to pairs $(\mathscr{F},F)$ which satisfy in addition:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),start=3]
\item We have $F\equiv1\pmod{N}$.
\end{enumerate}

We will construct pairs $(\mathscr{F},F)$ by considering irreducible polynomials $X^2+cX+d$ ($c,d\in\OO$) and taking $\mathscr{F}$ to be the integer ring of $K[X]/(X^2+cX+d)$ and $F$ to be multiplication by $X$. To satisfy conditions (a) and (b), we need:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item $d=q^b$;
\item $c^2-4q^b\ll0$;
\item $c\in(\OO\otimes\Z_p)^*$.
\end{enumerate}
For (c), we require that $\frac{X-1}{N}$ be integral:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),start=4]
\item $N\mid(2+c)$, $N^2\mid(1+q^b+c)$.
\end{enumerate}
Then $u^{-1}$, being the unit root of $X^2+cX+d$, will be congruent to $-c$, mod $q^b$.

To complete the proof of the theorem, we pick $c\in\OO$ so that $c\equiv-2\pmod{N^2}$ and $c\equiv-\bar{u}^{-1}\pmod{p^n}$. Next we choose $b$ so that $c^2-4q^b\ll0$, $q^b\equiv1\pmod{N}$, and $p^n\mid q^b$. Conditions (1) through (4) are satisfied, and we have $u^{-1}\equiv-c\equiv-\bar{u}^{-1}\pmod{p^n}$ as required.
\end{proof}

We now define a $\Gamma_0(N)$ structure on a Hilbert-Blumenthal abelian variety to be an $\OO$-linear immersion $\OO\otimes\frac{1}{N}\Z/\Z\hookrightarrow X$. (Here $N$ is a positive integer, not necessarily prime to $p$.) Let $\mathscr{G}$ be a locally free rank-one $\OO$-module with positivity, and let $F[\mathscr{G},N]$ be the functor
\begin{align*}
S\mapsto\{\text{isomorphism classes of triples }&(X,\lambda,\alpha)\mid X\text{ is a Hilbert-Blumenthal}\\
&\text{abelian variety over }S;\;\lambda\text{ is a positive isomorphism }\mathscr{P}(X)\cong\mathscr{G};\;\alpha\\
&\text{is a }\Gamma_0(N)\text{-structure}\}.
\end{align*}
For $N$ sufficiently large, such triples have no automorphisms, and $F[\mathscr{G},N]$ is represented by an algebraic space $\mathscr{M}[\mathscr{G},N]$, smooth over $\Z$.

\begin{corollary}[(4.6)]\label{cor:irreducible}
For each prime $p$, the reduction mod $p$ of $\mathscr{M}[\mathscr{G},N]$ is geometrically irreducible.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Let us write $N=p^nN'$, where $p\nmid N'$. Since $\mathscr{M}[\mathscr{G},N]$ is surjective mod $p$ when $N$ is a multiple of $N$, we may prove the corollary with $N$ replaced by any multiple of $N$. Hence we may assume that $N'$ is so large that the moduli space $\mathscr{M}[\mathscr{G},N']$ exists.

Over the algebraic closure $\bar{\F}$ of $\F_p$, let us fix an embedding $\mu_N\hookrightarrow(\Z/N'\Z)^2$. After tensoring with $\OO$, we get a map $g:\OO\otimes\mu_N\hookrightarrow(\OO/N'\OO)^2$. Via $g$, a level $N'$ structure on a Hilbert-Blumenthal abelian variety gives rise to a $\Gamma_0(N)$-structure on the variety, so that we have a map $\mathscr{M}_{N'}[\mathscr{G}]\to\mathscr{M}[\mathscr{G},N']$ over $\bar{\F}$. If $\Gamma$ is the subgroup of $\GL(2,\OO/N'\OO)$ fixing $g$, we find an isomorphism $\mathscr{M}_{N'}[\mathscr{G}]/\Gamma\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathscr{M}[\mathscr{G},N']$ over $\bar{\F}$. Now $\Gamma$ acts transitively on the geometric connected components of $\mathscr{M}_{N'}[\mathscr{G}]$ because $\det\Gamma=(\OO/N'\OO)^*$; this gives the irreducibility of $\mathscr{M}[\mathscr{G},N']$ over $\bar{\F}$, proving the corollary in case $N=N'$. If $p^n>1$ we remark that similarly there is an isomorphism
\[
\mathscr{M}_{N'}[\mathscr{G},p^n]/\Gamma\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathscr{M}[\mathscr{G},N]\quad\text{(over $\bar{\F}$).}
\]
The theorem implies that $\Gamma$ permutes the geometric connected components of $\mathscr{M}_{N'}[\mathscr{G},p^n]$ (they are the same as those of $\mathscr{M}_{N'}[\mathscr{G}]$), so we again get the statement of the corollary.
\end{proof}
