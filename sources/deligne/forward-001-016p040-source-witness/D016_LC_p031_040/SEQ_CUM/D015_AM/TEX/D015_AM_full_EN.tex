This exposé contains a description of the variety constructed by Artin and
Mumford, and the proof that it is unirational and non-rational. It also contains
the statements of the theorems proved by Clemens and Griffiths [4], and by Manin
and Iskovskih [6], for constructing other examples. I attended talks by Artin
and Mumford on the same subject and drew heavily on them.

In everything that follows, the expression ``algebraic variety'' will mean ``a
separated scheme of finite type over \(\CC\), reduced and irreducible''. We
shall write \(k(X)\) for the field of rational functions on an algebraic
variety \(X\).

\section*{1. Rationality and unirationality}

Let \(K\) be an extension of finite type of \(\CC\). There then exists an
algebraic variety \(X\) such that \(K\) is isomorphic to \(k(X)\). \(X\) is
called a \emph{model} of \(K\). By [5], \(K\) always admits a projective and
smooth model.

Let \(K\) and \(L\) be two extensions of finite type of \(\CC\), with models
\(X\) and \(Y\). The homomorphisms \(a^*:K\hookrightarrow L\) correspond
bijectively to the dominant rational maps \(a_*:Y\dashrightarrow X\), by the
formula \(a^*(f)=f\circ a_*\). By [5], if \(X\) and \(Y\) are projective and
smooth, every rational map \(a_*:Y\dashrightarrow X\) admits a decomposition
\[
  a=b\,c_n^{-1}\cdots c_1^{-1}
\]

% Authority physical page 2 / printed page 46
\sourceimage{0.78}{P0002-A01_diagram_1_1_presentation.png}

where \(b\) and the \(c_i\) are morphisms (defined everywhere), and where
\(Y_{i+1}\) is obtained from \(Y_i\) by blowing up a smooth subvariety \(Z_i\)
(of codimension \(\geq 2\)).

If \(a^*:K\to L\) is an isomorphism, the preceding result can be applied to
\(((a^*)^{-1},Y_{n+1},X)\). By iteration, one obtains a commutative diagram of
morphisms of schemes

\sourceimage{0.83}{P0002-A02_diagram_1_2_presentation.png}

In this diagram, the horizontal arrows \(x^i,y^i\) are composites of blow-ups
with nonsingular centers, as in (1.1); the \(a^i\) and \(b^i\) are birational.

An invariant of a projective and smooth variety \(X\) is called \emph{birational}
if it depends only on \(k(X)\). The birational character of an invariant is
often verified with the aid of (1.2) (for a typical example, see 2.1).
Invariants usable for a field \(K\) are generally defined as the value of a
birational invariant of any projective and smooth model of \(K\).

\sourcehead{Definition 1.3.-} Let \(X\) be an algebraic variety of dimension
\(n\). We say that \(X\) is unirational if the following conditions are
satisfied:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \(k(X)\) is a subfield of a purely transcendental extension
  \(\CC(T_1,\ldots,T_r)\) of \(\CC\);

% Authority physical page 3 / printed page 47
\item a finite extension of \(k(X)\) is isomorphic to
  \(\CC(T_1,\ldots,T_n)\);
\item (for \(X\) projective and smooth), there exists a diagram (1.1) with
  \(Y=\PP^r(\CC)\);
\item (for \(X\) projective and smooth), there exists a diagram (1.1) with
  \(Y=\PP^n(\CC)\).
\end{enumerate}
One may suppose \(X\) projective and smooth, and one has
\[
  \text{(ii)}\Rightarrow\text{(i)}\Leftrightarrow\text{(iii)}
  \Rightarrow\text{(iv)}\Leftrightarrow\text{(ii)}.
\]

\sourcehead{Definition 1.4.-} We say that \(X\) is rational if, alternatively,
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \(k(X)\) is a purely transcendental extension of \(\CC\);
\item (for \(X\) projective and smooth), there exists a diagram (1.2) with
  \(Y=\PP^n(\CC)\).
\end{enumerate}

The most evident birational invariants do not permit one to distinguish
unirational varieties from rational varieties.

\sourcehead{Proposition 1.5.-} Let \(X\) be a projective and smooth unirational
algebraic variety of dimension \(n>0\).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item The plurigenera
\[
  P_k=\dim H^0\bigl(X,(\Omega_X^n)^{\otimes k}\bigr)\qquad(k>0)
\]
vanish. More generally,
\(H^0\bigl(X,(\Omega_X^1)^{\otimes k}\bigr)=0\) for \(k>0\)
(see [9], Remark, page 02).
\item \(X\) is simply connected ([10]).
\end{enumerate}

For \(n=1\) or \(2\), property (i) above already characterizes rational
varieties. A unirational variety of dimension \(\leq 2\) is therefore rational,
and a subextension of transcendence degree \(\leq 2\) of
\(\CC(T_1,\ldots,T_k)\) is automatically purely transcendental. More precisely,
one has the following:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
\item For \(n=1\), if \(H^0(X,\Omega_X^1)=0\), then
\(X\simeq\PP^1(\CC)\). By the Riemann--Roch theorem, for every \(x\in X\),
\(\cO(x)\) indeed defines an embedding of \(X\) into \(\PP^1(\CC)\).

% Authority physical page 4 / printed page 48
\item For \(n=2\), the Castelnuovo--Enriques criterion asserts that \(X\) is
rational if \(P_a\simeq P_2=0\), i.e. if
\[
  H^0(X,\Omega_X^1)=H^0\bigl(X,(\Omega_X^2)^{\otimes2}\bigr)=0
\]
(see [9]).
\end{enumerate}
The symbols \(P_a\simeq P_2\) and \(\Omega_X^1\) in this criterion reproduce
the printed source.

For \(n>2\), it has been very difficult to define or calculate birational
invariants capable of distinguishing between rational and unirational
varieties.

\section*{2. Some birational invariants}

\subsection*{A) Artin and Mumford}

\sourcehead{Proposition 2.1.-} The torsion subgroup
\(H^3(X,\ZZ)_{\tors}\) of \(H^3(X,\ZZ)\) is a birational invariant of the
projective and smooth variety \(X\).

If a variety \(X_2\) is obtained from a smooth variety \(X_1\) by blowing up a
smooth subvariety \(Z\subset X_1\) of codimension \(r\geq2\), then (SGA 5 VII)
\[
  H^n(X_2,\ZZ)\simeq H^n(X_1,\ZZ)
  \oplus\bigoplus_{1}^{r-1}H^{n-2i}(Z,\ZZ).
\]
The lower summation index \(1\), without an explicit \(i=\), is retained from
the printed formula. In particular,
\[
  H^3(X_2,\ZZ)\simeq H^3(X_1,\ZZ)\oplus H^1(Z,\ZZ).
\]
Since \(H^1(Z,\ZZ)\) is torsion-free, one therefore has
\[
  H^3(X_1,\ZZ)_{\tors}\simeq H^3(X_2,\ZZ)_{\tors}.
\]

Suppose that \(k(X)\) is isomorphic to \(k(Y)\), and let there be a diagram
(1.2) From it one obtains a diagram

\sourceimage{1.00}{P0004-A01_cohomology_diagram_presentation.png}

Since \(a_2^*b_1^*\) is bijective, \(b_1^*\) is injective. Since
\(b_1^*a_1^*\) is bijective

% Authority physical page 5 / printed page 49
and \(b_1^*\) injective, \(a_1^*\) is bijective, and the assertion follows.

The example of Artin and Mumford is based on the

\sourcehead{Construction 2.2.-} We shall construct a unirational variety
\(X\), projective and smooth, of dimension \(3\), such that
\[
  H^3(X,\ZZ)_{\tors}\neq0
  \qquad\bigl(\text{we shall have }H^3(X,\ZZ)\simeq\ZZ/(2)\bigr).
\]
Multiplying this variety by \(\PP^r(\CC)\), one obtains an analogous example
in every dimension \(\geq3\).

\subsection*{B) Clemens and Griffiths}

Let \(X\) be a projective and smooth variety of dimension \(3\), such that
\(H^0(X,\Omega_X^3)=0\). The Hodge decomposition of \(H^3(X,\CC)\) then
reduces to
\[
  H^3(X,\CC)=H^{2,1}\oplus H^{1,2}
\]
and, according to Weil [11], the complex torus
\[
  J(X)=H^3(X,\ZZ)\backslash H^3(X,\CC)/H^{2,1}
\]
is an abelian variety: the \emph{intermediate Jacobian} of \(X\). Let
\[
  \psi:H^3(X,\ZZ)\otimes H^3(X,\ZZ)\longrightarrow
  H^6(X,\ZZ)\simeq\ZZ
\]
be the cup product. By the Poincaré duality theorem, the alternating form on
\(H^3(X,\ZZ)/\text{torsion}\) deduced from \(\psi\) has discriminant one. If
\(H^1(X,\ZZ)=0\), it follows from Weil [11] that this form defines a principal
polarization on \(J(X)\).

\sourcehead{Proposition 2.3 ([4] 3.26).-} If \(X\) is rational, the polarized
abelian variety \(J(X)\) is a product of Jacobians.

In the proof sketch that follows, the sign \(\simeq\) denotes an isomorphism of
\emph{polarized} abelian varieties. One verifies successively:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
\item \(J(\PP^3(\CC))=0\).
\item If \(Y''\) is obtained from the nonsingular projective variety \(Y'\) by
blowing up

% Authority physical page 6 / printed page 50
a smooth curve \(Z\) with Jacobian \(J(Z)\) (respectively, a point), one has
([4] 3.11)
\[
  J(Y'')\simeq J(Y')\times J(Z)
  \qquad\bigl(\text{respectively, }J(Y'')\simeq J(Y')\bigr).
\]
\item If a morphism \(b:Y'\to X\) is birational, \(J(X)\) is a direct factor
in \(J(Y')\): there exists a principally polarized abelian variety of the
principal series \(B\) such that
\[
  J(Y')\simeq J(X)\times B.
\]
\item Let \((A_i)_{1\leq i\leq n}\) be nonzero principally polarized abelian
varieties. Suppose that no \(A_i\) admits a decomposition
\(A_i\simeq A_i'\times A_i''\) (for example, that \(A_i\) is a Jacobian). Then
every decomposition of \(A=\prod A_i\) is of the form
\[
  A\simeq\left(\prod_{i\in I}A_i\right)
  \times\left(\prod_{i\notin I}A_i\right)
  \quad\text{for }I\subset[1,n]\quad([4]~3.23).
\]
\end{enumerate}

If \(X\) is rational, there exists a diagram 2.1 with \(Y=\PP^3(\CC)\) and
\(b\) birational. By a) and b), \(J(Y_{n+1})\) is a product of Jacobians. By c)
and d), \(J(X)\) is one as well. The reference ``diagram 2.1'' reproduces the
printed source.

Take \(X\) to be a cubic hypersurface in \(\PP^4(\CC)\). One has
\[
  H^1(X,\ZZ)=H^3(X,\ZZ)_{\tors}=H^0(X,\Omega_X^3)=0,
  \qquad
  \dim J(X)=\dim H^1(X,\Omega_X^2)=5,
\]
and \(X\) is unirational by [7]. Let \(S\) be the surface parametrizing the
lines contained in \(X\), and let \(\alpha:S\to\Alb(S)\) be the map of \(S\)
into its Albanese variety. One of the essential results of [4] is the following

\sourcehead{Theorem 2.4 ([4] 11.19 and 13.4).-} There exists an isomorphism
\[
  J(X)\xrightarrow{\sim}\Alb(S),
\]
which carries the divisor \(\Theta\) of \(J(X)\) to the image of \(S\times S\)
under the map
\[
  (x,y)\xrightarrow{\delta}\alpha(x)-\alpha(y).
\]

% Authority physical page 7 / printed page 51
Clemens and Griffiths then manage to use results of [1] to prove that
\((\Alb(S),\delta(S\times S))\) cannot be the polarized Jacobian of a curve.
Another proof of the irrationality of \(X\) is suggested in the appendix to
[4]. It is a matter of proving

\sourcehead{Theorem 2.5.-} The singular locus of the divisor \(\Theta\) of
\(J(X)\) has dimension \(0\), hence codimension \(>4\). It is even reduced to
the image under \(\delta\) of the diagonal of \(S\times S\).

It is known that this is never the case for the divisor \(\Theta\) of a
Jacobian.

\subsection*{C) Manin and Iskovskih}

The group of birational automorphisms of a variety \(X\) (equal to the group of
\(\CC\)-automorphisms of \(k(X)\)) is a birational invariant of \(X\). It is
this invariant that Manin and Iskovski use to show that a smooth quartic
hypersurface in \(\PP^4(\CC)\) is never rational:

\sourcehead{Theorem 2.6 ([6]).-} Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be two smooth quartic
hypersurfaces in \(\PP^4(\CC)\). Every birational isomorphism
\(a:X\longrightarrow Y\) is automatically biregular.

Since certain quartic hypersurfaces are unirational, [7] this theorem provides
a new example of unirational non-rational varieties.

\section*{3. The example of Artin and Mumford}

Consider in \(\PP^2(\CC)\) a configuration consisting of
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
\item two smooth cubic curves \(C_1\) and \(C_2\), with equations \(f_1=0\) and
\(f_2=0\), meeting transversely;
\item a smooth conic \(Q\), with equation \(q=0\), which meets each \(C_i\) in
three

% Authority physical page 8 / printed page 52
distinct points of tangency.
\end{enumerate}

We shall construct
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
\item a vector bundle \(V\) of rank \(3\) on \(\PP^2(\CC)\);
\item a quadratic form \(\Phi:V\to\mathcal L\) (\(\mathcal L\) an invertible
sheaf) of discriminant \(f_1f_2\) (if \(\Delta\), a section of
\(\mathcal L^{\otimes3}\otimes(\bigwedge^3V)^{\otimes(-2)}\), is the
discriminant, the subscheme of \(\PP^2(\CC)\) with equation \(\Delta=0\) is
\(C_1\cup C_2\)).
\end{enumerate}

Let \(X\subset\PP(V^*)\) be the conic bundle over \(\PP^2(\CC)\), degenerating
along \(C_1\cup C_2\), with homogeneous equation \(\Phi=0\). The following
conditions will be satisfied.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*),start=3]
\item \(\Phi\) has rank \(\geq2\) at every point: for
\(s\in C_1\cup C_2\), the fibre \(X_s=f^{-1}(s)\) is the union of two
concurrent lines; the only singular points of \(X\) are the singular points of
the fibres \(X_s\) for \(s\in C_1\cap C_2\).
\item Let \(S\) be the double cover of \(\PP^2(\CC)\), ramified along \(Q\),
and let \(X_S=X\times_{\PP^2(\CC)}S\). Then \(X_S/S\) has a section

\sourceimage{0.48}{P0008-A01_section_diagram_presentation.png}

\item For \(s\in(C_1\cup C_2)-Q\), the two points \(\alpha(t)\) for which
\(\pi(t)=s\) lie in the two irreducible components of \(X_s\).
\end{enumerate}

Locally (for the usual topology) on \(C_1\cup C_2\), the restriction of the
bundle \(X\) to \(C_1\cup C_2\) is obtained by gluing two projective-line
bundles along a section. However, when one traverses a loop in
\(C_1\cup C_2\), these two bundles may be exchanged: there exists an unramified
double cover \((C_1\cup C_2)^*\) of \(C_1\cup C_2\) whose two local sections
correspond, locally, to the two projective-line bundles of which
\(X|_{C_1\cup C_2}\) is the union. Let \(\pi_i:C_i^*\to C_i\)

% Authority physical page 9 / printed page 53
be the double cover of \(C_i\) induced by \((C_1\cup C_2)^*\). We shall use
from e) only the following corollary

\sourcehead{Lemma 3.1.-} \(C_i^*\) is irreducible.

By e), \(C_i^*\) is the normalization of the double cover of \(C_i\) induced by
\(S\). The lemma follows from the fact that the section \(q\) of
\(\cO(2)|_{C_i}\) is not the square of a section of \(\cO(1)|_{C_i}\); if
\(q\) were a square, the three intersection points of \(Q\) with \(C_i\) would
indeed be collinear.

To the double cover \(C_i^*\) of \(C_i\) correspond a character of order \(2\)
of the fundamental group of \(C_i\) and a local system \(A_i\), locally
isomorphic to \(\ZZ\), defined by the exact sequence of sheaves on \(C_i\)
\[
  0\longrightarrow A_i\longrightarrow\pi_{i*}\ZZ
  \xrightarrow{\Tr}\ZZ\longrightarrow0,
\]
where \(\Tr\) is the ``sum of the values on the two sheets of the covering''.
One has
\[
\tag{3.2}
  H^0(C_i,A_i)=0,\qquad H^1(C_i,A_i)=\ZZ/2,\qquad
  H^2(C_i,A_i)=\ZZ/2.
\]

We shall use d) only to prove the following fact

\sourcehead{Lemma 3.3.-} \(X\) is unirational.

The surface \(S\) is a quadric in \(\PP^3(\CC)\), hence is rational. Since
\(X_S/S\) has a section, there is a birational isomorphism
\(X_S\sim S\times\PP^1(\CC)\), and \(X\) is the image of the rational variety
\(X_S\).

The nine singular points of \(X\) are ordinary quadratic points. Each can be
resolved as follows (cf. [3])
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
\item Blow it up; this resolves \(X\) and replaces the singular point by a
quadric \(P\), isomorphic to \(\PP^1(\CC)\times\PP^1(\CC)\).
\item Choose a projection \(P\xrightarrow{\beta}\PP^1(\CC)\), and contract in
the blow-up \(\widetilde X_1\) of \(X\) the fibres of this projection; this is
possible because \(\beta\) is smooth, because its

% Authority physical page 10 / printed page 54
fibres are isomorphic to \(\PP^1(\CC)\), and because the normal bundle of \(P\)
in \(\widetilde X_1\) induces on each fibre a bundle \(\cO(-1)\) ([8]).
\end{enumerate}

Let \(\widetilde X\) be the resulting variety. There is a map from
\(\widetilde X\) into \(X\), and \(\widetilde X\) is obtained from \(X\) by
replacing each singular point by a projective line.

The fibres of the projection \(p:\widetilde X\to\PP^2(\CC)\) are of the
following three types

\sourceimage{1.00}{P0010-A01_fiber_types_presentation.png}

\begin{center}\small
English key: \(s\notin C_1\cup C_2\), a conic;
\(s\in C_1\cup C_2\) and \(s\notin C_1\cap C_2\), two concurrent lines;
\(s\in C_1\cap C_2\), three lines as shown.
\end{center}

When one passes from the generic point \(\eta\) of \(\PP^2(\CC)\) to the
generic point \(\eta_i\) of \(C_i\) (\(i=1,2\)), and to
\(s\in C_1\cap C_2\), the irreducible components specialize as indicated in the
following diagram

\sourceimage{0.72}{P0010-A02_specialization_diagram_3_4_presentation.png}

(the crosses represent irreducible components, all the multiplicities being
one). The tedious verification of this local result is omitted. From it one
obtains

\sourcehead{Lemma 3.5.-} One has
\[
  R^0p_*\ZZ=\ZZ,\qquad R^1p_*\ZZ=0,
\]
and an exact sequence of sheaves
\[
  0\longrightarrow A_1\oplus A_2\longrightarrow R^2p_*\ZZ
  \xrightarrow{\Tr}\ZZ\longrightarrow0.
\]

We can now use the Leray spectral sequence of \(p\) to compute
\(H^*(\widetilde X,\ZZ)\). The essential fact is the following (which implies
the irrationality of \(X\)).

% Authority physical page 11 / printed page 55
\sourcehead{Lemma 3.6.-} One has
\[
  H^3(\widetilde X,\ZZ)=\ZZ/(2).
\]

Let us compute the \(E_2\)-terms of the Leray spectral sequence of \(p\). One
has
\[
\begin{aligned}
R^0p_*\ZZ=\ZZ&\quad\text{hence}\quad
E_2^{p,0}=\ZZ,0,\ZZ,0,\ZZ\quad\text{for }p=0\text{ to }4,\\
R^1p_*\ZZ=0&\quad\text{hence}\quad E_2^{p,1}=0.
\end{aligned}
\]
To compute \(E_2^{p,2}=H^p(R^2p_*\ZZ)\), one uses the long exact sequence
defined by the short exact sequence of sheaves 3.5. One finds
\[
\begin{gathered}
0\longrightarrow E^{0,2}\xrightarrow{a}\ZZ\xrightarrow{j}
\ZZ/2\oplus\ZZ/2\longrightarrow E^{1,2}\longrightarrow0,\\
0\longrightarrow\ZZ/2\oplus\ZZ/2\longrightarrow E^{2,2}
\longrightarrow\ZZ\longrightarrow0,\\
E^{3,2}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad E^{4,2}=\ZZ.
\end{gathered}
\]
One easily verifies that \(a(E^{0,2})=2\ZZ\) (this fact is, moreover, not
necessary for the proof that \(H^3(\widetilde X,\ZZ)_{\tors}\neq0\)). The
\(E_2^{pq}\) are therefore as follows

\sourceimage{0.78}{P0011-A01_leray_e2_table_presentation.png}

The differentials \(d_r\) can only be zero, so that
\(E_2^{pq}=E_\infty^{pq}\), and the lemma follows.

It is apparent that it was essential to have two curves
\(C_i\subset\PP^2(\CC)\) along which \(X\) degenerates, for a group \(\ZZ/2\)
is killed in passing from
\[
H^1\bigl(\Ker(R^2p_*\ZZ\to\ZZ)\bigr)
\quad\text{to}\quad H^1R^2p_*\ZZ=E^{1,2}.
\]

It remains to carry out the promised constructions. Artin and Mumford know how
to prove \emph{a priori} the existence of a conic bundle of the required type. I
shall confine myself to giving equations that define one.

% Authority physical page 12 / printed page 56
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*)]
\item The image of \(f_1f_2\) in \(H^0(Q,\cO(6))\) is the square of
\(g_1\in H^0(Q,\cO(3))\), because \(Q\) is rational and the zeros of
\(f_1f_2\) on \(Q\) are double. This \(g_1\) lifts to
\(g\in H^0(\PP^2(\CC),\cO(3))\) because
\(H^1(\PP^2(\CC),\cO(1))=0\). Since \(f_1f_2-g^2\) vanishes on \(Q\), it is a
multiple of \(q\):
\[
  f_1f_2=g^2+qd.
\]
\item Take
\[
  V=\cO(-1)\oplus\cO(-2)\oplus\cO,
\]
and the form
\[
  \Phi:V\longrightarrow\cO:(x,y,z)\longmapsto
  qx^2+2gxy-dy^2-z^2.
\]
One has \(\Delta=g^2+qd=f_1f_2\). At a point where \(\Phi\) had rank \(\leq1\),
one would have \(\Delta=q=d=0\), whence \(g=0\), and
\(\Delta=g^2+qd\) would have a double zero. This is impossible because
\(C_1\cap C_2\cap Q=\varnothing\). Finally, \(S\) is identified with the
subscheme of \(X\) having equation \(y=0\), and d) and e) follow.
\end{enumerate}
