\section*{1. Statement of the results}

Let $V$ be a hypersurface of degree $d$ in projective space $\mathbf P^n$, and let $\mu$ be the integer part of $n/d$. If the base field is a finite field $\mathbf F_q$, a theorem of J.~Ax~[1], refining an earlier theorem of E.~Warning, is equivalent to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:1.1}
\# V(\mathbf F_q) \equiv \sum_0^n q^i \pmod{q^\mu}.
\tag{1.1}
\end{equation}
It would amount to the same thing to take at the right-hand side the sum from $0$ to $n-1$. If $U$ is the complement $\mathbf P^n-V$ of $V$, this congruence can be rewritten more simply as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:1.2}
\# U(\mathbf F_q) \equiv 0 \pmod{q^\mu}.
\tag{1.2}
\end{equation}

In this article we prove a Hodge-theoretic analogue of \eqref{eq:1.1} and \eqref{eq:1.2}. The analogy is based on Grothendieck's conjecture (extended to mixed motives) that if the de Rham realization of a motive $M$ satisfies $M_{\mathrm{DR}} = F^\mu M_{\mathrm{DR}}$, then $M$ is the tensor product of the Tate motive $\mathbf Q(-\mu)$ with an effective motive; consequently, after reduction modulo $p$ to $\mathbf F_q$, the Frobenius eigenvalues should be divisible by $q^\mu$. This conjecture, together with Dwork's work, lies behind Katz's conjecture (cf.~[11, 2.9] and B.~Mazur~[13]).

Assume now that the base field is $\mathbf C$. In rational or complex cohomology, the restriction morphism
\[
\mathrm H^*(\mathbf P^n) \to \mathrm H^*(V)
\]
is injective, except in degree $i=2n$. Indeed, its composite with the Gysin morphism $\mathrm H^*(V)\to \mathrm H^*(\mathbf P^n)$ is multiplication by the cohomology class of $V$, namely $d\eta$ where $\eta = c_1\mathscr O(1)$, and $\mathrm H^*(\mathbf P^n)=\mathbf Z[\eta]/(\eta^{n+1})$.

The restriction morphism sits in a long exact sequence of mixed Hodge structures:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:1.3}
\cdots \to \mathrm H_c^i(U) \to \mathrm H^i(\mathbf P^n) \to \mathrm H^i(V) \xrightarrow{\partial} \mathrm H_c^{i+1}(U) \to \cdots
\tag{1.3}
\end{equation}

Our analogue of \eqref{eq:1.1} is the following.

\begin{theorem}
The group $\Coker(\mathrm H^*(\mathbf P^n)\to \mathrm H^*(V))$ has Hodge filtration $\ge \mu$.
\end{theorem}

A mixed Hodge structure $H$ is said to have Hodge filtration $\ge \mu$ if $H = F^\mu H$. Exact sequence \eqref{eq:1.3} shows that the theorem is equivalent to the following analogue of \eqref{eq:1.2}.

\begin{variant}[1]
The compactly supported cohomology $\mathrm H_c^*(U)$ has Hodge filtration $\ge \mu$.
\end{variant}

The variety $U$ is smooth of pure dimension $n$. By Poincar\'e duality, $\mathrm H_c^i(U)$ and $\mathrm H^{2n-i}(U)$ are dual to one another, with values in the Hodge structure $\mathbf Q(-n)$ of pure Hodge type $(n,n)$. The theorem is therefore also equivalent to

\begin{variant}[2]
The cohomology of $U$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:1.4}
F^{n-\mu+1}\mathrm H^*(U)=0.
\tag{1.4}
\end{equation}
\end{variant}

If $V$ is the subvariety of $\mathbf P^n$ defined by equations $f_i=0$ of degrees $d_i$ ($1\le i\le r$), J.~Ax deduces from his theorem, applied to the hypersurface defined by the product of the $f_i$, that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:1.5}
\# V(\mathbf F_q) \equiv \sum_0^n q^i \pmod{q^\mu}
\tag{1.5}
\end{equation}
for
\[
\mu = \Bigl[\frac{n}{\sum d_i}\Bigr].
\]
For the complement $U$ of $V$, this is equivalent to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:1.6}
\# U(\mathbf F_q) \equiv 0 \pmod{q^\mu}.
\tag{1.6}
\end{equation}

Likewise, from Variant~1 of Theorem~1 and the spectral sequence defined by the open covering of $U$ by the open sets $f_i\neq 0$ in $\mathbf P^n$, one deduces that over $\mathbf C$ the compactly supported cohomology of $U$ has Hodge filtration $\ge [n/\sum d_i]$.

In the case of base field $\mathbf F_q$, N.~Katz~[11] proved that \eqref{eq:1.5} and \eqref{eq:1.6} remain true if $[n/\sum d_i]$ is replaced by the smallest integer $\ge (n+1-\sum d_i)/\sup(d_i)$. If $d_1$ is the largest of the $d_i$, this integer is
\[
\Bigl[\frac{n-\sum_2^r d_i}{d_1}\Bigr].
\]
We do not know how to prove the Hodge-theoretic analogue of this refinement --- except when $V$ is smooth, in which case the result (SGA~7 XI 2.5) predates~[11]. Added during revision: H.~Esnault has just proved it when $V$ has codimension $r$. Her approach is, roughly speaking, dual to ours, and has the advantage of requiring much less precise control over the desingularization procedure (cf.~2.8).

\medskip

Let $X$ be smooth over $\mathbf C$, let $D$ be a divisor, let $i$ denote the inclusion of $D$ in $X$, and let $j:U\subset X$ be the inclusion of the open complement. By A.~Grothendieck~[8], one has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:1.7}
\mathrm H^*(U,\Omega_U^*) \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathrm H^*(U).
\tag{1.7}
\end{equation}
On the left, one uses the Zariski topology on $U$ and the hypercohomology of the algebraic de Rham complex. On the right, cohomology means the complex cohomology of the usual topological space $U(\mathbf C)$. The arrow is defined by the continuous map $\varepsilon:U(\mathbf C)\to U$ and the natural morphism from $\varepsilon^*\Omega_U^*$ to the resolution $\Omega_{U(\mathbf C)}^*$ of the constant sheaf $\mathbf C$.

Since $j$ is affine, one has $R^i j_*\Omega_U^p = 0$ for $i>0$, hence $j_*\Omega_U^*\xrightarrow{\sim} Rj_*\Omega_U^*$ and therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:1.8}
\mathrm H^*(X, j_*\Omega_U^*) \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathrm H^*(U,\Omega_U^*).
\tag{1.8}
\end{equation}
The arrow is restriction to $U$. Altogether, one obtains
\begin{equation}\label{eq:1.9}
\mathrm H^*(X, j_*\Omega_U^*) \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathrm H^*(U).
\tag{1.9}
\end{equation}

For every subcomplex $P$ of $j_*\Omega_U^*$ one thus has a map
\[
\mathrm H^*(X,P) \to \mathrm H^*(U)
\]
defined either via \eqref{eq:1.9} or via \eqref{eq:1.7} followed by restriction to $U$.

Each sheaf $j_*\Omega_U^p$ is the increasing union of the $\Omega_X^p(aD)$ ($a\ge 0$). The \emph{pole-order filtration} $P$ (or $P_D$, if the choice of $D$ is ambiguous) on $j_*\Omega_U^*$ is the decreasing filtration defined by
\[
P^s j_*\Omega_U^p =
\begin{cases}
0, & p<s,\\[4pt]
\Omega_X^p((p-s+1)D), & p\ge s.
\end{cases}
\]
It is decreasing, $P^s=0$ for $s>\dim(X)$, and $j_*\Omega_U^*$ is the union of the $P^s$.

It is known that if $D$ is smooth, the inclusion of filtered complexes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:1.10}
(\Omega_X^*(\log D),F) \hookrightarrow (j_*\Omega_U^*,P)
\tag{1.10}
\end{equation}
is a filtered quasi-isomorphism ([4, II, 3.13], inspired by~[9]; the reader will also find there a more complicated analogue when $D$ is a normal-crossings divisor).

Recall that for any normal-crossings divisor $D$, $\Omega_X^*(\log D)\subset j_*\Omega_X^*$ is the subsheaf of differential forms $\alpha$ such that both $\alpha$ and $d\alpha$ have at most simple poles along $D$. Another description, when $D$ is smooth, is
\[
\Omega_X^*(\log D) = \Ker(\Omega_X^*\to i_*\Omega_D^*)(D).
\]
The Hodge filtration $F$ is the stupid filtration by the truncations $\sigma_{\ge p}$.

\begin{theorem}
Let $X$ be smooth proper and let $D\subset X$ be a divisor. With the notation above, for every $s$, the image of $\mathrm H^*(X,P^s(j_*\Omega_U^*))$ in $\mathrm H^*(U,\mathbf C)$ contains $F^s\mathrm H^*(U,\mathbf C)$.
\end{theorem}

If $D$ is smooth, it follows from the definitions and from the filtered quasi-isomorphism \eqref{eq:1.10} that
\[
\mathrm H^*(X, P^s j_*\Omega_U^*) \xrightarrow{\sim} F^s\mathrm H^*(U,\mathbf C).
\]

Let us deduce Variant~2 of Theorem~1 from Theorem~2. It suffices to show that, with the notation of Theorem~1,
\[
R\Gamma(\mathbf P^n, P^{n-\mu+1}j_*\Omega_U^*) = 0.
\]
A fortiori, it suffices to show that for each $p$ one has
\[
R\Gamma(\mathbf P^n, P^{n-\mu+1}j_*\Omega_U^p)=0.
\]
The sheaves whose cohomology must be taken are either zero or of the form $\Omega^p(a)$ with $0\le p\le n$, $a>0$, and $a=(p-(n-\mu+1)+1)d$. It is known that $R\Gamma\,\Omega^p(a)=0$ if $0<a\le p$ (R.~Bott~[3, Prop.~14.4, p.~246]; for an algebraic proof, valid in all characteristics, see SGA~7 XI 1.1). It remains to verify that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:1.11}
(p-(n-\mu+1)+1)d \le p.
\tag{1.11}
\end{equation}
As $p$ increases, the left-hand side grows faster than the right-hand side, so it is enough to consider the case $p=n$. Then \eqref{eq:1.11} reduces to the inequality $\mu d\le n$, which is precisely the definition of $\mu$.

\section*{2. Proof of Theorem 2}

\begin{proposition}[2.1]
Let $X$ be smooth, $Y\subset X$ a smooth closed subscheme, $i$ the inclusion of $Y$ into $X$, $f:\widetilde X\to X$ the blow-up of $X$ along $Y$, and $\widetilde Y=f^{-1}(Y)\subset \widetilde X$ the exceptional divisor. Then on $\widetilde X$ one has
\[
f_*\Omega_{\widetilde X}^p(\log \widetilde Y)(-\widetilde Y) = \Ker(\Omega_X^p \to i_*\Omega_Y^p)
\]
and
\[
R^i f_*\Omega_{\widetilde X}^p(\log \widetilde Y)(-\widetilde Y)=0 \qquad\text{for } i>0.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Using again $i$ for the inclusion of $\widetilde Y$ into $\widetilde X$, one has
\[
\Omega_{\widetilde X}^p(\log \widetilde Y)(-\widetilde Y)=\Ker(\Omega_{\widetilde X}^p\to i_*\Omega_{\widetilde Y}^p),
\]
hence a morphism
\[
\Ker(\Omega_X^p\to i_*\Omega_Y^p) \to f_*\Omega_{\widetilde X}^p(\log \widetilde Y)(-\widetilde Y),
\]
and it is enough to show that this induces an isomorphism from $\Ker(\Omega_X^p\to i_*\Omega_Y^p)$, placed in degree $0$, to $Rf_*\Omega_{\widetilde X}^p(\log \widetilde Y)(-\widetilde Y)$.

The proposition is local for the 'etale topology. It is therefore enough to treat the case where $X$ is affine space $\mathbf A^{n_1+n_2}=\mathbf A^{n_1}\times \mathbf A^{n_2}$ and $Y=\{0\}\times \mathbf A^{n_2}$. K\"unneth formulas then reduce matters to the case $n_2=0$: the pair $(X,Y)$ is obtained from $(\mathbf A^{n_1},0)$ by product with $\mathbf A^{n_2}$, and the direct sum over $p$ of the sheaves under consideration is obtained from the corresponding sum for $(\mathbf A^{n_1},0)$ by external tensor product with $\bigoplus \Omega_{\mathbf A^{n_2}}^p$. We may therefore assume, and do assume, that $Y$ is a point.

When $Y$ is a point, we give two proofs of 2.1. The first has the advantage of working in every characteristic. The second explains why 2.1 is ``obvious'' from the point of view of Hodge theory.

\smallskip
\emph{First proof.} Assume $Y$ is a point. For $p>0$, a local calculation shows that the morphism $f^*\Omega_X^p\to \Omega_{\widetilde X}^p$ induces an isomorphism
\[
f^*\Omega_X^p \xrightarrow{\sim} \Ker(\Omega_{\widetilde X}^p\to i_*\Omega_{\widetilde Y}^p).
\]
Since $\Omega_X^p$ is locally free and $\Omega_Y^p=0$, the desired statement reduces to
\[
\mathscr O_X \xrightarrow{\sim} Rf_*\mathscr O_{\widetilde X}.
\]
For $p=0$, one uses the long exact sequence deduced from
\[
0\to \Ker(\mathscr O_{\widetilde X}\to i_*\mathscr O_{\widetilde Y}) \to \mathscr O_{\widetilde X} \to i_*\mathscr O_{\widetilde Y}\to 0,
\]
together with the same formula $\mathscr O_X\xrightarrow{\sim} Rf_*\mathscr O_{\widetilde X}$ and the fact that for the projective space $\widetilde Y$, the cohomology $H^*(\widetilde Y,\mathscr O_{\widetilde Y})$ is reduced to $\mathbf C$ in degree $0$.

\smallskip
\emph{Second proof.} The relation with Hodge theory is given by the following lemma.

\begin{lemma}[2.2]
Let $S$ be smooth projective, let $i:T\subset S$ be a smooth closed subscheme, and put $U:=S-T$. The subcomplex $\Ker(\Omega_S^*\to i_*\Omega_T^*)$ of the de Rham complex has hypercohomology equal to the compactly supported cohomology $\mathrm H_c^*(U)$; the spectral sequence defined by the stupid filtration $\sigma_{\ge p}$ degenerates at $E_1$ and abuts to the Hodge filtration on $\mathrm H_c^*(U)$.
\end{lemma}

Apply this lemma to $(X,Y)$ and $(\widetilde X,\widetilde Y)$, with $X$ smooth projective and $Y$ a point. Because $\widetilde X-\widetilde Y\xrightarrow{\sim} X-Y$, one has $\mathrm H_c^*(X-Y)\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathrm H_c^*(\widetilde X-\widetilde Y)$ and, passing to $\mathrm{Gr}_F$, one obtains
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.2.1}
R\Gamma(X,\Ker(\Omega_X^*\to i_*\Omega_Y^*)) \xrightarrow{\sim} R\Gamma(\widetilde X, \Ker(\Omega_{\widetilde X}^*\to i_*\Omega_{\widetilde Y}^*)).
\tag{2.2.1}
\end{equation}
The right-hand side is also
\[
R\Gamma(X, Rf_*\Ker(\Omega_{\widetilde X}^*\to i_*\Omega_{\widetilde Y}^*)).
\]
Let $C$ be the cone of the morphism
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.2.2}
\Ker(\Omega_X^*\to i_*\Omega_Y^*) \to Rf_*\Ker(\Omega_{\widetilde X}^*\to i_*\Omega_{\widetilde Y}^*).
\tag{2.2.2}
\end{equation}
Applying $R\Gamma$ to the distinguished triangle defining it, one deduces from \eqref{eq:2.2.1} that $R\Gamma C=0$. On the other hand, $C$ --- or rather its cohomology sheaves --- is supported at the point $Y$. From $R\Gamma C=0$ one concludes that $C=0$: morphism \eqref{eq:2.2.2} is an isomorphism. This completes the second proof of 2.1, modulo 2.2.

Lemma~2.2 comes from the fact that the simple complex associated with the double complex $\Omega_S^*\to i_*\Omega_T^*$, together with the weight filtration
\[
0 \subset W_{-1}=s(0\to i_*\Omega_T^*) \subset W_0=s(\Omega_S^*\to i_*\Omega_T^*),
\]
and the Hodge filtration by the subcomplexes
\[
s(\sigma_{\ge p}\Omega_S^* \to \sigma_{\ge p} i_*\Omega_T^*)
\]
is a cohomological mixed Hodge complex. This implicitly includes the rational structure given by the filtered complex $\mathbf Q\to i_*\mathbf Q$ with $W^{-1}: 0\to i_*\mathbf Q$.

The morphism
\[
(\Ker(\Omega_S^*\to i_*\Omega_T^*), \sigma_{\ge p}) \to (s(\Omega_S^*\to i_*\Omega_T^*),F)
\]
is a filtered quasi-isomorphism. This simply expresses the fact that $\Omega_S^*\to i_*\Omega_T^*$ is an epimorphism. This suffices to establish 2.2 for a functorial mixed Hodge structure on $\mathrm H_c^*(U)$. This statement, slightly less precise than 2.2, is enough for the application to 2.1.
\end{proof}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{2.3.} Let $X$ be smooth, let $V\subset X$ be a divisor, and let $Y\subset X$ be a smooth closed subscheme contained in the singular locus of $V$. Let $f:\widetilde X\to X$ be the blow-up of $X$ along $Y$, let $\widetilde Y=f^{-1}(Y)$ be the exceptional divisor, and let $\widetilde V=f^{-1}(V)$. Because $Y$ is contained in the singular locus of $V$, one has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.3.1}
2\widetilde Y \le \widetilde V.
\tag{2.3.1}
\end{equation}
Let $V_1=\widetilde V-\widetilde Y$. By \eqref{eq:2.3.1} one has
\[
(V_1)_{\mathrm{red}} = (f^{-1}V)_{\mathrm{red}}.
\]
Let $U=X-V = \widetilde X-\widetilde V$, let $j$ denote its inclusion into $X$, and let $\widetilde j$ denote its inclusion into $\widetilde X$. We write $P$ for both pole-order filtrations, that on $j_*\Omega_U^*$ relative to $V$ and that on $\widetilde j_*\Omega_U^*$ relative to $V_1$.

\begin{corollary}[2.4]
With the hypotheses and notation of \textup{2.3}, there exists in the derived category a morphism
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.4.1}
Rf_* P^s\widetilde j_*\Omega_U^* \to P^s j_*\Omega_U^*
\tag{2.4.1}
\end{equation}
inducing the identity on $U$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The complex $P^s\widetilde j_*\Omega_U^*$ has components
\[
\Omega_{\widetilde X}^p((p-s+1)V_1) \qquad (p\ge s),
\]
and, by \eqref{eq:2.3.1}, these are subsheaves of
\[
\Omega_{\widetilde X}^p(\log \widetilde Y)(-\widetilde Y)((p-s+1)\widetilde V)
= \Omega_{\widetilde X}^p(\log \widetilde Y)(-\widetilde Y)\otimes f^*\mathscr O((p-s+1)V).
\]
By 2.1 the latter sheaves are acyclic: $R^i f_*=0$ for $i>0$. Hence the derived direct image of the complex formed by them is obtained simply by applying $f_*$ componentwise; this yields the complex of sheaves $\Ker(\Omega_X^p\to \Omega_Y^p)((p-s+1)V)$.

Applying $Rf_*$ to the inclusion
\[
P^s\widetilde j_*\Omega_U^* \to \sigma_{\ge s}(\Omega_{\widetilde X}^*(\log \widetilde Y)(-\widetilde Y)((*-s+1)\widetilde V)),
\]
one obtains a morphism
\[
Rf_*P^s\widetilde j_*\Omega_U^* \to \sigma_{\ge s}\Ker(\Omega_X^*\to \Omega_Y^p)((*-s+1)V).
\]
Composing with the inclusion of $\Ker(\Omega_X^*\to \Omega_Y^*)$ into $\Omega_X^*$ gives the promised morphism
\[
Rf_*P^s\widetilde j_*\Omega_U^* \to P^s j_*\Omega_U^*.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[2.5]
With the hypotheses and notation of \textup{2.3}, the image of $R^*\Gamma(\widetilde X,P^s\widetilde j_*\Omega_U^*)$ in $\mathrm H^*(U)$ contains the image of $R^*\Gamma(X,P^s j_*\Omega_U^*)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For any complex of sheaves $K$ on $\widetilde X$, one has $R\Gamma(X,Rf_*K)=R\Gamma(\widetilde X,K)$. Applying $R\Gamma(X,\cdot)$ to 2.4 therefore yields a commutative diagram
\[
\begin{array}{c}
R^*\Gamma(\widetilde X,P^s\widetilde j_*\Omega_U^*) \longrightarrow R^*\Gamma(X,P^s j_*\Omega_U^*)\\[6pt]
\searrow \qquad \swarrow\\[6pt]
\mathrm H^*(U)=R^*\Gamma(U,\Omega_U^*).
\end{array}
\]
\end{proof}

\noindent\textbf{2.6.} By Hironaka~[10], starting from $(X,V)$ one can construct a sequence of blow-ups $(X_i,V_i)$ $(0\le i\le r)$, with $(X_0,V_0)=(X,V)$, such that $(X_{i+1},V_{i+1})$ is obtained from $(X_i,V_i)$ in the same way as $(\widetilde X,V_1)$ is obtained from $(X,V)$ in 2.3, and in such a way that $(V_r)_{\mathrm{red}}$ is a normal-crossings divisor in $X_r$. Let $j_i$ be the inclusion of $U=X-V$ into $X_i$, and let $P$ denote the pole-order filtration on $j_{i*}\Omega_U^*$ relative to the divisor $V_i$. Iterating 2.5, one finds that the image of $R^*\Gamma(X,P^s j_*\Omega_U^*)$ in $\mathrm H^*(U)$ contains the image of $R^*\Gamma(X_r,P^s j_{r*}\Omega_U^*)$. In $X_r$, the open set $U$ is the complement of the normal-crossings divisor $(V_r)_{\mathrm{red}}$. By definition, $F^s\mathrm H^*(U)$ is therefore the hypercohomology on $X_r$ of the complex $\sigma_{\ge s}\Omega_{X_r}^*(\log (V_r)_{\mathrm{red}})$.

One has
\[
\sigma_{\ge s}\Omega_{X_r}^*(\log (V_r)_{\mathrm{red}}) \subset P^s j_{r*}\Omega_U^*,
\]
so $F^s\mathrm H^*(U)$ is contained in the image of the hypercohomology of $P^s j_{r*}\Omega_U^*$ on $X_r$, and Theorem~2 follows.

\begin{remark}[2.7]
Just as 2.5 was deduced from 2.4 on $X$, one can refine Theorem~2 by a result on $X$: for $X_r$, $V_r$ as in 2.6, there exists a morphism
\[
Rf_*F^s\Omega_{X_r}^*(\log (V_r)_{\mathrm{red}}) \to P^s j_*\Omega_U^*
\]
that is the identity on $U$. More precisely, in the filtered derived category of $X$ there is a morphism
\[
Rf_*(\Omega_{X_r}^*(\log (V_r)_{\mathrm{red}}),F) \to (j_*\Omega_U^*,P)
\]
inducing the identity on $U$. The proof is the same, using 2.4 instead of 2.5.

A variant of results of Ph.~Du~Bois~[7] also says that $Rf_*(\Omega_{X_r}^*(\log (V_r)_{\mathrm{red}}),F)$ is independent of the choice of $(X_r,V_r)$ dominating $(X,V)$, with $X_r$ proper over $X$, $X_r-V_r\xrightarrow{\sim} X-V$, and such that $V_r=f^{-1}(Y)$ is a normal-crossings divisor in $X_r$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[2.8]
Our proof used the fact that in Hironaka's embedded resolution theorem one may choose the successive blow-ups with centers contained in the singular locus of $f^{-1}(V)$. This fact is not stated explicitly in~[10], but it follows from the proof.
\end{remark}

\section*{3. Applications and variants}

\noindent\textbf{3.1.} Let $X$ be smooth proper of pure dimension $n$, let $D$ be a divisor, put $\mathscr L:=\mathscr O(D)$, and set $U:=X-D$. The arguments in Section~1 also show that if $\mathrm H^*(X,\Omega^{n-i}\otimes \mathscr L^{\otimes(\mu-i)})=0$ for $0\le i<\mu$, then $F^{n-\mu+1}\mathrm H^*(U)=0$. By Serre duality, the hypothesis may be reformulated as $\mathrm H^*(X,\Omega^i\otimes \mathscr L^{\otimes-(\mu-i)})=0$ for $0\le i<\mu$. By Poincar\'e duality, the conclusion is equivalent to saying that $\mathrm H_c^*(U)$ has Hodge filtration $\ge \mu$.

If one is interested in a single $\mathrm H^m(U)$, it suffices to consider the groups $\mathrm H^q(X,\Omega^{n-i}\otimes \mathscr L^{\otimes(\mu-i)})$ with $q+n-i=m$. In particular, if $U$ is affine, so that $\mathrm H^m(U)=0$ for $m>n$, it suffices to consider the groups $\mathrm H^q(X,\Omega^{n-i}\otimes \mathscr L^{\otimes(\mu-i)})$ with $q+n-i\le n$, i.e. $0\le q\le i<\mu$. For $\mu=1$ this gives:

\begin{proposition}[3.2]
With the above notation, if $U$ is affine and if $\Omega^n(D)$ has no nonzero global section, then $F^n\mathrm H^*(U)=0$.
\end{proposition}

Here is a more direct proof of 3.2. Consider again $f:\widetilde X\to X$ proper with $f^{-1}(U)\xrightarrow{\sim}U$, where $\widetilde X$ is smooth and $\widetilde D:=f^{-1}(D)$ is a normal-crossings divisor. By definition,
\[
F^n\mathrm H^i(U)=\mathrm H^{i-n}(\widetilde X, \Omega_{\widetilde X}^n(\log \widetilde D)).
\]
This group vanishes for $i<n$, and also for $i>n$ because $U$ is affine. For $i=n$, it is the space of $n$-forms on $U$ with at most a simple pole along $\widetilde D$. Since $f$ is an isomorphism above a subset of $X$ of codimension at least $2$, such a form $\alpha$ has at most a simple pole along $D$ at the generic points of $D$, hence everywhere. It must therefore be zero, which proves 3.2.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{3.3.} To use 3.1, one needs vanishing theorems for the cohomology of the twisted sheaves $\Omega_X^p$. In the case of compact Hermitian symmetric spaces, the results of Bott~[3] and Kostant~[12] reduce the required vanishing to a combinatorial problem (cf.~D.~M.~Snow~[14,15]).

The irreducible compact Hermitian symmetric spaces are necessarily of the form $X:=G/P$ with $G$ simple and $P$ a proper maximal parabolic. Their Picard group is $\mathbf Z$; we denote by $\mathscr O(1)$ its ample generator. Let $n=\dim X$. The invertible sheaf $\Omega_X^n$ is antiample, of the form $\mathscr O(-k)$; the integer $k$ is computed in A.~Borel and F.~Hirzebruch~[2, \S16]. Their table shows that $k$ is the dual Coxeter number of $G$, and in particular depends only on the type of $G$:
\[
\begin{array}{ccccccc}
\mathrm A_l & \mathrm B_l & \mathrm C_l & \mathrm D_l & \mathrm E_6 & \mathrm E_7\\
l+1 & 2l-1 & l+1 & 2l-2 & 12 & 18.
\end{array}
\]

\noindent\textbf{3.4.} The case where $X$ is the Grassmannian $G(r,s)$ of $r$-dimensional subspaces in a vector space of dimension $r+s$, with $r,s>0$, is the case where $G=\mathrm{SL}(r+s)$, hence of type $\mathrm A_l$ with $l=r+s-1$, and where $P$ is the parabolic stabilizing an $r$-dimensional subspace. Its dimension is $n=rs$.

For every subset $R\subset [1,r+s]\subset \mathbf Z$ of cardinality $r$, let $\bar R$ denote its complement and define
\[
l(R):=\#\{(i,j)\mid i\in R,\ j\in \bar R,\ j<i\}.
\]
The symmetric group on $[1,r+s]$ is the Weyl group of type $\mathrm A_l$, and $l(R)$ is the length of the permutation $\sigma$ of $[1,r+s]$ that is increasing on $[1,r]$ and on $[r+1,r+s]$, with $\sigma([1,r])=R$ and $\sigma([r+1,r+s])=\bar R$.

Proceeding as in D.~M.~Snow~[14], one deduces from Bott~[3] and Kostant~[12] that the following condition is necessary and sufficient for the vanishing of $\mathrm H^*(X,\Omega^p(-a))$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{3.4.1.} For every subset $R$ of cardinality $r$ in $[1,r+s]$, with complement $\bar R$ and with $l(R)=p$, one has
\[
(R+a)\cap \bar R \neq \varnothing.
\]

\begin{proposition}[3.5]
With the above notation:

\emph{(i)} $\mathrm H^*(G(r,s),\Omega^p(-a))=0$ for $0<a<l-p$.

\emph{(ii)} The same conclusion holds for $0<a\le l-p$, except in the following cases: $\mathrm H^*(G(3,3),\Omega^3(-2))$, $\mathrm H^*(G(2,r),\Omega^{r/2}(1+r/2))$ for $r$ even, and similarly for $G(r,2)\cong G(2,r)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We use criterion 3.4.1. Let $R,\bar R$ be as in 3.4 with $l(R)=p$. Put $x=\inf(\bar R)-1$ and $y=\sup(R)$. One has $[1,x]\subset R$, $x+1\in \bar R$, hence $(R+a)\cap\bar R\neq\varnothing$ if $0<a\le x$; likewise $[y+1,r+s]\subset \bar R$, $y\in R$, hence $(R+a)\cap\bar R\neq\varnothing$ if $0<a\le r+s-y$.

Assume therefore that $a>x$ and $a>r+s-y$. Then
\begin{align*}
\inf((R+a)\cup \bar R) &= \inf \bar R = x+1,\\
\sup((R+a)\cup \bar R) &= \sup(R+a) = y+a,
\end{align*}
and $(R+a)\cup\bar R\subset [x+1,y+a]$ has at most $y-x+a$ elements.

The pairs $(i,j)$ with $j=x+1$, $i\in R-[1,x]$, together with the pairs $(i,j)$ with $i=y$, $j\in \bar R-[y+1,r+s]$, satisfy $i\in R$, $j\in\bar R$, and $j<i$. The pair $(y,x)$ is counted twice. Hence
\[
l(R)\ge (r-x)+(s-(r+s-y))-1 = y-x-1.
\]
Equality holds if and only if $R$ has the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:3.5.1}
R=[1,x]\cup [x+2,r]\cup\{y\},\qquad R\neq [1,r].
\tag{3.5.1}
\end{equation}

If condition \emph{(i)} holds, then
\[
0<a<(r+s-1)-(y-x-1)=(r+s)-(y-x)
\]
and therefore
\[
\#((R+a)\cup \bar R) < (y-x)+[(r+s)-(y-x)] = r+s,
\]
so $(R+a)\cap\bar R\neq\varnothing$, as required.

To prove \emph{(ii)}, if one of the inequalities $a\le l-p$ or $l(R)\ge y-x-1$ is strict, the preceding argument still applies. Thus we may assume, and do assume, that $a=(r+s)-(y-x)$ and $l(R)=y-x-1$. In particular, $R$ has the form \eqref{eq:3.5.1}. If $x=0$, then $a\le r+s-y$. If $y=r+s$, then $a\le x$. These cases have already been treated, so we may assume that $x\neq 0$ and $y\neq r+s$. Then $1\le x\le r-1$ and $r+1\le y\le r+s-1$.

Assume $(R+a)\cap\bar R=\varnothing$ and show that this leads precisely to the exceptional cases in \emph{(ii)}. The translation $z\mapsto z+a$ sends $y$ to $(r+s)+x$, and if $i\in [1,r+s]$, then either $i+a\in [1,r+s]$ or else $i+a-(r+s)\in [1,x]\subset R$. Let $qR$ and $q\bar R$ denote the images of $R$ and $\bar R$ in $\mathbf Z/(r+s)$. Since $(R+a)\cap\bar R=\varnothing$, one has $qR+a\subset q\bar R$, hence $qR+a=q\bar R$ and $q\bar R+a=qR$.

Call the connected components of $R$ (resp. of $qR$) the largest intervals (resp. images of intervals) contained in $R$ (resp. in $qR$). The connected components of $qR$ are the images of those of $R$. By \eqref{eq:3.5.1}, $R$ has two or three connected components. Translation by $a$ permutes the connected components of $qR$, sending the one containing $y$ to the next one, namely the one containing $x$. The complement $q\bar R$ has the same number of connected components, permuted in the same way.

If $R$ has three connected components, then the component containing $y$ is reduced to $y$ and every component has one element. The same holds for the components of $\bar R$: $x$ is a component by itself and every component of $\bar R$ has one element. Hence $r=s=3$, $R=\{1,3,5\}$, and $a=2$. This is the first exceptional case. One has $l(R)=3$.

Assume now that $R$ has two connected components. There are two cases: either $x=r-1$ or $y=r+1$. If $x=r-1$, the component containing $y$ is reduced to $y$ and every component has one element. Thus $R=\{1,y\}$ and translation by $a$ permutes $1$ and $y$ in $\mathbf Z/(r+s)$: one gets $r=2$, $s$ even,
\[
R=\Bigl\{1, 2+\frac{s}{2}\Bigr\}.
\]
If $y=r+1$, the component of $x$ in $\bar R$ is reduced to $x$; likewise one gets $r$ even, $s=2$, and
\[
R=\Bigl[1,\frac r2\Bigr]\cup \Bigl[\frac r2+2, r+1\Bigr].
\]
This is the second exceptional case.
\end{proof}

By 3.1 this proposition has the following corollary.

\begin{corollary}[3.6]
Let $D\subset G(r,s)$ be the divisor defined by the vanishing of a section of $\mathscr O(d)$, and let $U$ be the complementary open subset. Put $\mu=[(r+s-1)/d]$. Except in the exceptional cases below, $\mathrm H_c^*(U)$ has Hodge filtration $\ge \mu$. The exceptions are $G(3,3)$ and $G(2,r)\cong G(r,2)$ with $d=1$, where the Hodge filtration is only $\ge \mu-1$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[3.7]
In the special case $X=\mathbf P^n$, Theorem~2 shows that a cohomology class in $\mathrm H^*(U,\mathbf C)$ can be represented by a holomorphic differential form on $U$ with controlled pole order along $D$. Dimca uses this fact in~[5,6] to relate the Betti numbers and Alexander polynomial of a hypersurface with isolated singularities to the defect of associated linear systems.
\end{remark}
