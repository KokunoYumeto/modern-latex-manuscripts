In this article I state a conjecture (1.8, 2.8) relating the values of certain L-functions at certain integral points to periods of integrals.\par
The L-functions considered are those of motives—a word to which no precise meaning will be attached. These include, in particular, Artin L-functions, L-functions attached to algebraic Hecke characters (\ensuremath{=} Grössencharakter of type A\ensuremath{_{0}}), and those attached to primitive holomorphic modular forms on the Poincaré upper half-plane (\ensuremath{=} new forms; all the functions L, L\ensuremath{_{k}}, attached to the symmetric powers Sym\ensuremath{^{k}} of the corresponding l-adic representation are considered).\par
This article owes its existence to D. Zagier: to his insistence that a conjecture be sought, and to the experimental confirmation he supplied, as soon as it was formulated, for the functions L\ensuremath{_{3}} and L\ensuremath{_{4}} attached to \ensuremath{Δ} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{Σ} \ensuremath{τ}(n)q\ensuremath{^{n}} (see [18]). It was this confirmation that gave me the confidence needed to verify that the conjecture was compatible with Shimura’s results [13] on values of L-functions of algebraic Hecke characters.\par
\SectionLead{0. Motives.} The reader is invited not to consult this section except as needs arise. We recall part of the formalism of motives, due to Grothendieck. For proofs, I refer to [8].\par
0.1. Grothendieck’s definition of motives over a field k has the following form. (a) Let \ensuremath{𝒱}(k) be the category of smooth projective varieties over k. One constructs an additive category \ensuremath{ℳ}\ensuremath{′}(k), for which the groups Hom(M, N) are vector spaces over \ensuremath{ℚ}, equipped with\par
\ensuremath{α}. A tensor product \ensuremath{⊗}, associative, commutative, and distributive with respect to addition of objects (“associative” and “commutative” are not properties of the functor \ensuremath{⊗}, but data subject to certain compatibilities—cf. Saavedra [19]);\par
\ensuremath{β}. A contravariant functor H* from \ensuremath{𝒱}(k) to \ensuremath{ℳ}\ensuremath{′}(k), bijective on objects and transforming disjoint sums into sums and products into tensor products (the datum of an isomorphism of functors H*(X \ensuremath{×} Y) \ensuremath{=} H*(X) \ensuremath{⊗} H*(Y), compatible with associativity and commutativity).\par
The essential point is to define Hom(H*(X), H*(Y)). For the definition, used—\par
\clearpage
\PageUnit{2}{314}{FRENCH MAIN BODY}
by Grothendieck, of this group as a group of classes of correspondences between X and Y, see 0.6.\par
(b) Recall (SGA 4 IV 7.5) that an additive category is Karoubian if every projector (\ensuremath{=} idempotent endomorphism) is defined by a direct-sum decomposition, and that every additive category has a Karoubian envelope, obtained by formally adjoining the images of projectors. The category \ensuremath{ℳ}\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{eff}}}(k) of effective motives over k is the Karoubian envelope of \ensuremath{ℳ}\ensuremath{′}(k).\par
(c) The Tate motive \ensuremath{ℤ}(\ensuremath{−}1) is defined as a suitable direct factor H\ensuremath{^{2}}(\ensuremath{ℙ}\ensuremath{^{1}}) of H*(\ensuremath{ℙ}\ensuremath{^{1}}). One checks that the symmetry (deduced from the commutativity of \ensuremath{⊗}), \ensuremath{ℤ}(\ensuremath{−}1) \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℤ}(\ensuremath{−}1) \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{ℤ}(\ensuremath{−}1) \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℤ}(\ensuremath{−}1), is the identity, and that the functor M \ensuremath{→} M \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℤ}(\ensuremath{−}1) is fully faithful.\par
(d) The category \ensuremath{ℳ}(k) of motives over k is obtained from \ensuremath{ℳ}\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{eff}}}(k) by making the functor M \ensuremath{→} M \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℤ}(\ensuremath{−}1) invertible.\par
Let (n) denote the (\ensuremath{−}n)th iterate of the auto-equivalence M \ensuremath{→} M \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℤ}(\ensuremath{−}1). The category \ensuremath{ℳ}(k) contains \ensuremath{ℳ}\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{eff}}}(k) as a full subcategory, and every object of \ensuremath{ℳ}(k) is of the form M(n), with M in \ensuremath{ℳ}\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{eff}}}(k) and n an integer.\par
By definition, if F is an additive functor from \ensuremath{ℳ}\ensuremath{′}(k) to a Karoubian category \ensuremath{𝒜}, it extends to \ensuremath{ℳ}\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{eff}}}(k). If \ensuremath{𝒜} is endowed with an auto-equivalence A \ensuremath{→} A(\ensuremath{−}1), and the extension of F with an isomorphism of functors F(M(\ensuremath{−}1)) \ensuremath{=} F(M)(\ensuremath{−}1), it extends to \ensuremath{ℳ}(k).\par
EXAMPLE 0.1.1. If k\ensuremath{′} is an extension of k, and F is the functor H*(X) \ensuremath{→} H*(X \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{k}} k\ensuremath{′}) from \ensuremath{ℳ}\ensuremath{′}(k) to \ensuremath{ℳ}\ensuremath{′}(k\ensuremath{′}), one obtains the scalar-extension functor from \ensuremath{ℳ}(k) to \ensuremath{ℳ}(k\ensuremath{′}). If k\ensuremath{′} is a finite extension of k, one has Grothendieck’s restriction-of-scalars functor\par
\ensuremath{Π}\ensuremath{_{k′/k}}: \ensuremath{𝒱}(k\ensuremath{′}) \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{𝒱}(k): (X \ensuremath{→} Spec(k\ensuremath{′})) \ensuremath{↦} (X \ensuremath{→} Spec(k\ensuremath{′}) \ensuremath{→} Spec(k)).\par
It extends by F: H*(X) \ensuremath{↦} H*(\ensuremath{Π}\ensuremath{_{k′/k}}X), yielding a restriction-of-scalars functor R\ensuremath{_{k′/k}}: \ensuremath{ℳ}(k\ensuremath{′}) \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{ℳ}(k).\par
EXAMPLE 0.1.2. Let \ensuremath{ℋ} be a “cohomology theory” with values in a Karoubian category \ensuremath{𝒜}, functorial for morphisms in \ensuremath{ℳ}\ensuremath{′}(k). The functor \ensuremath{ℋ} extends to \ensuremath{ℳ}\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{eff}}}(k). If \ensuremath{𝒜} has a tensor product, \ensuremath{ℋ} satisfies a Künneth formula, and tensoring with \ensuremath{ℋ}(\ensuremath{ℤ}(\ensuremath{−}1)) is an auto-equivalence of \ensuremath{𝒜}, then it extends to \ensuremath{ℳ}(k). This extension is the functor “realization of a motive in the theory \ensuremath{ℋ}.”\par
We shall write (n) for the auto-equivalence of \ensuremath{𝒜} obtained as the (\ensuremath{−}n)th iterate of tensoring with \ensuremath{ℋ}(\ensuremath{ℤ}(\ensuremath{−}1)). For the determination of \ensuremath{ℋ}(\ensuremath{ℤ}(\ensuremath{−}1)) in various theories \ensuremath{ℋ}, see 3.1.\par
0.2. We shall use the following realizations: 0.2.1. Betti realization H\ensuremath{_{B}}. Corresponding to k \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ℂ}, \ensuremath{𝒜} \ensuremath{=} vector spaces over \ensuremath{ℚ}, \ensuremath{ℋ} \ensuremath{=} rational cohomology: X \ensuremath{↦} H*(X(\ensuremath{ℂ}), \ensuremath{ℚ}); 0.2.2. de Rham realization H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}. Corresponding to k of characteristic 0, \ensuremath{𝒜} \ensuremath{=} vector spaces over k, \ensuremath{ℋ} \ensuremath{=} de Rham cohomology: X \ensuremath{↦} H*(X, \ensuremath{Ω}\ensuremath{_{X}}*); 0.2.3. l-adic realization H\ensuremath{_{l}}. Corresponding to algebraically closed k of characteristic \ensuremath{≠} l, \ensuremath{𝒜} \ensuremath{=} vector spaces over \ensuremath{ℚ}\ensuremath{_{l}}, \ensuremath{ℋ} \ensuremath{=} l-adic cohomology: X \ensuremath{↦} H*(X, \ensuremath{ℚ}\ensuremath{_{l}}).\par
And their variants: 0.2.4. Hodge realization. Here k is an algebraic closure of \ensuremath{ℝ}, and \ensuremath{𝒜} is the category of vector spaces over \ensuremath{ℚ} whose scalar extension V \ensuremath{⊗} k is endowed with a bigrading\par
\clearpage
\PageUnit{3}{315}{FRENCH MAIN BODY}
V \ensuremath{⊗} k \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{⊕}\ensuremath{_{p,q}} V\ensuremath{^{p,q}}, with V\ensuremath{^{q,p}} the complex conjugate of V\ensuremath{^{p,q}}. For the cohomology theory, one takes the functor X \ensuremath{↦} H*(X(k), \ensuremath{ℚ}), endowed with the bigrading of its scalar extension H*(X(k), k) supplied by Hodge theory. The Betti realization underlies the Hodge realization.\par
0.2.5. For arbitrary k and a complex embedding \ensuremath{σ} of k, write H\ensuremath{_{σ}}(M) for the Betti realization of the motive over \ensuremath{ℂ} obtained from M by scalar extension through \ensuremath{σ}: k \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{ℂ}. Let c denote complex conjugation. Transport of structure gives an isomorphism F\ensuremath{_{∞}}: H\ensuremath{_{σ}}(M) \ensuremath{≅} H\ensuremath{_{cσ}}(M), and F\ensuremath{_{∞}} \ensuremath{⊗} c sends H\ensuremath{_{σ}}\ensuremath{^{p,q}} to H\ensuremath{_{cσ}}\ensuremath{^{q,p}}. If \ensuremath{σ} is real, F\ensuremath{_{∞}} is an involution of H\ensuremath{_{σ}}(M), whose complexification interchanges H\ensuremath{_{σ}}\ensuremath{^{p,q}}(M) and H\ensuremath{_{σ}}\ensuremath{^{q,p}}(M).\par
The \ensuremath{σ}-Hodge realization is H\ensuremath{_{σ}}(M), endowed with its Hodge bigrading and, when \ensuremath{σ} is real, the involution F\ensuremath{_{∞}}. For k \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ℚ}, or k \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ℝ} with \ensuremath{σ} the identity embedding, the index \ensuremath{σ} is replaced by B. For k \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ℝ} and M \ensuremath{=} H*(X), the involution F\ensuremath{_{∞}} on H\ensuremath{_{B}}*(M) \ensuremath{=} H*(X(\ensuremath{ℂ}), \ensuremath{ℚ}) is induced by complex conjugation F\ensuremath{_{∞}}: X(\ensuremath{ℂ}) \ensuremath{→} X(\ensuremath{ℂ}).\par
0.2.6. de Rham realization. de Rham cohomology carries a natural filtration, the Hodge filtration, arising as the abutment of the hypercohomology spectral sequence\par
E\ensuremath{_{1}}\ensuremath{^{p,q}} \ensuremath{=} H\ensuremath{^{q}}(X, \ensuremath{Ω}\ensuremath{^{p}}) \ensuremath{⇒} H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}\ensuremath{^{p+q}}(X).\par
This gives a filtration F on H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M).\par
0.2.7. l-adic realization. If X is a variety over a field k, with algebraic closure \ensuremath{\bar{k}}, the Galois group Gal(\ensuremath{\bar{k}}/k) acts by transport of structure on H*(X\ensuremath{_{\bar{k}}}, \ensuremath{ℚ}\ensuremath{_{l}}). If M is a motive over k and H\ensuremath{_{l}}(M) is defined as the l-adic realization of the motive over \ensuremath{\bar{k}} obtained from it by scalar extension, this gives an action of Gal(\ensuremath{\bar{k}}/k) on H\ensuremath{_{l}}(M).\par
0.2.8. Finite adelic realization. For algebraically closed k of characteristic 0, the l-adic cohomologies can be assembled into a finite adelic cohomology\par
H*(X, \ensuremath{𝔸}\ensuremath{^{f}}) \ensuremath{=} (\ensuremath{∏}\ensuremath{_{l}} H*(X, \ensuremath{ℤ}\ensuremath{_{l}})) \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{ℤ}} \ensuremath{ℚ}.\par
The variants 0.2.7 and 0.2.8 may be combined.\par
0.2.9. In Examples 0.2.1, 0.2.2, 0.2.3 and their variants, the category \ensuremath{𝒜} may be replaced by the category \ensuremath{𝒜}* of graded objects of \ensuremath{𝒜}, using the natural grading of H*. In order that the Künneth formula yield an isomorphism of functors \ensuremath{ℋ}(M \ensuremath{⊗} N) \ensuremath{≅} \ensuremath{ℋ}(M) \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℋ}(N), compatible with associativity and commutativity, the commutativity datum in \ensuremath{𝒜}* must be the one given by the Koszul rule.\par
0.3. For k \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ℂ}, the comparison theorem between classical and étale cohomology gives an isomorphism H*(X(\ensuremath{ℂ}), \ensuremath{ℚ}) \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℚ}\ensuremath{_{l}} \ensuremath{≅} H*(X, \ensuremath{ℚ}\ensuremath{_{l}}). If X is defined over \ensuremath{ℝ}, this isomorphism carries F\ensuremath{_{∞}} (0.2.5) to the action (0.2.7) of complex conjugation. For a motive one likewise has H\ensuremath{_{B}}(M) \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℚ}\ensuremath{_{l}} \ensuremath{≅} H\ensuremath{_{l}}(M).\par
0.4. For k \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ℂ}, the holomorphic de Rham complex on X\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{an}}} is a resolution of the constant sheaf \ensuremath{ℂ}. By GAGA one therefore has an isomorphism\par
H*(X(\ensuremath{ℂ}), \ensuremath{ℚ}) \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ} \ensuremath{=} H*(X(\ensuremath{ℂ}), \ensuremath{ℂ}) \ensuremath{≅} \ensuremath{ℍ}*(X\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{an}}}, \ensuremath{Ω}\ensuremath{^{*an}}) \ensuremath{≅} H*(X, \ensuremath{Ω}*).\par
For a motive one obtains an isomorphism, compatible with the Hodge filtrations in 0.2.4—F\ensuremath{^{p}} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{⊕}\ensuremath{_{p′≥p}} V\ensuremath{^{p′,q′}}—and 0.2.6: H\ensuremath{_{B}}(M) \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ} \ensuremath{≅} H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M).\par

More generally, let M be a motive over a field k and \ensuremath{σ} a complex embedding of k. Applying the preceding construction to the motive over \ensuremath{ℂ} obtained from M by scalar extension gives an isomorphism\par
(0.4.1)    I: H\ensuremath{_{σ}}(M) \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ} \ensuremath{≅} H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M) \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{k,σ}} \ensuremath{ℂ}.\par
In the particular case k \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ℚ}, this gives two natural \ensuremath{ℚ}-structures on the realization of M in complex cohomology: one, H\ensuremath{_{B}}(M), linked to the description of cohomology in terms of cycles; the other, H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M), linked to its description in terms of algebraic differential forms.\par
0.5. The behavior of these realizations and compatibilities under extension of the scalar field is clear. For restriction of scalars, the results are as follows. Let k\ensuremath{′} be a finite extension of k, let M\ensuremath{′} be a motive over k\ensuremath{′}, and let M \ensuremath{=} R\ensuremath{_{k′/k}}(M\ensuremath{′}).\par
(0.5.1)    H\ensuremath{_{σ}}(M) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{⊕}\ensuremath{_{τ}} H\ensuremath{_{τ}}(M\ensuremath{′}),\par
where the sum extends over the set J(\ensuremath{σ}) of complex embeddings of k\ensuremath{′} extending \ensuremath{σ}. This isomorphism is compatible with the Hodge bigradings and with F\ensuremath{_{∞}}.\par
(0.5.2)    H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M) \ensuremath{=} H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M\ensuremath{′})    (restriction of scalars from k\ensuremath{′} to k).\par
This isomorphism is compatible with the Hodge filtration.\par
(0.5.3)    H\ensuremath{_{l}}(M) \ensuremath{=} Ind H\ensuremath{_{l}}(M\ensuremath{′})    (the representation induced from Gal(\ensuremath{\bar{k}}/k\ensuremath{′}) to Gal(\ensuremath{\bar{k}}/k)).\par
(0.5.4) Via the isomorphism k\ensuremath{′} \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{k,σ}} \ensuremath{ℂ} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ℂ}\ensuremath{^{J(σ)}}, and the resulting isomorphism H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M\ensuremath{′}) \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{k,σ}} \ensuremath{ℂ} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{⊕}\ensuremath{_{τ∈J(σ)}} H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M\ensuremath{′}) \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{k′,τ}} \ensuremath{ℂ}, (0.4.1) for M and \ensuremath{σ} is the sum of the isomorphisms (0.4.1) for M\ensuremath{′} and \ensuremath{τ} (\ensuremath{τ} \ensuremath{∈} J(\ensuremath{σ})):\par

0.6. For X smooth and projective over k, let Z\ensuremath{^{d}}(X) be the \ensuremath{ℚ}-vector space with basis the irreducible closed subschemes of codimension d in X, and let Z\ensuremath{_{R}}\ensuremath{^{d}}(X) be its quotient by an equivalence relation R. In characteristic 0, one of Grothendieck’s definitions of motives is obtained by setting, for connected X and Y,\par
Hom(H*(Y), H*(X)) \ensuremath{=} Z\ensuremath{_{R}}\ensuremath{^{dim(Y)}}(X \ensuremath{×} Y),\par
with R equal to homological equivalence. In characteristic p there are two difficulties to note: it is not known whether homological equivalence in l-adic cohomology (l \ensuremath{≠} p) is independent of l, and defining the class of a cycle raises problems in crystalline cohomology.\par
0.7. Let X be a smooth projective complex variety. A Hodge cycle of codimension d on X will mean an element of H\ensuremath{^{2d}}(X(\ensuremath{ℂ}), \ensuremath{ℚ}) of type (d, d), or equivalently an element of H\ensuremath{^{2d}}(X(\ensuremath{ℂ}), \ensuremath{ℚ})(d) of type (0, 0).\par
Let k be an algebraically closed field and X a smooth projective variety over k. Set\par
\clearpage
\PageUnit{5}{317}{FRENCH MAIN BODY}
H\ensuremath{^{2d}}(X, k \ensuremath{×} \ensuremath{𝔸}\ensuremath{^{f}})(d) \ensuremath{=} H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}\ensuremath{^{2d}}(X)(d) \ensuremath{×} H\ensuremath{^{2d}}(X, \ensuremath{𝔸}\ensuremath{^{f}})(d).\par
For k \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ℂ}, the image in\par
H\ensuremath{^{2d}}(X, k \ensuremath{×} \ensuremath{𝔸}\ensuremath{^{f}})(d) \ensuremath{=} H\ensuremath{^{2d}}(X(\ensuremath{ℂ}), \ensuremath{ℚ})(d) \ensuremath{⊗} (k \ensuremath{×} \ensuremath{𝔸}\ensuremath{^{f}})\par
of a Hodge cycle is again called a Hodge cycle. For algebraically closed k admitting complex embeddings, an absolute Hodge cycle of codimension d on X is an element of H\ensuremath{^{2d}}(X, k \ensuremath{×} \ensuremath{𝔸}\ensuremath{^{f}})(d) whose image in H\ensuremath{^{2d}}(X \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{k}} \ensuremath{ℂ}, \ensuremath{ℂ} \ensuremath{×} \ensuremath{𝔸}\ensuremath{^{f}})(d) is a Hodge cycle for every complex embedding \ensuremath{σ} of k. One verifies the following.\par
PROPOSITION 0.8. (i) The \ensuremath{ℚ}-vector space Z\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{ha}}}\ensuremath{^{d}}(X) of absolute Hodge cycles is invariant under extension of scalars from k to an algebraically closed field k\ensuremath{′} that still admits a complex embedding.\par
(ii) Suppose k is algebraically closed of characteristic 0 and X is defined over an algebraically closed subfield k\ensuremath{_{0}} of k admitting a complex embedding, with X \ensuremath{=} X\ensuremath{_{0}} \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{k₀}} k. Set\par
Z\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{ha}}}\ensuremath{^{d}}(X) \ensuremath{=} Z\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{ha}}}\ensuremath{^{d}}(X\ensuremath{_{0}}) \ensuremath{⊂} H\ensuremath{^{2d}}(X\ensuremath{_{0}}, k\ensuremath{_{0}} \ensuremath{×} \ensuremath{𝔸}\ensuremath{^{f}})(d) \ensuremath{⊂} H\ensuremath{^{2d}}(X, k \ensuremath{×} \ensuremath{𝔸}\ensuremath{^{f}})(d).\par
By (i), this definition is independent of the choices of X\ensuremath{_{0}} and k\ensuremath{_{0}}.\par
(iii) If X is defined over a subfield k\ensuremath{_{0}} of k, with X \ensuremath{=} X\ensuremath{_{0}} \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{k₀}} k, then Aut(k/k\ensuremath{_{0}}), acting on H\ensuremath{^{2d}}(X, k \ensuremath{×} \ensuremath{𝔸}\ensuremath{^{f}})(d), stabilizes Z\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{ha}}}\ensuremath{^{d}}(X). Its action on Z\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{ha}}}\ensuremath{^{d}}(X) factors through a finite group corresponding to a finite extension k\ensuremath{′}\ensuremath{_{0}} of k\ensuremath{_{0}}. Set Z\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{ha}}}\ensuremath{^{d}}(X\ensuremath{_{0}}) \ensuremath{=} Z\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{ha}}}\ensuremath{^{d}}(X)\ensuremath{^{Aut(k/k₀)}}.\par
0.9. A useful notion of motive is obtained by setting, for connected X and Y,\par
Hom(H*(Y), H*(X)) \ensuremath{=} Z\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{ha}}}\ensuremath{^{dim(Y)}}(X \ensuremath{×} Y).\par
The Künneth components of the diagonal of X \ensuremath{×} X are absolute Hodge cycles. This makes it possible to decompose H*(X) into a sum of motives H\ensuremath{^{i}}(X), to grade the category of motives—with H\ensuremath{^{i}}(X) of weight i—and to modify the commutativity constraint for \ensuremath{⊗} as in [19, VI, 4.2.1.4]. With these conventions, the tensor category of motives over k is Tannakian and isomorphic to the category of representations of a reductive proalgebraic group.\par
For k \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ℂ}, the following conjecture, weaker than the Hodge conjecture, is equivalent to saying that the Hodge-realization functor is an equivalence between the category of motives in 0.9 and the category of Hodge structures that occur as direct factors of the cohomology of algebraic varieties, or are obtained from such factors by Tate twist.\par
Hope 0.10. Every Hodge cycle is absolute.\par
If X is an abelian variety with complex multiplication by an imaginary quadratic field K, with Lie(X) free over k \ensuremath{⊗} K, the methods of B. Gross [7] prove that certain nontrivial Hodge cycles are absolute Hodge cycles.\par
Added in proof: starting from this special case, I have been able to verify 0.10 for abelian varieties. I have also verified that the tensor category of motives (0.9) generated by H\ensuremath{^{n}}(X), for X an abelian variety, contains the motives H*(X) for X a K3 surface or a Fermat hypersurface.\par
0.11. The principal defect of definition 0.9 of motives is that it does not lend itself to reduction modulo p. It is not known whether a motive in the sense of 0.9 over a number field F gives a compatible system of l-adic representations of Gal(\ensuremath{\bar{F}}/F).\par

0.12. We shall use the word “motive” freely, without concerning ourselves with placing the motives under consideration within Grothendieck’s framework. What matters for us is to have realizations \ensuremath{ℋ}(M), for the theories \ensuremath{ℋ} considered in 0.2, and to have for these groups the same formalism as for the \ensuremath{ℋ}*(X).\par
1. Statement of the conjecture (rational case).\par
1.1. Let M be a motive over \ensuremath{ℚ}. We shall assume that the l-adic realizations H\ensuremath{_{l}}(M) of M form a strictly compatible system of l-adic representations, in the sense of Serre [11, I.11]. Namely, there is a finite set S of prime numbers such that each H\ensuremath{_{l}}(M) is unramified outside S \ensuremath{∪} \{l\}, and, if F\ensuremath{_{p}} \ensuremath{∈} Gal(\ensuremath{\bar{ℚ}}/\ensuremath{ℚ}) denotes a geometric Frobenius element at p—the inverse of a Frobenius substitution \ensuremath{φ}\ensuremath{_{p}}—then the polynomial det(1 \ensuremath{−} F\ensuremath{_{p}} t, H\ensuremath{_{l}}(M)) \ensuremath{∈} \ensuremath{ℚ}\ensuremath{_{l}}[t], for p \ensuremath{∉} S \ensuremath{∪} \{l\}, has rational coefficients and is independent of l. Let Z\ensuremath{_{p}}(M, t) denote its inverse, and set L\ensuremath{_{p}}(M, s) \ensuremath{=} Z\ensuremath{_{p}}(M, p\ensuremath{^{−s}}).\par
The Dirichlet series with rational coefficients given by the Euler product\par
L\ensuremath{_{S}}(M, s) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∏}\ensuremath{_{p∉S}} L\ensuremath{_{p}}(M, s)\par
converges for Re(s) sufficiently large. For arbitrary s, L\ensuremath{_{S}}(M, s) is defined by analytic continuation, which is expected to exist.\par
Our aim is to state a conjecture giving the value of L\ensuremath{_{S}}(M, s) at certain integral points, up to a rational factor. Since p\ensuremath{^{−s}} is rational for integral s, the choice of S is immaterial, except that enlarging S can introduce unwanted zeros.\par
1.2. To write the conjectural functional equation of L-functions properly, the Euler product L\ensuremath{_{S}}(M, s) must be completed by local factors at p \ensuremath{∈} S and at infinity. Defining the local factors at p \ensuremath{∈} S requires an additional hypothesis, which we assume:\par
(1.2.1) Let p be a prime number, D\ensuremath{_{p}} \ensuremath{⊂} Gal(\ensuremath{\bar{ℚ}}/\ensuremath{ℚ}) a decomposition group at p, I\ensuremath{_{p}} \ensuremath{⊂} D\ensuremath{_{p}} its inertia subgroup, and F\ensuremath{_{p}} \ensuremath{∈} D\ensuremath{_{p}} a geometric Frobenius. The polynomial det(1 \ensuremath{−} F\ensuremath{_{p}} t, H\ensuremath{_{l}}(M)\ensuremath{^{I_p}}) \ensuremath{∈} \ensuremath{ℚ}\ensuremath{_{l}}[t], for l \ensuremath{≠} p, has rational coefficients and is independent of l.\par
Set Z\ensuremath{_{p}}(M, t) \ensuremath{=} det(1 \ensuremath{−} F\ensuremath{_{p}} t, H\ensuremath{_{l}}(M)\ensuremath{^{I_p}})\ensuremath{^{−1}} \ensuremath{∈} \ensuremath{ℚ}(t), and L\ensuremath{_{p}}(M, s) \ensuremath{=} Z\ensuremath{_{p}}(M, p\ensuremath{^{−s}}). Define\par
(1.2.2)    L(M, s) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∏}\ensuremath{_{p}} L\ensuremath{_{p}}(M, s).\par
The factor at infinity L\ensuremath{_{∞}}(M, s), essentially a product of \ensuremath{Γ}-functions, depends on the Hodge realization of M—in fact only on the isomorphism class of the complex vector space H\ensuremath{_{B}}(M) \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ}, endowed with its Hodge decomposition and the involution F\ensuremath{_{∞}}. Its definition is recalled in 5.2.\par
Setting \ensuremath{Λ}(M, s) \ensuremath{=} L\ensuremath{_{∞}}(M, s)L(M, s), the conjectural functional equation of L-functions is\par
(1.2.3)    \ensuremath{Λ}(M, s) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ε}(M, s)\ensuremath{Λ}(\ensuremath{\check{M}}, 1 \ensuremath{−} s),\par
where \ensuremath{\check{M}} is the dual of M—its realizations are dual to those of M—and \ensuremath{ε}(M, s), as a function of s, is the product of a constant and an exponential. Its definition is recalled in 5.2. It depends on an additional hypothesis.\par
DEFINITION 1.3. An integer n is critical for M if neither L\ensuremath{_{∞}}(M, s) nor L\ensuremath{_{∞}}(\ensuremath{\check{M}}, 1 \ensuremath{−} s) has a pole at s \ensuremath{=} n.\par
Our aim is to conjecture the value of L(M, n) for critical n, up to multiplication—\par

by a rational number. We have L(M(n), s) \ensuremath{=} L(M, n \ensuremath{+} s) (3.1.2), and likewise for L\ensuremath{_{∞}}. This allows us to consider only the numbers L(M) \ensuremath{=}\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{def}}} L(M, 0). We shall say that M is critical if 0 is critical for M. One verifies that M is critical if and only if the Hodge numbers h\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{pq}}} \ensuremath{=}\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{def}}} dim H\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{pq}}}(M) of M, for p \ensuremath{≠} q, can be nonzero only for (p, q) in the shaded part of the diagram below, and F\ensuremath{_{∞}} acts on H\ensuremath{^{p,p}} as the identity if p \ensuremath{<} 0 and as \ensuremath{−}1 if p \ensuremath{≥} 0.\par

Suppose that M is homogeneous of weight w, so h\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{pq}}} \ensuremath{=} 0 for p \ensuremath{+} q \ensuremath{≠} w, and set \ensuremath{ℜ}(M) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{−}w/2. The Weil conjecture implies that, for S sufficiently large, the Dirichlet series L\ensuremath{_{S}}(M, s) converges absolutely when \ensuremath{ℜ}(M) \ensuremath{+} Re(s) \ensuremath{>} 1. When \ensuremath{ℜ}(M) \ensuremath{+} Re(s) \ensuremath{=} 1, one is on the boundary of the half-plane of convergence, and one conjectures (a) that L\ensuremath{_{S}}(M, s) does not vanish; (b) that L\ensuremath{_{S}}(M, s) is holomorphic, except when M has even weight \ensuremath{−}2n and contains \ensuremath{ℤ}(n) as a factor, in which case one expects a pole at s \ensuremath{=} 1 \ensuremath{−} n, a noncritical value.\par
The analogy with function fields and the known cases suggest that the local factors L\ensuremath{_{p}}(M, s), for arbitrary p including infinity, have poles only when \ensuremath{ℜ}(M) \ensuremath{+} Re(s) \ensuremath{≤} 0. If this is so, (a), (b), and the conjectural functional equation (1.2.3) imply that L(M) is neither zero nor infinite for critical M with \ensuremath{ℜ}(M) \ensuremath{≠} 1/2. When \ensuremath{ℜ}(M) \ensuremath{=} 1/2, L(M) sometimes vanishes, and Conjecture 1.8 is then vacuous.\par
PROPOSITION 1.4. Let M be a motive over \ensuremath{ℝ}. Under the isomorphism (0.4.1)\par
H\ensuremath{_{B}}(M) \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ}  --\ensuremath{≅}--\ensuremath{>}  H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M) \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{ℝ}} \ensuremath{ℂ},\par
H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M) identifies with the subspace of H\ensuremath{_{B}}(M) \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ} fixed by c \ensuremath{↦} F\ensuremath{_{∞}} \ensuremath{\bar{c}}.\par
Take M \ensuremath{=} H*(X). Complex conjugation on H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M)\ensuremath{_{ℂ}} \ensuremath{=} H*(X\ensuremath{_{ℂ}}, \ensuremath{Ω}*) follows functorially from the antilinear automorphism F\ensuremath{_{∞}} of the scheme X\ensuremath{_{ℂ}}. Tracing back through the composite arrow (0.4) defining I, it is identified with the involution induced by the automorphism (F\ensuremath{_{∞}}, F\ensuremath{_{∞}}*) of (X(\ensuremath{ℂ}), \ensuremath{Ω}\ensuremath{^{*an}}), and then with the composite of F\ensuremath{_{∞}}*: H*(X(\ensuremath{ℂ}), \ensuremath{ℂ}) \ensuremath{→} H*(X(\ensuremath{ℂ}), \ensuremath{ℂ}) and complex conjugation on the coefficients. This proves 1.4.\par

1.5. For a motive M over \ensuremath{ℝ}, let H\ensuremath{_{B}}\ensuremath{^{+}}(M), respectively H\ensuremath{_{B}}\ensuremath{^{−}}(M), denote the subspace of H\ensuremath{_{B}}(M) fixed by F\ensuremath{_{∞}}, respectively the subspace on which F\ensuremath{_{∞}} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{−}1. Set d(M) \ensuremath{=} dim H\ensuremath{_{B}}(M) and d\ensuremath{^{±}}(M) \ensuremath{=} dim H\ensuremath{_{B}}\ensuremath{^{±}}(M). If M is a motive over k and \ensuremath{σ} is a complex embedding of k factoring through \ensuremath{ℝ}, write H\ensuremath{_{σ}}\ensuremath{^{±}}(M), d\ensuremath{_{σ}}\ensuremath{^{±}}(M), and d\ensuremath{_{σ}}(M) for these objects attached to the motive over \ensuremath{ℝ} obtained from M through \ensuremath{σ}. For k \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ℚ}, the reference to \ensuremath{σ} is omitted.\par
The following corollary follows immediately from 1.4 and from the fact that both F\ensuremath{_{∞}} and complex conjugation interchange H\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{pq}}} and H\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{qp}}}.\par
COROLLARY 1.6. Let M be a motive over \ensuremath{ℝ}. For the real structure H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M) on H\ensuremath{_{B}}(M) \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ}, the subspaces H\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{pq}}} are defined over \ensuremath{ℝ}; the subspace H\ensuremath{_{B}}\ensuremath{^{+}}(M) \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℝ} is real, and H\ensuremath{_{B}}\ensuremath{^{−}}(M) \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℝ} is purely imaginary.\par
1.7. In the remainder of this section we consider only motives over \ensuremath{ℚ} and, unless explicitly stated otherwise, assume them homogeneous. If their weight w is even, we also assume that F\ensuremath{_{∞}} acts on H\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{pp}}}(M), where w \ensuremath{=} 2p, by a scalar, either \ensuremath{+}1 or \ensuremath{−}1. This hypothesis holds for critical M. Since F\ensuremath{_{∞}} interchanges H\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{pq}}} and H\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{qp}}}, the dimensions d\ensuremath{^{+}}(M) and d\ensuremath{^{−}}(M) are, in some order, \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{p>q}} h\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{pq}}} and \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{p≥q}} h\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{pq}}}. In particular, these dimensions equal those of the subspaces F\ensuremath{^{+}} and F\ensuremath{^{−}} occurring in the Hodge filtration. Setting H\ensuremath{_{\overline{D}\overline{R}}}\ensuremath{^{±}}(M) \ensuremath{=} H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M)/F\ensuremath{^{∓}}, one also has dim H\ensuremath{_{\overline{D}\overline{R}}}\ensuremath{^{±}}(M) \ensuremath{=} d\ensuremath{^{±}}(M).\par
It follows that the composite maps\par
(1.7.1)    I\ensuremath{^{±}}: H\ensuremath{_{B}}\ensuremath{^{±}}(M)\ensuremath{_{ℂ}} \ensuremath{→} H\ensuremath{_{B}}(M)\ensuremath{_{ℂ}} --I,\ensuremath{≅}\ensuremath{→} H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M)\ensuremath{_{ℂ}} \ensuremath{→} H\ensuremath{_{\overline{D}\overline{R}}}\ensuremath{^{±}}(M)\ensuremath{_{ℂ}}\par
are isomorphisms. Set\par
(1.7.2)    c\ensuremath{^{±}}(M) \ensuremath{=} det(I\ensuremath{^{±}}), (1.7.3)    \ensuremath{δ}(M) \ensuremath{=} det(I),\par
where the determinant is computed in rational bases of H\ensuremath{_{B}}\ensuremath{^{±}} and H\ensuremath{_{\overline{D}\overline{R}}}\ensuremath{^{±}}, respectively H\ensuremath{_{B}} and H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}. The definition of \ensuremath{δ}(M) does not require the hypotheses imposed on M.\par
By 1.6, I\ensuremath{^{+}} is real—that is, it induces I\ensuremath{^{+}}: H\ensuremath{_{B}}\ensuremath{^{+}}(M)\ensuremath{_{ℝ}} \ensuremath{→} H\ensuremath{_{\overline{D}\overline{R}}}\ensuremath{^{+}}(M)\ensuremath{_{ℝ}}—whereas I\ensuremath{^{−}} is purely imaginary. Thus c\ensuremath{^{+}}(M), i\ensuremath{^{d^−(M)}}c\ensuremath{^{−}}(M), and i\ensuremath{^{d^−(M)}}\ensuremath{δ}(M) are nonzero real numbers. Up to multiplication by a rational number, they depend only on M.\par
The periods of M are classically the numbers \ensuremath{⟨}\ensuremath{ω}, c\ensuremath{⟩} with \ensuremath{ω} \ensuremath{∈} H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M) and c \ensuremath{∈} H\ensuremath{_{B}}(M)\ensuremath{^{∨}}. For example, if X is an algebraic variety over \ensuremath{ℚ}, \ensuremath{ω} is an n-form on X defined over \ensuremath{ℚ}, and c is an n-cycle on X(\ensuremath{ℂ}), then \ensuremath{⟨}\ensuremath{ω}, c\ensuremath{⟩} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∫}\ensuremath{_{c}} \ensuremath{ω} is a period of H\ensuremath{^{n}}(X). Let us express c\ensuremath{^{±}}(M) in terms of periods. The dual of H\ensuremath{_{\overline{D}\overline{R}}}\ensuremath{^{±}}(M) is the subspace F\ensuremath{^{±}} of H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M\ensuremath{^{∨}}), where M\ensuremath{^{∨}} is the dual motive. If the chosen basis of H\ensuremath{_{\overline{D}\overline{R}}}\ensuremath{^{±}}(M) has dual basis (\ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{_{i}}) in F\ensuremath{^{±}}(H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M\ensuremath{^{∨}})), and (c\ensuremath{_{i}}) is the chosen basis of H\ensuremath{_{B}}\ensuremath{^{±}}(M), then the matrix of I\ensuremath{^{±}} is (\ensuremath{⟨}\ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{_{i}}, c\ensuremath{_{j}}\ensuremath{⟩}), and c\ensuremath{^{±}}(M) \ensuremath{=} det((\ensuremath{⟨}\ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{_{i}}, c\ensuremath{_{j}}\ensuremath{⟩})).\par
Conjecture 1.8. If M is critical, L(M) is a rational multiple of c\ensuremath{^{+}}(M).\par
2. Statement of the conjecture (general case).\par
2.1. Until now we have considered only L-functions given by Dirichlet series with rational coefficients. To do better, we must consider motives with coefficients in number fields.\par
There are two equivalent ways to construct the category of motives over k,\par
\clearpage
\PageUnit{9}{321}{FRENCH MAIN BODY}
with coefficients in a number field E, from the category of motives over k. The construction applies to any additive Karoubian category in which Hom(X, Y) is a \ensuremath{ℚ}-vector space.\par
A. A motive over k with coefficients in E is a motive M over k endowed with an E-module structure E \ensuremath{→} End(M).\par
B. The category \ensuremath{ℳ}\ensuremath{_{k,E}} of motives over k with coefficients in E is the Karoubian envelope of the category whose objects are motives over k—when regarded as an object of \ensuremath{ℳ}\ensuremath{_{k,E}}, a motive M is denoted M\ensuremath{_{E}}—and whose morphisms are\par
Hom(X\ensuremath{_{E}}, Y\ensuremath{_{E}}) \ensuremath{=} Hom(X, Y) \ensuremath{⊗} E.\par
Passage from B to A. For a motive X and a finite-dimensional \ensuremath{ℚ}-vector space V, let X \ensuremath{⊗} V denote the motive isomorphic to a sum of dim(V) copies of X and characterized by Hom(Y, X \ensuremath{⊗} V) \ensuremath{=} Hom(Y, X) \ensuremath{⊗} V, or equivalently by Hom(X \ensuremath{⊗} V, Y) \ensuremath{=} Hom(V, Hom(X, Y)). Associate to M\ensuremath{_{E}} the motive M \ensuremath{⊗} E with its natural E-module structure.\par
Passage from A to B. If M has an E-module structure, M\ensuremath{_{E}} carries two E-module structures: the one induced by the structure on M and the one carried by every object of \ensuremath{ℳ}\ensuremath{_{k,E}}. The object of \ensuremath{ℳ}\ensuremath{_{k,E}} corresponding to M is the largest direct factor of M\ensuremath{_{E}} on which these structures agree. More explicitly, E \ensuremath{⊗} E is a product of fields, including a copy of E in which x \ensuremath{⊗} 1 and 1 \ensuremath{⊗} x both project to x. The corresponding idempotent e acts on M\ensuremath{_{E}}, which is an E \ensuremath{⊗} E-module, and its image is the object of \ensuremath{ℳ}\ensuremath{_{k,E}} corresponding to M.\par
Motives with coefficients in E are most often given in form A. Form B has the advantage of remaining meaningful when E is not of finite rank over \ensuremath{ℚ}. It is useful for understanding the tensor formalism: tensor products and duals of motives with coefficients in E are defined by functoriality and the formulas\par
X\ensuremath{_{E}} \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{E}} Y\ensuremath{_{E}} \ensuremath{=} (X \ensuremath{⊗} Y)\ensuremath{_{E}} and (X\ensuremath{_{E}})\ensuremath{^{∨}} \ensuremath{=} (X\ensuremath{^{∨}})\ensuremath{_{E}}.\par
In language A, X \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{E}} Y is the largest direct factor of X \ensuremath{⊗} Y on which the two E-module structures agree, and X\ensuremath{^{∨}} is the ordinary dual of X endowed with the transposed E-module structure. Applying these observations to vector spaces over \ensuremath{ℚ} rather than to motives recovers the isomorphism between the E-dual of an E-vector space and its \ensuremath{ℚ}-dual, given by\par
\ensuremath{ω} \ensuremath{↦} the form Tr\ensuremath{_{E/ℚ}}(\ensuremath{⟨}\ensuremath{ω}, v\ensuremath{⟩}).\par
In 0.1.1 we defined restriction and extension functors for the base field k. They carry motives with coefficients in E to motives with coefficients in E. There are also restriction and extension functors for the coefficient field; let F be a finite extension of E.\par
Extension of coefficients. In language A this is X \ensuremath{↦} X \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{E}} F; in language B it is X\ensuremath{_{E}} \ensuremath{↦} X\ensuremath{_{F}}.\par
Restriction of coefficients. In language A one restricts the F-module structure to E.\par
Care must be taken not to confuse the roles of k and E. A standard example is H\ensuremath{_{1}} of an abelian variety over k with complex multiplication by an order in E. In terms of the abelian variety, taken up to isogeny, the preceding functors become extension of the base field, Weil restriction of scalars, the construction \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{E}} F, which multiplies the dimension by [F:E], and restriction to E of the F-module structure.\par

2.2. Let M be a motive over \ensuremath{ℚ} with coefficients in a number field E. For each prime number l, the l-adic realization H\ensuremath{_{l}}(M) is a module over the l-adic completion E\ensuremath{_{l}} of E. This completion is the product of the completions E\ensuremath{_{λ}} over the prime ideals \ensuremath{λ} above l, giving a decomposition of H\ensuremath{_{l}}(M) into a product of E\ensuremath{_{λ}}-modules H\ensuremath{_{λ}}(M).\par
One conjectures for the H\ensuremath{_{λ}}(M) a compatibility analogous to 1.2.1. If it holds, then for each complex embedding \ensuremath{σ} of E one may define a Dirichlet series with coefficients in \ensuremath{σ}E, convergent for Re(s) sufficiently large:\par
L(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∏}\ensuremath{_{p}} L\ensuremath{_{p}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s), where\par
(2.2.1)    L\ensuremath{_{p}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{σ}Z\ensuremath{_{p}}(M, p\ensuremath{^{−s}}), with Z\ensuremath{_{p}} \ensuremath{∈} E(t) \ensuremath{⊂} E\ensuremath{_{λ}}(t), given by Z\ensuremath{_{p}}(M, t) \ensuremath{=} det(1 \ensuremath{−} F\ensuremath{_{p}} t, H\ensuremath{_{λ}}(M)\ensuremath{^{I_p}})\ensuremath{^{−1}} for \ensuremath{λ}\ensuremath{∤}p.\par
As \ensuremath{σ} varies, these Dirichlet series are obtained from one another by conjugating their coefficients. We regard the system L(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s) as a function L*(M, s) with values in the \ensuremath{ℂ}-algebra E \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ}, identified with \ensuremath{ℂ}\ensuremath{^{Hom(E,ℂ)}} by\par
(2.2.2)    E \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ} --\ensuremath{≅}\ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{ℂ}\ensuremath{^{Hom(E,ℂ)}}: e \ensuremath{⊗} z \ensuremath{↦} (z, \ensuremath{σ}(e))\ensuremath{_{σ}}.\par
This function can also be defined directly by an Euler product. As in 1.1, it is expected to admit analytic continuation in s.\par
The Euler product L(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s) must be completed by an infinite factor L\ensuremath{_{∞}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s) depending on the Hodge realization of M. Setting \ensuremath{Λ}(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s) \ensuremath{=} L\ensuremath{_{∞}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s)L(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s), the conjectural functional equation is\par
(2.2.3)    \ensuremath{Λ}(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ε}(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s)\ensuremath{Λ}(\ensuremath{σ}, \ensuremath{\check{M}}, 1 \ensuremath{−} s),\par
where \ensuremath{ε}(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s), as a function of s, is the product of a constant and an exponential. The definitions of L\ensuremath{_{∞}} and \ensuremath{ε} are recalled in 5.2. As above, the systems \ensuremath{Λ} and \ensuremath{ε}, for varying \ensuremath{σ}, are regarded as functions \ensuremath{Λ}* and \ensuremath{ε}* with values in E \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ}.\par
It follows from 2.5 below that L\ensuremath{_{∞}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s) is independent of \ensuremath{σ}, and that the infinite factor for the motive R\ensuremath{_{E/ℚ}}M obtained by restriction of the coefficient field is the [E:\ensuremath{ℚ}]-th power of L\ensuremath{_{∞}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s). This justifies the following.\par
PROPOSITION-DEFINITION 2.3. Let M be a motive over \ensuremath{ℚ} with coefficients in E. An integer n is critical for M if the following equivalent conditions hold: (i) n is critical for R\ensuremath{_{E/ℚ}}M; (ii) neither L\ensuremath{_{∞}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s) nor L\ensuremath{_{∞}}(\ensuremath{σ}, \ensuremath{\check{M}}(1), s) has a pole at s \ensuremath{=} n. M is called critical if 0 is critical for M.\par
Our aim is to conjecture the value L*(M) \ensuremath{=}\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{def}}} L*(M, 0), for critical M, up to multiplication by an element of E. Equivalently, the values L(\ensuremath{σ}, M) \ensuremath{=}\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{def}}} L(\ensuremath{σ}, M, 0) are to be conjectured simultaneously, up to multiplication by a system of numbers \ensuremath{σ}(e), with e \ensuremath{∈} E.\par
2.4. The Betti realization H\ensuremath{_{B}}(M) is an E-vector space. Its dimension is the rank of M over E. The involution F\ensuremath{_{∞}} is E-linear, so its \ensuremath{+} and \ensuremath{−} parts are E-vector subspaces. Their dimensions are denoted d\ensuremath{^{+}}(M) and d\ensuremath{^{−}}(M).\par
\clearpage
\PageUnit{11}{323}{FRENCH MAIN BODY}
The complexification of H\ensuremath{_{B}}(M) is a free E \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ}-module. Identifying E \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ} with \ensuremath{ℂ}\ensuremath{^{Hom(E,ℂ)}} by (2.2.2), one obtains a decomposition\par
H\ensuremath{_{B}}(M) \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{⊕}\ensuremath{_{σ}} H\ensuremath{_{B}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M),\par
where\par
H\ensuremath{_{B}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M) \ensuremath{=} (H\ensuremath{_{B}}(M) \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ}) \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{E⊗ℂ,σ}} \ensuremath{ℂ},\par
or equivalently\par
H\ensuremath{_{B}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M) \ensuremath{=} H\ensuremath{_{B}}(M) \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{E,σ}} \ensuremath{ℂ}.\par
Since the Hodge summands H\ensuremath{_{B}}\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{pq}}}(M) are stable under E, each H\ensuremath{_{B}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M) inherits a Hodge decomposition H\ensuremath{_{B}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{⊕} H\ensuremath{_{B}}\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{pq}}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M). The involution F\ensuremath{_{∞}} interchanges H\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{pq}}} and H\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{qp}}}; in particular it stabilizes H\ensuremath{_{B}}\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{pp}}}, which it splits into \ensuremath{+} and \ensuremath{−} parts. Let h\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{pq}}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M) be the dimension of H\ensuremath{_{B}}\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{pq}}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M), and h\ensuremath{^{pp±}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M) that of H\ensuremath{_{B}}\ensuremath{^{pp±}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M). The following proposition allows \ensuremath{σ} to be omitted from the notation.\par
PROPOSITION 2.5. The numbers h\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{pq}}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M) and h\ensuremath{^{pp±}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M) are independent of \ensuremath{σ}.\par
We may and do assume that M is homogeneous. Then H\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{pq}}}(M) is the complexification of the E-vector space Gr\ensuremath{_{F}}\ensuremath{^{p}}(H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M)); it is a free E \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ}-module, proving the first assertion. For the second, h\ensuremath{^{pp±}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M) is the excess of d\ensuremath{^{±}}(M) over \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{p>q}} h\ensuremath{^{\mathrm{pq}}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M).\par

For the remainder of this section, unless explicitly stated otherwise, assume that R\ensuremath{_{E/ℚ}}M satisfies the hypotheses of 1.7. The spaces F\ensuremath{^{±}} and H\ensuremath{_{\overline{D}\overline{R}}}\ensuremath{^{±}} are now E-vector spaces. The isomorphisms (0.4.1) and (1.7.1),\par

are isomorphisms of E \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ}-modules between complexifications of E-vector spaces. Set\par
c\ensuremath{^{±}}(M) \ensuremath{=} det(I\ensuremath{^{±}}) \ensuremath{∈} (E \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ})*,    \ensuremath{δ}(M) \ensuremath{=} det(I) \ensuremath{∈} (E \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ})*,\par
where the determinant is computed in E-rational bases of H\ensuremath{_{B}}\ensuremath{^{±}}(M) and H\ensuremath{_{\overline{D}\overline{R}}}\ensuremath{^{±}}(M), respectively H\ensuremath{_{B}}(M) and H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M). The definition of \ensuremath{δ}(M) does not require the hypotheses imposed on M. Up to multiplication by an element of E*, these numbers depend only on M. Again by 1.6, c\ensuremath{^{+}}(M), i\ensuremath{^{d^−(M)}}c\ensuremath{^{−}}(M), and i\ensuremath{^{d^−(M)}}\ensuremath{δ}(M) lie in (E \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℝ})*.\par
Conjecture 2.7. Set \ensuremath{ℜ}(M) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{−}w/2. 2.13. Let D be a simple algebra over a field E, or even an Azumaya algebra over a ring. For the tensor formalism it is convenient to regard D-modules as “fictitious E-vector spaces”:\par
(a) To give an E-vector space V is equivalent to giving, for every étale extension F/E, an F-vector space V\ensuremath{_{F}} and compatible isomorphisms V\ensuremath{_{G}} \ensuremath{=} V\ensuremath{_{F}} \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{F}} G for extensions G/F. One takes V\ensuremath{_{F}} \ensuremath{=} V \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{E}} F. By descent it is enough to give V\ensuremath{_{F}} for sufficiently large F.\par
(b) Let W be a D-module. For every étale extension F/E and every F-isomorphism D \ensuremath{⊗} F \ensuremath{≅} End\ensuremath{_{F}}(L), with L free, set W\ensuremath{_{F,L}} \ensuremath{=} Hom\ensuremath{_{D⊗F}}(L, W \ensuremath{⊗} F), where all unlabelled tensor products are over E. There is an isomorphism of D \ensuremath{⊗} F-modules\par
W \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{E}} F \ensuremath{=} L \ensuremath{⊗}\ensuremath{_{F}} W\ensuremath{_{F,L}}.\par

\PageUnit{13}{325}{FRENCH MAIN BODY}
If F is large enough to split D, L is unique up to a nonunique isomorphism; the nonuniqueness is due to scalar homotheties, which act trivially on End(L). This is why the data W\ensuremath{_{F,L}} are not of type (a); they form a “fictitious vector space over E.”\par
Let (W\ensuremath{^{α}}) be a family of D-modules, and let T be a tensor operation. If the scalar homotheties of L act trivially on T(W\ensuremath{_{F,L}}\ensuremath{^{α}}), the F-vector space T(W\ensuremath{_{F,L}}\ensuremath{^{α}}) is independent of the choice of L, and one obtains a system of type (a), hence an E-vector space T(W\ensuremath{^{α}}).\par

\SectionLead{4. Compatibility with the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture.} 4.1. Let A be an abelian variety over \ensuremath{ℚ}, of dimension d. (4.1.1)    c\ensuremath{^{+}}(H\ensuremath{^{1}}(A)(1)) \ensuremath{=} det\ensuremath{⟨}\ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{_{i}}, e\ensuremath{_{j}}\ensuremath{⟩}.\par
Again denoting by e\ensuremath{_{i}} a representative cycle, one has \ensuremath{⟨}\ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{_{i}}, e\ensuremath{_{j}}\ensuremath{⟩} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∫}\ensuremath{_{e_j}} \ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{_{i}}. Take as (e\ensuremath{_{i}}) a \ensuremath{ℤ}-basis of H\ensuremath{_{1}}(A(\ensuremath{ℝ})\ensuremath{^{\circ}}, \ensuremath{ℤ}) \ensuremath{⊂} H\ensuremath{_{1}}(A(\ensuremath{ℂ}), \ensuremath{ℤ}). The Pontryagin product of the e\ensuremath{_{i}} is represented by the d-cycle A(\ensuremath{ℝ})\ensuremath{^{\circ}} in A(\ensuremath{ℂ}), with a suitable orientation; if \ensuremath{ω} is the exterior product of the \ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{_{i}}, the determinant (4.1.1) is \ensuremath{∫}\ensuremath{_{A(ℝ)°}} \ensuremath{ω}. Thus\par
\ensuremath{∫}\ensuremath{_{A(ℝ)}} \ensuremath{|}\ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{|} \ensuremath{=} [A(\ensuremath{ℝ}): A(\ensuremath{ℝ})\ensuremath{^{\circ}}] \ensuremath{|}\ensuremath{∫}\ensuremath{_{A(ℝ)°}} \ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{|},\par
and 1.8 for H\ensuremath{^{1}}(A)(1) is therefore equivalent to 4.1(b) above.\par
4.2. The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture gives the exact value of L(H\ensuremath{^{1}}(A), 1); the description of the rational factor in 4.1(b) in terms of the motive H\ensuremath{^{1}}(A)(1) has the following form: (a) The datum M \ensuremath{=} H\ensuremath{^{1}}(A)(1) is equivalent to that of A up to isogeny. One must begin by choosing A. This amounts to choosing an integral lattice in H\ensuremath{_{B}}(M) whose l-adic completions are stable under Gal(\ensuremath{\bar{ℚ}}/\ensuremath{ℚ}). (b) One then chooses \ensuremath{ω}, for example as the exterior product of the elements of a basis of an integral lattice in H\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{DR}}}(M). This \ensuremath{ω} determines the period c\ensuremath{^{+}}(M) and, for each prime p, a rational factor c\ensuremath{_{p}}(M). Almost all c\ensuremath{_{p}}(M) equal 1, and the product formula ensures that c\ensuremath{^{+}}(M)\ensuremath{·}\ensuremath{∏}\ensuremath{_{p}} c\ensuremath{_{p}}(M) is independent of \ensuremath{ω}. (c) Another rational number h(M) is defined in terms of cohomological invariants of A; the desired rational number is h(M)\ensuremath{·}\ensuremath{∏}\ensuremath{_{p}} c\ensuremath{_{p}}(M)\ensuremath{^{-1}}.\par
The invariance of the conjecture under isogeny is, moreover, a nontrivial theorem.\par

The group A(\ensuremath{ℚ}) may be interpreted as the group of extensions of \ensuremath{ℤ}(0) by H\ensuremath{_{1}}(A) in the category of 1-motives [6, \S{}10] over \ensuremath{ℚ}. We work in the general case of a motive over a number field k with coefficients in a field E equipped with a complex embedding \ensuremath{σ} (cf. 2.9). First, the general form: (a) For each place v of k, define a local factor L\ensuremath{_{v}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s). For complex v, the minimal subspaces are one-dimensional, of type (p, q). Write \ensuremath{Λ}(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s) for the product of the L\ensuremath{_{v}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s). (b) Let \ensuremath{Ψ} be a nontrivial character of the idele-class group \ensuremath{𝔸} \ensuremath{⊗} k/k, with components \ensuremath{Ψ}\ensuremath{_{v}}. Define local constants \ensuremath{ε}\ensuremath{_{v}}(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s, \ensuremath{Ψ}\ensuremath{_{v}}, dx\ensuremath{_{v}}), almost all equal to 1 and each, as a function of s, a constant times an exponential. Let \ensuremath{ε}(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s) be their product; it is independent of \ensuremath{Ψ} and the dx\ensuremath{_{v}}. (c) The conjectural functional equation is\par
\ensuremath{Λ}(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ε}(\ensuremath{σ}, M, s)\ensuremath{Λ}(\ensuremath{σ}, \ensuremath{\check{M}}, 1 \ensuremath{−} s).\par

\SectionLead{6. Example: Artin L-functions.} DEFINITION 6.1. The category of Artin motives is the Karoubian envelope of the dual of the category whose objects are zero-dimensional varieties over \ensuremath{ℚ} and whose morphisms are correspondences defined over \ensuremath{ℚ}.\par
By definition, every zero-dimensional variety X defines an Artin motive H(X); H is a fully faithful contravariant functor,\par
H: (zero-dimensional varieties, correspondences) \ensuremath{⟶} (Artin motives),\par
and every Artin motive is a direct factor of some H(X).\par
6.2. Let us make this definition explicit. Let \ensuremath{\bar{ℚ}} be an algebraic closure of \ensuremath{ℚ}, and let G be the Galois group Gal(\ensuremath{\bar{ℚ}}/\ensuremath{ℚ}). A zero-dimensional variety is the spectrum of a finite product A of number fields, and Galois theory, in the form given to it by Grothendieck, says that the functor\par
X \ensuremath{=} spec(A) \ensuremath{↦} X(\ensuremath{\bar{ℚ}}) \ensuremath{=} Hom(A, \ensuremath{\bar{ℚ}}):\par
(category of zero-dimensional varieties over \ensuremath{ℚ} and morphisms of schemes) \ensuremath{⟶} (category of finite sets with a continuous G-action)\par
is an equivalence of categories. The inverse functor sends I to the spectrum of the ring of G-invariant functions from I to \ensuremath{\bar{ℚ}}.\par
A correspondence from a zero-dimensional variety X to another Y is a formal rational linear combination of connected components of X \ensuremath{×} Y. The map\par
\ensuremath{∑} a\ensuremath{_{i}} Z\ensuremath{_{i}} \ensuremath{↦} \ensuremath{∑} a\ensuremath{_{i}}(characteristic function of Z\ensuremath{_{i}}(\ensuremath{\bar{ℚ}}) \ensuremath{⊂} (X \ensuremath{×} Y)(\ensuremath{\bar{ℚ}}))\par
identifies correspondences with rational-valued G-invariant functions on (X \ensuremath{×} Y)(\ensuremath{\bar{ℚ}}) \ensuremath{=} X(\ensuremath{\bar{ℚ}}) \ensuremath{×} Y(\ensuremath{\bar{ℚ}}), and composition of correspondences with matrix multiplication.\par
Let H be the contravariant functor sending X to the vector space \ensuremath{ℚ}\ensuremath{^{X(\bar{ℚ})}} with its natural G-action; a correspondence F: X \ensuremath{→} Y is sent to the morphism F*: \ensuremath{ℚ}\ensuremath{^{Y(\bar{ℚ})}} \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{ℚ}\ensuremath{^{X(\bar{ℚ})}} whose matrix is \ensuremath{^{t}}F. This functor is fully faithful and identifies the category of Artin motives with that of rational representations of G.\par
6.3. In this model, if \ensuremath{\bar{ℚ}} is the algebraic closure of \ensuremath{ℚ} inside \ensuremath{ℂ}, the “Betti realization” functor H\ensuremath{_{B}} is the “underlying vector space” functor. The Hodge structure is purely of type (0, 0), and the involution F\ensuremath{_{∞}} is the action of complex conjugation F\ensuremath{_{∞}} \ensuremath{∈} G. Indeed, there is an isomorphism, functorial for correspondences,\par
\clearpage

The “l-adic realization” functor H\ensuremath{_{l}} is H\ensuremath{_{l}}(V) \ensuremath{=} V \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℚ}\ensuremath{_{l}}. Indeed, there is an isomorphism, functorial for correspondences,\par
H\ensuremath{^{*}}(X(\ensuremath{\bar{ℚ}}), \ensuremath{ℚ}\ensuremath{_{l}}) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ℚ}\ensuremath{_{l}}\ensuremath{^{X(\bar{ℚ})}} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ℚ}\ensuremath{^{X(\bar{ℚ})}} \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℚ}\ensuremath{_{l}}.\par

If \ensuremath{ι}: E \ensuremath{→} E\ensuremath{′} \ensuremath{⊂} \ensuremath{ℂ} is a finite extension of E, the left-hand side does not change when \ensuremath{η} is replaced by \ensuremath{ι}\ensuremath{η}, by (8.18.2). One has (\ensuremath{ι}\ensuremath{η})* \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{η}* \ensuremath{∘} N\ensuremath{_{E′/E}}; by (8.18.3), the right-hand side is likewise unchanged.\par
If \ensuremath{ι}: k \ensuremath{→} k\ensuremath{′} is a finite extension of degree d, the left-hand side is raised to the d-th power when k is replaced by k\ensuremath{′} and \ensuremath{η} by \ensuremath{η} \ensuremath{∘} N\ensuremath{_{k′/k}}: a complex embedding of k is induced by d embeddings of k\ensuremath{′}, and (8.18.3) applies. The same holds for the right-hand side, by (8.18.2) and the equality (\ensuremath{η} \ensuremath{∘} N\ensuremath{_{k′/k}})* \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ι}\ensuremath{η}*.\par
These compatibilities reduce us to the case in which E is Galois and k is isomorphic to E. For each isomorphism \ensuremath{ω} from k to E, put n(\ensuremath{ω}) \ensuremath{=} n(\ensuremath{η}; 1 \ensuremath{∘} \ensuremath{ω}, 1). Writing the group of homomorphisms k* \ensuremath{→} E* additively, \ensuremath{η} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∑} n(\ensuremath{ω})\ensuremath{ω}. Since p(\ensuremath{η}; 1 \ensuremath{∘} \ensuremath{ω}, 1) \ensuremath{=} p(\ensuremath{η} \ensuremath{∘} \ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{^{-1}}; 1, 1), by (8.15.4), one obtains\par
(8.20.1)    \ensuremath{∏}\ensuremath{_{n(σ,1)<w/2}} p(\ensuremath{η}; \ensuremath{σ}, 1) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∏}\ensuremath{_{n(ω)<w/2}} p(\ensuremath{η} \ensuremath{∘} \ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{^{-1}}; 1, 1) \ensuremath{=} p(\ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{n(ω)<w/2}} \ensuremath{η} \ensuremath{∘} \ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{^{-1}}; 1, 1).\par
On the other hand,\par
\clearpage
\PageUnit{31}{343}{SOURCE ENGLISH KOBLITZ OGUS APPENDIX}
\ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{n(ω)<w/2}} \ensuremath{η} \ensuremath{∘} \ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{^{-1}} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{n(ω₁)<w/2;\,ω₂}} n(\ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{_{2}}) \ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{_{2}} \ensuremath{∘} \ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{_{1}}\ensuremath{^{-1}} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{ω₂}} n(\ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{_{2}}) \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{n(ω₁)<w/2}} \ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{_{2}} \ensuremath{∘} \ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{_{1}}\ensuremath{^{-1}} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{ω₂}} n(\ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{_{2}}) \ensuremath{ω}\ensuremath{_{2}} \ensuremath{∘} \ensuremath{η}*.\par
This permits (8.20.1) to be continued as\par
\ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∏}\ensuremath{_{ω}} p(\ensuremath{ω} \ensuremath{∘} \ensuremath{η}*; 1, 1)\ensuremath{^{n(ω)}} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∏}\ensuremath{_{ω}} p(\ensuremath{η}*; 1, 1 \ensuremath{∘} \ensuremath{ω})\ensuremath{^{n(ω)}} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∏}\ensuremath{_{σ}} p(\ensuremath{η}*; 1, \ensuremath{σ})\ensuremath{^{n(η;σ,1)}},\par
(a new application of 8.15.4), and proves 8.20.\par
8.21. Combining 8.16 and 8.20, one obtains, for the component of index 1 of\par
c\ensuremath{^{+}} R\ensuremath{_{k/ℚ}} M(\ensuremath{χ}) \ensuremath{∈} (E \ensuremath{⊗} \ensuremath{ℂ})*/E* \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ℂ}\ensuremath{^{*J}}/E*,\par
the following expression, modulo \ensuremath{\bar{Q}}*:\par
c\ensuremath{_{1}}\ensuremath{^{+}} R\ensuremath{_{k/ℚ}} M(\ensuremath{χ}) \ensuremath{∼} \ensuremath{∏}\ensuremath{_{σ}} p(\ensuremath{χ}*\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{alg}}}; 1, \ensuremath{σ})\ensuremath{^{−n(χ;σ,1)}}.\par
If \ensuremath{χ} (i.e., M(\ensuremath{χ})) is critical, Conjecture 2.8 therefore asserts that\par
L(1 \ensuremath{∘} \ensuremath{χ}, 0) \ensuremath{∼} \ensuremath{∏}\ensuremath{_{σ}} p(\ensuremath{χ}*\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{alg}}}; 1, \ensuremath{σ})\ensuremath{^{−n(χ;σ,1)}}    (mod \ensuremath{\bar{Q}}*).\par
For E sufficiently large, \ensuremath{χ}*\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{alg}}} is the algebraic part of a Hecke character \ensuremath{χ}*, and the periods are interpreted as periods of abelian integrals (8.19), (8.3). The resulting statement is the one Shimura proved when k is of CM type, or is abelian over a field of CM type (with a restriction on the weight).\par
REMARK 8.22. If \ensuremath{η} : k* \ensuremath{→} E* satisfies (8.2.1), there are subfields k\ensuremath{′} of k and E\ensuremath{′} of E, each either of CM type or equal to \ensuremath{ℚ}, an inclusion \ensuremath{ι} : E\ensuremath{′} \ensuremath{→} E, and a factorization \ensuremath{η} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{ι}\ensuremath{η}\ensuremath{′}N\ensuremath{_{k/k′}}. One then has p(\ensuremath{η}; \ensuremath{σ}, \ensuremath{τ}) \ensuremath{=} p(\ensuremath{η}\ensuremath{′}; \ensuremath{σ} \ensuremath{|} k\ensuremath{′}, \ensuremath{τ} \ensuremath{|} E\ensuremath{′}).\par
If now k and E are of CM type (or \ensuremath{ℚ}), and c again denotes their complex conjugation, then c\ensuremath{σ} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{σ}c, c\ensuremath{τ} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{τ}c, and \ensuremath{η}c \ensuremath{=} c\ensuremath{η}; hence\par
p(\ensuremath{η}; \ensuremath{σ}, \ensuremath{τ}\ensuremath{\bar{)}} \ensuremath{=} p(\ensuremath{η}; c\ensuremath{σ}, c\ensuremath{τ}) \ensuremath{=} p(\ensuremath{η}; \ensuremath{σ}c, \ensuremath{τ}c) \ensuremath{=} p(c\ensuremath{η}c\ensuremath{^{-1}}; \ensuremath{σ}, \ensuremath{τ}) \ensuremath{=} p(\ensuremath{η}; \ensuremath{σ}, \ensuremath{τ}).\par
Thus the periods, a priori in \ensuremath{ℂ}*/\ensuremath{\bar{Q}}*, are real, i.e., lie in \ensuremath{ℝ}*/(\ensuremath{\bar{Q}}* \ensuremath{∩} \ensuremath{ℝ}*).\par
REMARK 8.23. Let G be the Galois group of the union of the CM-type extensions of \ensuremath{ℚ} in \ensuremath{\bar{Q}} \ensuremath{⊂} \ensuremath{ℂ}, and let c \ensuremath{∈} G be complex conjugation. It is a central element of G. If \ensuremath{φ} is a locally constant integer-valued function on G, put \ensuremath{φ}*(x) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{φ}(xc). Suppose that \ensuremath{φ} \ensuremath{+} \ensuremath{φ}* is constant. Let G\ensuremath{_{1}} be a finite quotient of G through which \ensuremath{φ} factors as a function \ensuremath{φ}\ensuremath{_{1}} on G\ensuremath{_{1}}, and let k be the corresponding field. The hypothesis means that the endomorphism \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{φ}\ensuremath{_{1}}(\ensuremath{σ})\ensuremath{σ} of k* satisfies (8.2.1). The period p(\ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{φ}\ensuremath{_{1}}(\ensuremath{σ})\ensuremath{σ}; 1, 1) is independent of the choice of G\ensuremath{_{1}}; put P(\ensuremath{φ}) \ensuremath{=} p(\ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{φ}\ensuremath{_{1}}(\ensuremath{σ})\ensuremath{σ}; 1, 1). The functional P is a homomorphism into \ensuremath{ℂ}*/\ensuremath{\bar{Q}}* from the group of locally constant integer-valued functions on G satisfying \ensuremath{φ} \ensuremath{+} \ensuremath{φ}* \ensuremath{=} constant.\par
Appendix by N. Koblitz and A. Ogus. Algebraicity of some products of values of the \ensuremath{Γ} function. Let A\ensuremath{_{N}} \ensuremath{=} N\ensuremath{^{-1}}\ensuremath{ℤ}/\ensuremath{ℤ} \ensuremath{−} \{0\}, and let U\ensuremath{_{N}} \ensuremath{=} (\ensuremath{ℤ}/N\ensuremath{ℤ})* operate on A\ensuremath{_{N}} in the obvious way. If f : A\ensuremath{_{N}} \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{ℂ}, define \ensuremath{⟨}f\ensuremath{⟩} : U\ensuremath{_{N}} \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{ℂ} by\par
\ensuremath{⟨}f\ensuremath{⟩}(u) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{a∈A_N}} \ensuremath{⟨}a\ensuremath{⟩}f(ua),\par
where \ensuremath{⟨}a\ensuremath{⟩} denotes the representative of a between 0 and 1.\par
We first compute H\ensuremath{_{Q}} \ensuremath{=} \{f : A\ensuremath{_{N}} \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{ℚ} : \ensuremath{⟨}f\ensuremath{⟩} is constant\}.\par
EXAMPLE. If n is a divisor of N and a \ensuremath{∈} A\ensuremath{_{N}}, consider the set\par
S\ensuremath{_{n,a}} \ensuremath{=} [\{a \ensuremath{+} k/n : k \ensuremath{=} 0, 1, …, n \ensuremath{−} 1\} \ensuremath{∪} \{\ensuremath{−}na\}] \ensuremath{∩} A\ensuremath{_{N}}.\par
It is easy to see that its characteristic function \ensuremath{ε}\ensuremath{_{n,a}} is in H\ensuremath{_{Q}}. Note that S\ensuremath{_{1,a}} \ensuremath{=} \{a, \ensuremath{−}a\}.\par
\clearpage
\PageUnit{32}{344}{SOURCE ENGLISH KOBLITZ OGUS APPENDIX}
PROPOSITION. H\ensuremath{_{Q}} is generated by \{\ensuremath{ε}\ensuremath{_{n,a}} : n \ensuremath{=} 1 or n is prime and na \ensuremath{≠} 0\}.\par
PROOF. The orbits of A\ensuremath{_{N}} under the action of U\ensuremath{_{N}} correspond to the divisors d of N with 1 \ensuremath{<} d \ensuremath{≤} N: each orbit can be written uniquely in the form U\ensuremath{_{N}}(1/d). The stabilizer subgroup I\ensuremath{_{d}} of 1/d is \{u \ensuremath{∈} U\ensuremath{_{N}} : u \ensuremath{≡} 1 mod d\} and the orbit of 1/d is canonically isomorphic to U\ensuremath{_{d}}. Thus, a function f on A\ensuremath{_{N}} is determined by the collection of functions f\ensuremath{_{d}} : U\ensuremath{_{d}} \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{ℚ} defined by f\ensuremath{_{d}}(v) \ensuremath{=} f(v/d).\par
To prove the proposition, we first complexify, so as to be able to work with characters of U\ensuremath{_{N}}.\par
LEMMA. If f : A\ensuremath{_{N}} \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{ℂ} and if \ensuremath{χ} is a character of U\ensuremath{_{N}}, then the inner product of \ensuremath{⟨}f\ensuremath{⟩} and \ensuremath{χ} is given by:\par
(\ensuremath{⟨}f\ensuremath{⟩} \ensuremath{|} \ensuremath{χ}) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{−}\ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{d}} L(0, \ensuremath{χ}\ensuremath{_{d}})\ensuremath{|}I\ensuremath{_{d}}\ensuremath{|} \ensuremath{·} (f\ensuremath{_{d}} \ensuremath{|} \ensuremath{χ}\ensuremath{_{d}}),\par
where the sum is taken over those divisors d of N such that \ensuremath{χ} is pulled back from a character \ensuremath{χ}\ensuremath{_{d}} of U\ensuremath{_{d}}.\par
PROOF. We have\par
(\ensuremath{⟨}f\ensuremath{⟩} \ensuremath{|} \ensuremath{χ}) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{u∈U_N}} \ensuremath{⟨}f\ensuremath{⟩}(u)\ensuremath{\bar{χ}}(u) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{a∈A_N}} \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{u∈U_N}} \ensuremath{⟨}a\ensuremath{⟩}f(ua)\ensuremath{\bar{χ}}(u) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{d∣N}} \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{v∈U_d}} \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{u∈U_N}} \ensuremath{⟨}v/d\ensuremath{⟩}f\ensuremath{_{d}}(u\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{dv}}})\ensuremath{\bar{χ}}(u),\par
where u\ensuremath{_{d}} \ensuremath{∈} U\ensuremath{_{d}} is the image of u.\par
Now if we choose a set U\ensuremath{′}\ensuremath{_{d}} \ensuremath{⊆} U\ensuremath{_{N}} of coset representatives for U\ensuremath{_{N}} \ensuremath{→} U\ensuremath{_{d}}, we can write:\par
\ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{u∈U_N}} f\ensuremath{_{d}}(u\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{dv}}})\ensuremath{\bar{χ}}(u) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{u′∈U′_d}} \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{u∈I_d}} f\ensuremath{_{d}}(u\ensuremath{′}\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{dv}}})\ensuremath{\bar{χ}}(u\ensuremath{′})\ensuremath{\bar{χ}}(u) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{u′∈U′_d}} f\ensuremath{_{d}}(u\ensuremath{′}\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{dv}}})\ensuremath{\bar{χ}}(u\ensuremath{′}) \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{u∈I_d}} \ensuremath{\bar{χ}}(u).\par
Of course, this sum is zero unless \ensuremath{χ} is trivial on I\ensuremath{_{d}}, i.e., unless \ensuremath{χ} is pulled back from a character \ensuremath{χ}\ensuremath{_{d}} of U\ensuremath{_{d}}, in which case it is\par
\ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{u′∈U′_d}} f\ensuremath{_{d}}(u\ensuremath{′}\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{dv}}})\ensuremath{\bar{χ}}\ensuremath{_{d}}(u\ensuremath{′})\ensuremath{|}I\ensuremath{_{d}}\ensuremath{|} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{w∈U_d}} f\ensuremath{_{d}}(wv)\ensuremath{\bar{χ}}\ensuremath{_{d}}(w)\ensuremath{χ}\ensuremath{_{d}}(v)\ensuremath{|}I\ensuremath{_{d}}\ensuremath{|} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{χ}\ensuremath{_{d}}(v)\ensuremath{|}I\ensuremath{_{d}}\ensuremath{|}(f\ensuremath{_{d}} \ensuremath{|} \ensuremath{χ}\ensuremath{_{d}}).\par
If we substitute this into the above expression for (\ensuremath{⟨}f\ensuremath{⟩} \ensuremath{|} \ensuremath{χ}) and note that \ensuremath{⟨}v/d\ensuremath{⟩} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{|}v\ensuremath{|}/d, where \ensuremath{|}v\ensuremath{|} is the smallest positive representative of v \ensuremath{∈} U\ensuremath{_{d}}, we find:\par
(\ensuremath{⟨}f\ensuremath{⟩} \ensuremath{|} \ensuremath{χ}) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{d}} \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{v∈U_d}} \ensuremath{|}v\ensuremath{|}\ensuremath{χ}\ensuremath{_{d}}(v)\ensuremath{|}I\ensuremath{_{d}}\ensuremath{|}d\ensuremath{^{-1}}(f\ensuremath{_{d}} \ensuremath{|} \ensuremath{χ}\ensuremath{_{d}}) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{−}\ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{d}} L(0, \ensuremath{χ}\ensuremath{_{d}})\ensuremath{|}I\ensuremath{_{d}}\ensuremath{|}(f\ensuremath{_{d}} \ensuremath{|} \ensuremath{χ}\ensuremath{_{d}}),\par
as claimed. \ensuremath{□}\par
To prove the proposition, we let d\ensuremath{_{f}} be the largest divisor of N such that f\ensuremath{_{d_f}} \ensuremath{≠} 0; it suffices to prove that any f in H\ensuremath{_{Q}} can be written as g \ensuremath{+} f\ensuremath{′}, with g a linear combination of the \ensuremath{ε}’s and d\ensuremath{_{f′}} \ensuremath{<} d\ensuremath{_{f}}. Let d \ensuremath{=} d\ensuremath{_{f}}; we shall show below that f\ensuremath{_{d}} is a linear combination of functions h\ensuremath{_{1}} which factor through U\ensuremath{_{d}} \ensuremath{→} U\ensuremath{_{d}}/\{\ensuremath{±}1\}, and functions h\ensuremath{_{p}} which factor through U\ensuremath{_{d}} \ensuremath{→} U\ensuremath{_{d/p}} for some prime divisor p of d, p \ensuremath{≠} d. Any function of the first type is invariant under \ensuremath{±}1, and hence the corresponding function on the orbit U\ensuremath{_{N}}(1/d) is a linear combination of \ensuremath{ε}\ensuremath{_{1,a}}’s. A function of the second type is invariant under the kernel K of U\ensuremath{_{d}} \ensuremath{→} U\ensuremath{_{d/p}}, which has order p if p divides d/p and order p \ensuremath{−} 1 otherwise. In the first case, K \ensuremath{=} \{1 \ensuremath{+} kd/p : k \ensuremath{=} 0, …, p \ensuremath{−} 1\}, and in\par
\clearpage
\PageUnit{33}{345}{SOURCE ENGLISH KOBLITZ OGUS APPENDIX}
the second, it is this same set with one element deleted, namely, the value of 1 \ensuremath{+} kd/p which is divisible by p. Thus, the set S\ensuremath{_{p,w/d}} \ensuremath{=} (Kw)/d, with the addition of one or two elements in the orbit U\ensuremath{_{N}}(p/d). Hence it is clear that the corresponding function on the orbit U\ensuremath{_{N}}(1/d) can be written as a linear combination of \ensuremath{ε}\ensuremath{_{p,a}}’s and functions supported on the orbit of p/d, and we have obtained our desired decomposition f \ensuremath{=} g \ensuremath{+} f\ensuremath{′}.\par
It remains for us to prove the claim about f\ensuremath{_{d}}. If d is twice an odd number the map U\ensuremath{_{d}} \ensuremath{→} U\ensuremath{_{d/2}} is an isomorphism, and the claim is trivial. In the other cases, choose a primitive odd character \ensuremath{χ}\ensuremath{_{d}} of U\ensuremath{_{d}}, and let \ensuremath{χ} be the pull-back of \ensuremath{χ}\ensuremath{_{d}} to U\ensuremath{_{N}}. Since \ensuremath{χ} is nontrivial and \ensuremath{⟨}f\ensuremath{⟩} is constant, (\ensuremath{⟨}f\ensuremath{⟩} \ensuremath{|} \ensuremath{χ}) \ensuremath{=} 0. Let us look at the expression for this inner product provided by the lemma; the only nonzero terms come from those f\ensuremath{_{e}} such that f\ensuremath{_{e}} \ensuremath{≠} 0 and I\ensuremath{_{e}} \ensuremath{⊆} Ker(\ensuremath{χ}). But then Ker(\ensuremath{χ}) contains I\ensuremath{_{e}} and I\ensuremath{_{d}}, hence also I\ensuremath{_{\mathrm{dI}}}\ensuremath{_{e}} \ensuremath{=} I\ensuremath{_{m}}, where m \ensuremath{=} g.c.d.(d, e). Since \ensuremath{χ} is primitive, m \ensuremath{=} d and d divides e, and since f\ensuremath{_{e}} \ensuremath{=} 0 for e \ensuremath{>} d,\par
0 \ensuremath{=} (\ensuremath{⟨}f\ensuremath{⟩} \ensuremath{|} \ensuremath{χ}) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{−}L(0, \ensuremath{χ}\ensuremath{_{d}})\ensuremath{|}I\ensuremath{_{d}}\ensuremath{|}(f\ensuremath{_{d}} \ensuremath{|} \ensuremath{χ}\ensuremath{_{d}}).\par
Since \ensuremath{χ}\ensuremath{_{d}} is odd and primitive, we conclude that (f\ensuremath{_{d}} \ensuremath{|} \ensuremath{χ}\ensuremath{_{d}}) \ensuremath{=} 0, i.e., f\ensuremath{_{d}} is orthogonal to every odd primitive character of U\ensuremath{_{d}}. It follows that f\ensuremath{_{d}} can be written as a linear combination of characters which are even (factor through U\ensuremath{_{d}}/\{\ensuremath{±}1\}) or imprimitive (factor through some U\ensuremath{_{d/p}}). When d \ensuremath{=} p, we should also remark that the constant functions on U\ensuremath{_{p}} are also invariant under \ensuremath{±}1, and hence are already covered by the first case. \ensuremath{□}\par
THEOREM. Suppose that f : A\ensuremath{_{N}} \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{ℚ} is such that \ensuremath{⟨}f\ensuremath{⟩} is constant: \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{a}}\ensuremath{⟨}a\ensuremath{⟩}f(ua) \ensuremath{=} k for all u \ensuremath{∈} U\ensuremath{_{N}}. Then\par
\ensuremath{Γ}(f) \ensuremath{≔} \ensuremath{π}\ensuremath{^{-k}} \ensuremath{∏}\ensuremath{_{a}} \ensuremath{Γ}(\ensuremath{⟨}a\ensuremath{⟩})\ensuremath{^{f(a)}}\par
is an algebraic number.\par
PROOF. The map \ensuremath{⟨} \ensuremath{⟩} : H\ensuremath{_{Q}} \ensuremath{→} \ensuremath{ℚ} sending any f to \ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{a}}\ensuremath{⟨}a\ensuremath{⟩}f(a) is evidently linear, and hence \ensuremath{Γ}(f \ensuremath{+} g) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{Γ}(f)\ensuremath{Γ}(g), for f and g in H\ensuremath{_{Q}}. By the proposition, any f in H\ensuremath{_{Q}} is a \ensuremath{ℚ}-linear combination of the \ensuremath{ε}’s; hence nf is a sum of \ensuremath{ε}’s for some n. Since \ensuremath{Γ}(f)\ensuremath{^{n}} \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{Γ}(nf), we are reduced to checking the theorem for the \ensuremath{ε}’s described in the proposition. Noting that \ensuremath{⟨}\ensuremath{ε}\ensuremath{_{1,a}}\ensuremath{⟩} \ensuremath{=} 1 and that \ensuremath{⟨}\ensuremath{ε}\ensuremath{_{p,a}}\ensuremath{⟩} \ensuremath{=} (p \ensuremath{+} 1)/2 if pa \ensuremath{≠} 0, one has only to appeal to the identities:\par
\ensuremath{Γ}(x)\ensuremath{Γ}(1 \ensuremath{−} x) \ensuremath{=} \ensuremath{π}(sin \ensuremath{π}x)\ensuremath{^{-1}},\par
\ensuremath{∑}\ensuremath{_{k=1}}\ensuremath{^{p−1}} \ensuremath{Γ}(x \ensuremath{+} k/p) \ensuremath{=} p\ensuremath{^{1/2−x}}(2\ensuremath{π})\ensuremath{^{(p−1)/2}} \ensuremath{Γ}(px). \ensuremath{□}\par
