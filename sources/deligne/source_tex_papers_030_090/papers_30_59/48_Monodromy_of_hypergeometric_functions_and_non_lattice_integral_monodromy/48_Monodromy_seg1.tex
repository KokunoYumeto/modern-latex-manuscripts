\section*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}

The hypergeometric series
\begin{equation}\label{eq:1}
F(a,b;c;x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(a,n)(b,n)}{(c,n)\, n!} x^n, \qquad |x| < 1
\end{equation}
where $(a,n) = \prod_{i=0}^{n-1} (a+i)$, defined for $c$ not an integer $\leq 0$, was first introduced by Euler in 1778 as a solution of the hypergeometric differential equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2}
x(1-x)y'' + (c - (a+b+1)x) y' - aby = 0.
\end{equation}
$F(a,b;c;x)$ represents the unique solution of \eqref{eq:2} which is holomorphic at $x=0$ and takes the value $1$ at $x=0$. If neither $b$ nor $c-b$ is an integer $< 0$, Euler knew the integral representation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:3}
F(a,b;c;x) = \frac{\Gamma(b)\Gamma(c-b)}{\Gamma(c)} \int_0^1 z^{b-1} (1-z)^{c-b-1} (1-zx)^{-a} \, dz.
\end{equation}
Replacing $z$ by $u^{-1}$, \eqref{eq:3} also has the form
\begin{equation*}\tag{$3'$}
\int_1^{\infty} u^{-a} (u-1)^{c-b-1} (u-x)^{-b} \, du.
\end{equation*}
If we integrate instead from $g$ to $h$ with $g$ and $h$ in $\{0, 1, \infty, x\}$ we get other solutions of \eqref{eq:2}, a fact discovered independently by Hermite \cite{Hermite}, Pochhammer \cite{Pochhammer}, and Sch\"afli \cite{Schaefli}. Even when the integral diverges, it yields solutions of \eqref{eq:2} when taken as its Hadamard ``finite part'', provided that the integrand does not have a pole of integral order at $g$ or $h$.

The hypergeometric equation \eqref{eq:2} is the unique second order linear differential equation with regular singularities, singular only at $0, 1, \infty$ with exponents $(0, 1-c)$, $(0, c-a-b)$, $(a, b)$ respectively. ``Exponents $(\alpha, \alpha')$ at a singularity $p$'' means that suitable linearly independent linear combinations of the branches of a solution have the form $(x-p)^\alpha f_1(x)$, $(x-p)^{\alpha'} f_2(x)$ with $f$ holomorphic around $p$; if $\alpha - \alpha' \in \ZZ$ an additional logarithmic term is allowed. In 1857, Riemann proved that the solutions of \eqref{eq:2} are the only multivalued functions with exactly two linearly independent branches, branching only at $0, 1, \infty$ with exponents as above (cf.\ \cite{Riemann}). Riemann's proof assumed that none of the exponent differences at a singularity was an integer and proceeded by first computing global monodromy. The above characterization of the hypergeometric equation is the basis of Fuchs' proof of Riemann's theorem (cf.\ \cite{Fuchs}).

In his seminal paper \cite{Schwarz}, which seeks to determine the values $a$, $b$, $c$ for which the hypergeometric function is an algebraic function of $x$, Schwarz considered the map $x \mapsto w_2(x)/w_1(x)$ where $w_1$ and $w_2$ form a base of $W$, the two dimensional linear space of all solutions of \eqref{eq:2}. Let $\widetilde{Q}$ denote the universal covering space of $Q := \PP^1(\CC) - \{0, 1, \infty\}$. The map
\begin{equation}\label{eq:4}
w : x \mapsto w_2(x)/w_1(x)
\end{equation}
is a multivalued map from $Q$ to the projective space $\PP(W^*)$ of lines in the dual space $W^*$ of $W$, i.e.\ $w$ may be construed as a single-valued map
\begin{equation}\label{eq:4p}
\widetilde{w} : \widetilde{Q} \to \PP(W^*).
\end{equation}
The fundamental group $\pi_1(Q)$ acts on $\PP(W^*)$ (``monodromy action'') and the map is $\pi_1(Q)$-equivariant. Let $\Gamma$ denote the image of $\pi_1(Q)$ via the monodromy action. For Schwarz's original problem, the question reduces to ``when is $\Gamma$ finite?'' Schwarz also solved the problem: when can \eqref{eq:4} be inverted to provide a univalued map from an open domain to $Q$? When this happens $\Gamma$ has a fundamental domain for its action on either (i) $\PP(W^*)$, (ii) $\PP(W^*)$ minus a point, or (iii) a disc in $\PP(W^*)$, and is consequently discrete in $\PGL_2(\CC)$.

It is case (iii) of this latter question which Picard generalized in \cite{Picard16b} to a two variable analogue of the hypergeometric function. In the more general $d$ variable case, this function is best defined by its integral representation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:3pp}
F(x_2, \ldots, x_{d+1}) = \int_1^{\infty} u^{-\mu_0}(u-1)^{-\mu_1} \prod_{i=2}^{d+1} (u-x_i)^{-\mu_i} \, du.
\end{equation}
Let $b_\infty$ be the order of the pole of the integrand at $\infty$. When the sum is extended over all the $\mu$'s, one has $\sum b_\mu = 2$. In this introduction, we assume that none of the $\mu_i$ is an integer.

The function $G$ obtained from $F$ by holding fixed all variables but one was investigated by Pochhammer \cite{Pochhammer}. He formed the $(d+1)$-order linear differential equation satisfied by $G$ and characterized its solutions as the multivalued functions with exactly $d+1$ linearly independent branches and ramification of a prescribed type. At each finite ramification point, $d$ branches of $G$ are holomorphic, and at $\infty$ the same holds true after multiplying $G$ by a suitable power of the variable.

The function $F$ defined by \eqref{eq:3pp} has a power series expansion
\begin{equation}\label{eq:1p}
\sum_{\substack{n_i \geq 0 \\ 2 \leq i \leq d+1}} \frac{\prod_{i=2}^{d+1} (\mu_i, n_i) \cdot (1 - \sum \mu_i, \sum n_i)}{(1 - \mu_0, \sum n_i) \cdot (\sum n_i)!} \prod_{i=2}^{d+1} x_i^{n_i}.
\end{equation}
For $d = 2$, this series has been investigated by Appell \cite{Appell} who showed that it satisfies a system of linear partial differential equations expressing each second derivative of $F$ in terms of its first derivatives. In \cite{Picard16a} Picard characterized the solutions of this system as the multivalued functions of two variables $x, y$ with exactly three linearly independent branches and with ramification of a prescribed type along the seven lines $x$ or $y = 0, 1, \infty$, $x = y$. The function $F$ is the only solution holomorphic at $(0,0)$.

For $d$ arbitrary, the series \eqref{eq:1p} has been investigated by Lauricella \cite{Lauricella}, and Terada \cite{Terada} obtained results parallel to those of Picard \cite{Picard16a}.

The existence of differential or partial differential equations of Fuchsian type as above is to be expected if one considers \eqref{eq:3pp} as a period integral. The equations are satisfied not only by $F$ but also by any integral of the same integrand taken from $g$ to $h$ where $g$ and $h$ are in $\{\infty, 0, 1, x_2, \ldots, x_{d+1}\}$. There are $d+1$ linearly independent such integrals, and, following Schwarz, it is natural to take them as the projective coordinates of a point in projective $d$-space $\PP^d$. This yields a map
\begin{equation*}\tag{$4''$}
\widetilde{Q} \to \PP^d
\end{equation*}
where $\widetilde{Q}$ is the universal covering of the space $Q \subset (\PP^1)^d$ defined as
\[
Q = \{(x_i) \mid x_i \neq 0, 1, \infty \text{ and } x_i \neq x_j \text{ for } i \neq j\}.
\]
The action of $\pi_1(Q)$ on $\PP^d$ is called the monodromy action. The map $(4'')$ is $\pi_1(Q)$-equivariant. Let $\Gamma$ denote the image of $\pi_1(Q)$ in $\PGL(d+1, \CC)$. In \cite{Picard16a} and \cite{Picard16b} Picard gives a criterion for the multivalued map $w : \widetilde{Q} \to \PP^d$ ($d = 2$) defined by $(4'')$ to invert as a univalued map from a ball in $\PP^d$ to a partial compactification of $Q$. When this happens, $\Gamma$ is discrete in a $\PU(1,d)$ subgroup of $\PGL(d+1, \CC)$.

As pointed out in \cite{DM} the proof of discreteness of $\Gamma$ that Picard sketches in \cite{Picard16a} leads to an obstacle and is inadequate. Our first objective in this paper is to give a correct proof for $d = 2$ and also for general $d$. In order to carry this out in modern concepts, we need only deal with rational $\mu_i$ and multivalued integrands on $\PP^1$ which are single valued on a finite ramified covering of $\PP^1$. However, in order to provide a framework for dealing with arbitrary parameters $\mu_i$, we have introduced local systems on $\PP^1 - \{\infty, 0, 1, x_2, \ldots, x_{d+1}\}$. This necessitates an \textit{ab ovo} development of holomorphic cohomology in order to validate the corresponding Hodge decomposition. In the end, this viewpoint does have the advantage of directness.

The two main theorems of this paper are Theorems (11.4) and (12.11). The first says in effect: If $0 < \mu_i < 1$ for all $i$ ($0 \leq i \leq d+1$ or $i = \infty$) and $(1 - \mu_i - \mu_j)^{-1}$ is an integer for all $i \neq j$ such that $\mu_i + \mu_j < 1$, then the monodromy group of the hypergeometric function \eqref{eq:3pp} is a lattice in the projective unitary group $\PU(1,d)$, i.e.\ is discrete and of finite covolume.

Theorem (12.11) combined with (12.9) says in effect: For each non-arithmetic lattice arising in Theorem (11.4), there is an algebraic family defined over $\overline{\QQ}$ of algebraic curves $X$ whose monodromy group is a subgroup of $\Aut H^1(X_0, \ZZ)$ which is not of finite covolume in its Zariski-closure.

The example 15 of \S (14.3) corresponds for instance to the family of curves, depending on the parameters $x, y$, with equation
\[
U_1^{19} = U_0^6 (U-1)^5 (U-x)^4 (U-y)^4
\]
(a cyclic covering of order 19 of $\PP^1$).

The integrality condition that $(1 - \mu_i - \mu_j)^{-1}$ be an integer ensures that the key map of this paper is \\'etale in codimension one. A model situation in which integrality is used in such a way is the following. Let $D$ be the unit disc, $D^* := D - \{0\}$, and $\zeta = r/s$ a rational number $> 0$, written as a reduced fraction. Let $\widetilde{D^*}$ be the finite covering of $D^*$ on which the multivalued function $z \mapsto z^\zeta$ is defined. We complete it to the ramified covering $\widetilde{D} = \widetilde{D^*} \cup \{0\}$ of $D$. The multivalued function $z^{1/s}$ on $D^*$ ``is'' a uniformizing parameter for $\widetilde{D}$ at $0$. The map $z \mapsto z^\zeta$ from $\widetilde{D}$ to $D$ is \\'etale at $0$ (i.e.\ \\'etale in codimension 1) if and only if $\zeta^{-1}$ is an integer. Indeed $z^\zeta = (z^{1/s})^r$. For a description of how this enters the proof, we refer to the comments after (3.11).

The lists in Section 14 provide examples of non-arithmetic lattices in $\PU(1,d)$ for $d = 2, 3$---both cocompact and non-cocompact for $d = 2$. For $d > 5$, Theorem (11.4) yields no lattice at all, by (14.2).

The case $d = 1$ is treated in (12.5.5) and (12.6.3). The lattices arising from Theorem (11.4) in this case are the triangle groups $(p, q, r)$ generated by rotations through double the angles with centers the vertex of a geodesic triangle in the Poincar\\'e disc, with angles $\pi/p$, $\pi/q$, $\pi/r$ when $p, q, r$ are positive integers (or $\infty$) satisfying
\[
\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} + \frac{1}{r} \leq 1.
\]
These lattices are described in Fricke-Klein \cite{FrickeKlein} and are infinite in number.

However, the number of arithmetic lattices arising is finite. An explicit list can be deduced from results of Takeuchi on arithmetic triangle groups \cite{Takeuchi}.

In the final Section 15, we make some comments about the list of 102 solutions of R. Levavasseur in \cite{LeVavasseur} to Picard's integrality conditions for discreteness in the case $d = 2$.

In the paper ``Generalized Picard lattices arising from half-integral conditions'', printed after this one, the second named author is able to relax the integrality condition on $(1 - \mu_i - \mu_j)^{-1}$ and obtains lattices for $d < 9$ \cite{MostowII}.

\section{Cohomology of a rank one local system on a punctured projective line}

\subsection{}
Let us start with the following data:
\begin{itemize}
\item[$\bullet$] $P$: a complex projective line;
\item[$\bullet$] $N$: an integer $\geq 0$;
\item[$\bullet$] $S$: a set of $N$ points of $P$;
\item[$\bullet$] $\alpha = (\alpha_s)_{s \in S}$: a family of complex numbers indexed by $S$, satisfying the condition $\prod_{s \in S} \alpha_s = 1$.
\end{itemize}
We will be mainly interested in the case when $N > 3$ and none of the $\alpha_s$ is $1$.

If a base point $o \in P - S$ is given, the functor ``fibre at $o$'' is an equivalence of the category of complex local systems (= flat vector bundles) on $P - S$ with that of complex vector spaces provided with an action of $\pi_1(P-S, o)$. In particular, rank one local systems correspond to homomorphisms $\HH_1(P-S) = \pi_1(P-S, o)^{\mathrm{ab}} \to \CC^*$. The group $\HH_1$ is generated by small positive loops $\gamma_s$ around each $s \in S$, with the relation $\prod \gamma_s = 1$ as the only relation. Up to isomorphism, there is hence a unique one dimensional complex local system $L$ on $P-S$ such that the monodromy of $L$ around each $s \in S$ is multiplication by $\alpha_s$, or, as we will say, of monodromy $\alpha$. By definition, if $v_0$ is in the fibre of $L$ at a point $x$ near $s \in S$, and if we let $x$ turn counter clockwise once around $s$, and push $v_0$ horizontally along the path of $x$, then when coming back, $v_0$ becomes $\alpha_s \cdot v_0$. In other words, in term of a local coordinate $z$ centered at $s$, and of a multivalued section $e(z)$ of $L$ defined in a neighborhood of $s$, one has
\[
e(\exp(2\pi i u) \cdot z)_{u=1} = \alpha_s \cdot e(\exp(2\pi i u) \cdot z)_{u=0}.
\]
The complex local system $L$ has automorphisms: $\Aut(L) = \CC^*$, with $\nu \in \CC^*$ corresponding to multiplication by the scalar $\nu$. Because of them, even though the isomorphism class of $L$ is uniquely determined by the $\alpha_s$, $L$ is not determined up to unique isomorphism.

Let us fix one $L$. In (2.2)--(2.20), we will review some of the descriptions of the cohomology of $P-S$ with coefficients in $L$.

\subsection{Combinatorial description}
Let us fix a triangulation $\mathscr{T}$ of $P-S$. One can then identify $H^*(P-S, L)$ with the cohomology of the complex of $L$-valued cochains of $\mathscr{T}$: cochains $c$ for which the value of $c$ on an oriented simplex $\sigma$ is a horizontal section of $L$ on $\sigma$ (thus $c(\sigma) \in H^0(\sigma, L)$). To make sense of the formula $(dc)(\tau) = c(\partial \tau)$, one uses that a horizontal section of $L$ on a face of $\tau$ extends uniquely as a horizontal section on $\tau$. This complex is the dual of the complex of chains of $\mathscr{T}$ with coefficients in the dual local system $L^\vee$: chains $C$ which are finite sums $\sum c_\sigma \cdot \sigma$ with $c_\sigma \in H^0(\sigma, L^\vee)$ and $\sigma$ an oriented simplex of $\mathscr{T}$. The sign rule $\langle dc, C \rangle = -\langle c, \partial C \rangle$ is however unusual for duality of complexes. We write $H_*(P-S, L^\vee)$ for the corresponding homology.

The cohomology with compact support $H^*_c(P-S, L)$ is the cohomology of the complex of compactly supported $L$-valued cochains of $\mathscr{T}$. The dual complex is the complex of locally finite chains with coefficient in $L^\vee$: chains $C$ which are possibly infinite ``sums'' $\sum c_\sigma \cdot \sigma$. We write $H^{\mathrm{lf}}_*(P-S, L^\vee)$ for the corresponding homology.

Let $X$ be obtained from $P$ by deleting a small open disc around each $s \in S$. ``Small'' means ``small enough''. What matters here is that the closure of the discs be disjoint. A homotopy argument shows that ($\mathring{X}$ denoting $X - \partial X$)
\[
H^*(P-S, L) \xrightarrow{\sim} H^*(X, L) \xrightarrow{\sim} H^*(\mathring{X}, L)
\]
and that $H^*(X \bmod \partial X, L) \xrightarrow{\sim} H^*_c(\mathring{X}, L) \xrightarrow{\sim} H^*_c(P-S, L)$.

To compute cohomology, one can hence use a triangulation of $X$, instead of one of $P-S$. As $X$ is compact, the triangulation is finite, and this combinatorial description makes clear that the Euler-Poincar\\'e characteristic
\[
\chi(P-S, L) := \sum (-1)^i \dim H^i(P-S, L)
\]
(resp.\ $\chi_c(P-S, L) := \sum (-1)^i \dim H^i_c(P-S, L)$) is independent of $L$. Each $H^i$ (resp.\ $H^i_c$) is indeed expressed as the $i$-th cohomology group of a finite dimensional chain complex $K$, and $\dim K^i$ is independent of $L$. In the case of cohomology with compact support, the additivity of $\chi_c$ (deduced from the long exact sequence $\cdots \to H^i_c(P-S, \CC) \to H^i(P, \CC) \to H^i(S, \CC) \to \cdots$) gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.2.1}
\chi_c(P-S, L) = \chi_c(P-S, \CC) = \chi_c(P) - \chi_c(S) = 2 - N.
\end{equation}
For an algebraic variety $Y$, one always has $\chi_c(Y) = \chi(Y)$. In the case at hand ($Y = P-S$), this can be deduced from Poincar\\'e duality. One gets
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.2.2}
\chi(P-S, L) = 2 - N.
\end{equation}

\subsection{De Rham ($C^\infty$) description}
$H^*(P-S, L)$ is the cohomology of the de Rham complex of $L$-valued $C^\infty$ differential forms on $P-S$, and $H^*_c(P-S, L)$ that of the subcomplex of compactly supported forms. If the triangulation $\mathscr{T}$ of (2.2) is smooth, integration: $\omega \mapsto c$, $c(\sigma) = \int_\sigma \omega$ is defined. It is a map of complexes from the de Rham complex to the cochain complex (both with or without support condition), inducing an isomorphism on cohomology.

The Poincar\\'e duality pairing obtained by integration on $P-S$: $\alpha, \beta \mapsto \int_{P-S} \alpha \wedge \beta$ induces a perfect pairing
\[
H^i(P-S, L) \otimes H^{2-i}_c(P-S, L^\vee) \to \CC,
\]
the composite of cup product with value in $H^2_c(P-S, \CC)$, and of the trace, or integration, map: $H^2_c(P-S, \CC) \to \CC$.

If at least one of the $\alpha_s$ is not $1$, there can be no global horizontal section: $H^0(P-S, L) = 0$, and a fortiori none with compact support: $H^0_c(P-S, L) = 0$ (for this, $i = 0$ suffices). The same applies to the dual $L^\vee$ of $L$, with monodromy $\alpha^{-1}$, and Poincar\\'e duality gives vanishing for $H^2$ and $H^2_c$. The Euler-Poincar\\'e characteristic being known (\eqref{eq:2.2.1} and \eqref{eq:2.2.2}), we get
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:2.3}
If $\alpha_s \neq 1$ for at least one $s$, then $H^i(P-S, L)$ and $H^i_c(P-S, L)$ vanish for $i \neq 1$, and
\[
\dim H^1(P-S, L) = \dim H^1_c(P-S, L) = N - 2.
\]
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Currents}
Instead of using the $C^\infty$ de Rham complex, consisting of forms written in local coordinates as $\sum_i f_I \, dx_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{i_k} \cdot e$, with $e$ an horizontal section of $L$ and $f$ a $C^\infty$ function, one can as well use the complex of currents, where $f$ is allowed to be a generalized function (distribution). This complex can be used to express Poincar\\'e duality as a cap-product isomorphism between homology and cohomology: for $C$ a $L^\vee$-valued chain, there is a unique current $\delta(C)$ such that $\int_C \omega = \int \delta(C) \wedge \omega$; one has $d\delta(C) = (-1)^k \delta(\partial C)$, and the map $C \mapsto \delta(C)$ provides isomorphisms
\[
H_k(P-S, L^\vee) \xrightarrow{\sim} H^{2-k}_c(P-S, L) \quad \text{and} \quad H^{\mathrm{lf}}_k(P-S, L^\vee) \xrightarrow{\sim} H^{2-k}(P-S, L).
\]
It is often more convenient to use currents than the chains of a fixed triangulation.

If $\beta$ is a rectifiable proper map from an open, semi-open or closed interval $I$ to $P-S$, and for $e \in H^0(I, \beta^*L^\vee)$, we let $(e \cdot \beta)$ be the $L^\vee$-valued current for which
\[
\int (e \cdot \beta) \wedge \omega = \int_I \langle e, \beta^*\omega \rangle.
\]
If $\beta: [0,1] \to P$ maps $0$ and $1$ to $S$, and $]0,1[$ to $P-S$, then for $e \in H^0(]0,1[, \beta^*L^\vee)$, $e \cdot \beta$ is a cycle and as such defines an homology class in $H^{\mathrm{lf}}_1(P-S, L^\vee)$. We will use such cycles to construct convenient bases of $H^{\mathrm{lf}}_1(P-S, L^\vee)$.

\subsection{}
Assume the following is given:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
\item a partition of $S$ into two subsets $S_1$ and $S_2$;
\item trees $T_1$ and $T_2$ (a tree is a contractible CW complex of dimension $\leq 1$), and an embedding $\beta: T_1 \amalg T_2 \hookrightarrow P$, mapping the set of vertices of $T_1$ (resp.\ $T_2$) onto $S_1$ (resp.\ $S_2$);
\item for each (open) edge $a$ of $T_1$ or $T_2$, an orientation of $a$, and $t(a) \in H^0(a, \beta^*L^\vee)$.
\end{enumerate}
For each edge $a$, $t(a) \cdot \beta|_a$ is then a locally finite cycle on $P-S$, with coefficient in $L$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:2.5.1}
If $\prod_{s \in S_i} \alpha_s \neq 1$, the elements $t(a) \cdot \beta|_a$, for $a$ an edge of $T_1$ or $T_2$, form a basis of $H^{\mathrm{lf}}_1(P-S, L^\vee)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $T'_i$ be the disjoint union of the (open) edges of $T_i$. We have $(P-S) - \beta(T'_1 \amalg T'_2) = P - \beta(T_1 \amalg T_2)$, hence a long exact sequence
\[
\cdots \to H^{\mathrm{lf}}_k(T'_1 \amalg T'_2, \beta^*L^\vee) \to H^{\mathrm{lf}}_k(P-S, L) \to H_k(P - \beta(T_1 \amalg T_2), L^\vee) \to \cdots
\]
The group $H^{\mathrm{lf}}_1(T'_1 \amalg T'_2, \beta^*L^\vee)$ has as basis the $t(a) \cdot a$. It remains to be proved that $H^{\mathrm{lf}}_1(P - \beta(T_1 \amalg T_2), L) = 0$.

The space $P - \beta(T_1) - \beta(T_2)$ is the complement of two disjoint trees in $S^2$. It is essentially an annulus. The monodromy of ``turning around $T_1$'' is the product of the $\alpha_s$ ($s \in S_1$). In $P-S$, a loop around $T_1$ can indeed be homotoped to a product of loops around each $s \in S_1$.

The group $H^{\mathrm{lf}}_1(P - \beta(T_1 \amalg T_2), L^\vee)$ is dual to $H^1_c(P - \beta(T_1 \amalg T_2), L)$, which is the Poincar\\'e dual of $H^{2-1}(P - \beta(T_1 \amalg T_2), L^\vee)$. One can homotope $P - \beta(T_1 \amalg T_2)$ to $S^1$, and the group becomes $H^{2-1}(S^1, L^\vee)$, for $L^\vee$ a non trivial local system on $S^1$. It remains to use the
\begin{lemma}
If $L$ is a non trivial rank one local system on $S^1$, then $H^*(S^1, L) = 0$.
\end{lemma}
The $H^0$ is $0$ by non triviality, and $\chi(S^1, L) = \chi(S^1) = 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
As $T_1 \amalg T_2$ has $N-2$ edges, (2.5.2) reproves that $\dim H^1_c(P-S, L) = N-2$ when the $\alpha_s$ are not all $1$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Sheaf cohomology}
We will always identify a local system $L$ with its sheaf of locally constant sections. Let $j$ denote the inclusion of $P-S$ in $P$. Then, $H^*_c(P-S, L)$ is the cohomology of $P$ with coefficients in $j_!L$, the extension of $L$ by $0$. It is the hypercohomology on $P$ of any complex of sheaves $K$ with $\mathscr{H}^0(K) = j_!L$ and $\mathscr{H}^i(K) = 0$ for $i \neq 0$. On the other hand, if $L^*$ is any resolution of $L$ whose components are acyclic for $j_*$ ($R^qj_*L^k = 0$ for $q > 0$), then $H^*(P-S, L)$ is the hypercohomology on $P$ of $j_*L^*$. To prove it, one reduces to the case where $L^*$ (and hence $j_*L^*$) is soft, one has $\Gamma(P-S, L^*) = \Gamma(P, j_*L^*)$, and
\[
H^*(P-S, L) := H^*\Gamma(P-S, L^*) = H^*\Gamma(P, j_*L^*) =: H^*(P, j_*L^*).
\]

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:2.6.1}
If all $\alpha_s$ are different from $1$, then
\[
H^*_c(P-S, L) \xrightarrow{\sim} H^*(P-S, L).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $L^*$ be a soft resolution of $L$, for instance the $L$-valued $C^\infty$ de Rham complex of sheaves. It suffices to check that $j_!L^*$ is a resolution of $j_!L$, and this results from the local fact that for $D$ any small disc around $s \in S$, one has $H^*\Gamma(D-s, L^*) = H^*(D-s, L) = 0$. To check this vanishing, one can replace $D-s$ by a homotopic $S^1$ and apply (2.5.2).
\end{proof}

\subsection{The de Rham (holomorphic) description}
The holomorphic $L$-valued de Rham complex $\Omega^*(L): \mathcal{O}(L) \to \Omega^1(L)$ is a resolution of $L$ on $P-S$. One can hence interpret $H^*(P-S, L)$ as the hypercohomology on $P-S$ of $\Omega^*(L)$. As $S \neq \emptyset$, $P-S$ is Stein, hence $H^q(P-S, \Omega^p(L)) = 0$ for $q > 0$, and this gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.7.1}
H^*(P-S, L) = H^*\Gamma(P-S, \Omega^*(L)).
\end{equation}
Similarly, $R^qj_*\Omega^p(L) = 0$ for $q > 0$ hence
\[
H^*(P-S, L) = H^*(P, j_*\Omega^*(L))
\]
(hypercohomology of $P$, with coefficients in the complex $j_*\Omega^*(L)$; see (2.6)).

After the preliminaries (2.8), (2.9), we will show how to replace $j_*\Omega^*(L)$ by smaller complexes of sheaves.

\subsection{}
We will often describe a section of $\mathcal{O}(L)$ on a connected open set $U$ as the product of a multivalued function with a multivalued section of $L$. This has the following meaning: $U$ should be provided with a base point $0$, and a multivalued section of a sheaf $\mathscr{F}$ ($\mathcal{O}$, or $L$, $\ldots$) is a section of the pull back of $\mathscr{F}$ on the universal covering $(\widetilde{U}, 0)$ of $(U, 0)$. Products are taken on $\widetilde{U}$. A multivalued section, which is the pull-back of a section on $U$, is then identified with that section. The role of the base point can be played by a contractible subset of $U$. A section of $L$ at $0$ extends uniquely to a multivalued section and will be denoted by the same symbol. A multivalued section of $\mathcal{O}$ is determined by its germ at $0$, and will be denoted by the same symbol. As multivalued sections of $\mathcal{O}$, we will mainly use products $\prod g^{\mu_i}$. When $g$ is not real and negative, the principal determination of $g^{\mu}$ is $\exp(2\pi i \mu \log g)$, with $|\operatorname{Im} \log g| < \pi$. As a rule, $g^{\mu}$ will denote the multivalued function whose germ at the base contractible set is the principal determination when $g^{\mu}$ is not real and negative on it. When the determination used is irrelevant, we will not take the trouble of specifying which one we mean.

\subsection{}
Fix $s \in S$ and let $z$ be a local coordinate, which we take to be an isomorphism, carrying $s$ to $0$, of a small neighborhood $D$ of $s$ with a disc in $\CC$ centered at $0$. If $\mu \in \CC$ is such that $\alpha_s = \exp(2\pi i \mu)$, the monodromy of $z^{-\mu}$ around $s$ is the inverse of that of a horizontal section of $L$. Any section $u$ of $\mathcal{O}(L)$ (resp.\ $\Omega^1(L)$) on $D^*$ can hence be written $u = z^{-\mu} \cdot e \cdot f$ (resp.\ $u = z^{-\mu} \cdot e \cdot f \, dz$) with $e$ a non zero multivalued section of $L$ and $f$ a holomorphic function on $D^*$. We define $u$ to be meromorphic at $s$ iff $f$ is, and we define its valuation by
\[
v_s(u) = v_0(f).
\]
These definitions are independent of the choices of local coordinate and of $\mu$.

\subsection{The de Rham (meromorphic at $S$) description}
Let us write $j_*^m \Omega^*(L)$ for the subcomplex of $j_*\Omega^*(L)$ of meromorphic forms. A local computation around each $s \in S$ shows that the inclusion of $j_*^m \Omega^*(L)$ in $j_*\Omega^*(L)$ induces an isomorphism on the cohomology sheaves. This implies that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.10.1}
H^*(P, j_*^m\Omega^*(L)) \xrightarrow{\sim} H^*(P, j_*\Omega^*(L)) = H^*(P-S, L).
\end{equation}
It follows from (2.11) below that $j_*^m \Omega^p(L)$ is an inductive limit of line bundles whose degrees tends to $\infty$. From this one concludes first that $H^q(P, j_*^m \Omega^p(L)) = 0$ for $q > 0$, and then that $H^*(P, j_*^m \Omega^*(L))$ is simply $H^*\Gamma(P, j_*^m \Omega^*(L))$, the cohomology of the complex of $L$-valued forms holomorphic on $P-S$ and meromorphic at each $s \in S$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.10.2}
H^*(P-S, L) = H^*\Gamma(P, j_*^m \Omega^*(L)).
\end{equation}

\subsection{}
Let $(\mu_s)_{s \in S}$ be a family of complex numbers, such that $\exp(2\pi i \mu_s) = \alpha_s$. We define the line bundle $\mathcal{O}(\sum \mu_s \cdot s)(L)$ as the subsheaf of $j_*^m \mathcal{O}(L)$ whose local holomorphic sections are the local sections $u$ of $j_*^m \mathcal{O}(L)$ such that for $s \in S$, the integer $v_s(u) + \mu_s$ is $> 0$ (in short: $v_s(u) \geq -\mu_s$). With the local coordinate notation (2.9), $z^{-\mu_s} \cdot e$ determines an invertible section of $\mathcal{O}(\sum \mu_s \cdot s)(L)$ near $s \in S$. The degree of a meromorphic section $u$ of a line bundle $\mathscr{L}$ at a point $x$ is the order of the zero (or minus the order of the pole) of $u$ at $x$; for $z$ a local coordinate centered at $x$, it is the supremum of the integers $n$ such that $z^{-n}u$ is a holomorphic section of $\mathscr{L}$ at $x$. The degree of $\mathscr{L}$ is the sum of the degrees at all points of any non zero meromorphic section of $\mathscr{L}$. If $u$ is a meromorphic section of $\mathcal{O}(\sum \mu_s \cdot s)(L)$ and $x \notin S$, one has $\deg_x(u) = v_x(u)$. If one defines $\mu_x = 0$ for $x \in P-S$, the same holds at any $x \in P$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:2.11.1}
The line bundle $\mathcal{O}(\sum \mu_s \cdot s)(L)$ is of degree $\sum \mu_s$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We may assume $P = \PP^1$ and $\infty \notin S$. Let $e$ be a multivalued horizontal section of $L$. The product
\[
u = \prod_{s \in S} (z-s)^{-\mu_s} \cdot e
\]
is an invertible section of $\mathcal{O}(\sum \mu_s \cdot s)(L)$ on the affine line. At $\infty$, $u$ is meromorphic, of valuation $\sum \mu_s$. The degree of $\mathcal{O}(\sum \mu_s \cdot s)(L)$, equal to the sum at all $x \in P$ of the degree of any non zero meromorphic section, is hence $\sum \mu_s$.
\end{proof}

We define $\Omega^1(\sum \mu_s \cdot s)(L) := \mathcal{O}(\sum \mu_s \cdot s)(L) \otimes \Omega^1$. It results from (2.11.1) that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.11.2}
\deg \Omega^1(\sum \mu_s \cdot s)(L) = -2 + \sum \mu_s.
\end{equation}
In particular, if $\sum \mu_s = 2$, the line bundle $\Omega^1(\sum \mu_s \cdot s)(L)$ is of degree $0$, hence isomorphic to $\mathcal{O}$, the trivial line bundle.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:2.12}
If $\sum \mu_s = 2$, there is up to a constant factor one and only one non zero form $\omega \in \Gamma(P, j_*^m \Omega^1(L))$ whose valuation at $s \in S$ is $\geq -\mu_s$. One has $v_s(\omega) = -\mu_s$, and $\omega$ is invertible on $P-S$.
\end{corollary}

For $P = \PP^1$, $\infty \notin S$, and $e$ a multivalued horizontal section of $L$, one has (up to a factor) $\omega = \prod_{s \in S} (z-s)^{-\mu_s} \cdot e \, dz$. If $\infty \in S$, one has $\omega = \prod_{s \in S - \{\infty\}} (z-s)^{-\mu_s} \cdot e \, dz$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:2.13}
Assume that $\sum \mu_s = 2$, $N \geq 3$, and that none of the $\alpha_s$ is $1$ (i.e.\ that none of the $\mu_s$ is an integer). Then the cohomology class of the form $\omega$ of (2.12) is not zero.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
One has $\sum v_s(\omega) = \sum -\mu_s = -2 > 1-N$. The proposition hence results from the
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:2.14}
Assume that none of the $\alpha_s$ is $1$. If a non zero form $\omega$ is such that $\sum v_s(\omega) \geq 1 - N$, its cohomology class is not zero.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By (2.10.2), it suffices to verify that the equation $\omega = du$ has no solution $u \in \Gamma(P, j_*^m \mathcal{O}(L))$.

For any local section $u$ of $j_*^m \mathcal{O}(L)$ near $s$, one has $v_s(du) > v_s(u) - 1$, with equality if $v_s(u)$ is not $0$, or if $v_s(du)$ is not a positive (meaning $> 0$) integer. The integer case being excluded, a solution $u$ would be a section of $\mathcal{O}(-\sum (v_s(\omega)+1)s)(L)$, a line bundle of degree $-\sum(v_s(\omega)+1) \leq -1$, a contradiction.
\end{proof}
\end{proof}

\subsection{}
Here is how (2.13), (2.14) has to be modified for integral $\mu$'s. As the result will not be needed, except for some historical comments in \S 15, we will be sketchy.

(2.15.1) When some $\alpha_s$ are $1$, (2.6.1) is not true, one has to distinguish between $H^1_c$ and $H^1$, and even to introduce some intermediate groups: for $S' \subset S$, the family $\Phi(S')$ of subsets of $P-S$ closed in $P-S'$ is a family of supports, and $H^*_{\Phi(S')}(P-S, L)$ is defined. For $j'$ the inclusion of $P-S$ into $P-S'$, it is $H^*(P-S', j'_!L)$. For $T \subset S$, define $T(1) := \{s \in T \mid \alpha_s = 1\}$. The proof of (2.6.1) shows that
\[
H^*_{\Phi(S')}(P-S, L) \xrightarrow{\sim} H^*_{|S'|}(P-S, L).
\]
These groups are also the hypercohomology groups, on $P$, of the subcomplex of $j_*^m \Omega^*(L)$ consisting of the $u$ in $j_*^m \mathcal{O}(L)$ and of the $\omega$ in $j_*^m \Omega^1(L)$ such that $v_s(\omega) > 0$ and $v_s(u) > 0$ for $s \in (S-S')(1)$. If the $\mu_s$ are such that $\alpha_s = \exp(2\pi i \mu_s)$, and that $S'(1)$ is the set of $s$ for which $\mu_s$ is integral $> 0$, the groups $H_{\Phi(S')}(P-S, L)$ are also the hypercohomology groups of the sub-complex $\mathcal{O}(\sum(\mu_s-1)s)(L) \to \Omega^1(\sum \mu_s \cdot s)(L)$ of $j_*^m \Omega^*(L)$.

(2.15.2) If $\omega \in \Gamma(P, j_*^m \Omega^1(L))$, and if all $s$ with $\alpha_s = 1$ and $v_s(\omega) < 0$ are in $S'$, then $\omega$ defines a cohomology class in $H^*_{\Phi(S')}(P-S, L)$. If in addition $S'$ contains none of the $s$ for which simultaneously $\alpha_s = 1$ and $v_s(\omega) \geq 0$, and if further $\sum v_s(\omega) > 1-N$ (which amounts to $\omega$ having at most $N-3$ zeros, counted with multiplicities, on $P-S$), the proof of (2.14) still shows that the class of $\omega$ in $H^*_{\Phi(S')}(P-S, L)$ is not zero. This applies to the form $\omega$ of (2.12), with $S' = S$, provided $N > 3$ and $\mu_s > 0$ whenever $\mu_s \in \ZZ$.

\begin{remark}\label{rmk:2.16}
Let us assume that none of the $\alpha_s$ is $1$. If $\omega$ is an holomorphic section of $\Omega^1(L)$ on $P-S$, by (2.6.1), the class $[\omega]$ of $\omega$ in $H^1(P-S, L)$ is the image of a unique class, again denoted $[\omega]$, in $H^1_c(P-S, L)$. For any locally finite cycle or current $C$ with coefficient in $L^\vee$, $\langle [C], [\omega] \rangle$ is defined: $H^{\mathrm{lf}}_1(P-S, L^\vee)$ is indeed paired with $H^1_c(P-S, L)$. We want to prove that
\[
\langle [C], [\omega] \rangle = \int_C \omega.
\]
This is not completely obvious, especially since the integral may be divergent. The problem is rather to understand which value the cohomological formalism attaches to the integral.

1st answer: One replaces $C$ by a finite homologous cycle $C'$, as pictured in (2.6), and $\langle [C], [\omega] \rangle = \int_{C'} \omega$.

2nd answer: If $D_s$ is a small open disk around $s \in S$, one has $H^1(D_s-s, L) = 0$ (cf.\ (2.6)). On $D_s^* := D_s - s$, $\omega$ is hence of the form $du_s$, for a unique section $u_s$ of $\mathcal{O}(L)$ on $D_s^*$. Let $\varphi_s$ be a compactly supported $C^\infty$ function on $D_s$, equal to $1$ in a neighborhood of $s$. The $C^\infty$ $1$-form $\omega - \sum d(\varphi_s u_s)$ is cohomologous to $\omega$ and is compactly supported. One has hence
\[
\langle [C], [\omega] \rangle = \int_C \omega - \sum \int_C d(\varphi_s u_s).
\]
It is sometimes more convenient to take for $\varphi_s$ the (discontinuous) characteristic function of $D_s$. The current $\omega - \sum d(\varphi_s u_s)$ is again compactly supported and cohomologous to $\omega$.

These formulae can also be used to define the integral of $\omega$ on a locally finite chain or current, which is a cycle only near each $s \in S$. Given for instance a path $\beta: [0,1] \to P$, with $\beta([0,1[)$ in $P-S$ and $\beta(1) = s \in S$, and given $e \in H^0(]0,1[, \beta^*L^\vee)$, the formula for the ``finite part'' $\int_{[0,1]} e \cdot \beta^*\omega - d(\varphi_s u_s)$ can be rewritten
\[
\mathrm{P.V.} \int_{[0,1]} \langle e, \beta^*\omega \rangle - \langle e(1-\varepsilon), \beta^*u_s(1-\varepsilon) \rangle \quad (\varepsilon \to 0).
\]
For a meromorphic $\omega$, this agrees with Hadamard's ``finite part'' of a divergent integral.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}\label{ex:2.17}
We take $P = \PP^1$, $S = \{0, 1, \infty\}$ and we assume that none of the $\mu_s$ is an integer, and that $\sum \mu_s = 2$. For $\lambda_s = 1 - \mu_s$, this means $\sum \lambda_s = 1$. We normalize $L$ by fixing a section $e$ of it on $]0, 1[$, and we let $e^\vee$ be the dual base of $L^\vee$ on $]0, 1[$. Take
\[
\omega = z^{\lambda_0-1} (1-z)^{\lambda_1-1} e \, dz;
\]
the ``principal'' determination of $z^{\lambda_0-1}$ and of $(1-z)^{\lambda_1-1}$ on $]0, 1[$ is used (cf.\ (2.8)). By (2.5.1), the homology $H^{\mathrm{lf}}_1(P-S, L^\vee)$ is one-dimensional, generated by the current $e^\vee \cdot ]0, 1[$. The content of (2.13) here is that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.17.1}
\mathrm{P.V.} \int_0^1 z^{\lambda_0-1} (1-z)^{\lambda_1-1} \, dz \neq 0.
\end{equation}
The generalization (2.15) of (2.13) can be used to extend this to the case when $\lambda_0$ and $\lambda_1$, if integers, are $> 0$, and $\lambda_\infty = 1 - \lambda_0 - \lambda_1$, if an integer, is $< 0$.

Of course, (2.17.1) is easy to deduce from the formula for the B-function in terms of $\Gamma$-functions.
\end{example}

\subsection{}
We now assume that each $\alpha_s$ is of absolute value $1$, and not equal to $1$. There is then a horizontal positive Hermitian structure $(\,,\,)$ on $L$. Let us choose one. One can view $(\,,\,)$ as a perfect pairing between $L$ and the complex conjugate local system $\overline{L}$. As such, it induces a perfect pairing
\[
\psi_0: H^1_c(P-S, L) \otimes \overline{H^1_c(P-S, L)} \to H^2_c(P-S, \CC) = \CC
\]
((2.3), (2.6.1)). The vector space $\overline{H^1_c(P-S, L)}$ is the complex conjugate of $H^1_c(P-S, L)$, and $\psi_0(u, \overline{v})$ defines a non degenerate skew-Hermitian form $\psi$ on $H^1_c(P-S, L)$. Anticommutativity of the cup product shows that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.18.1}
\psi(v, u) = -\overline{\psi(u, v)}, \quad \text{i.e.}\quad \psi(u, v) = -\overline{\psi(v, u)}.
\end{equation}
Writing $(u, v) := \frac{1}{2\pi i} \psi(u, v) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \psi_0(u, \overline{v})$, we have a Hermitian form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.18.2}
(u, v) = \overline{(v, u)}.
\end{equation}

A section $\omega$ of $j_*^m \Omega^1(L)$ is said to be of the \emph{first kind} if $v_s(\omega) > -1$ for each $s \in S$. Those are the forms for which the integral $\int_{P-S} \omega \wedge \overline{\omega}$ is convergent. The integrand is defined as follows: if, locally, $\omega_i = e_i \cdot f_i$ ($i = 1, 2$, $f_i$ a $1$-form, $e_i$ a section of $L$), then $\omega_1 \wedge \overline{\omega_2}$ is the $(1,1)$-form $(e_1, e_2) \, f_1 \wedge \overline{f_2}$.

We define $H^{1,0}(P-S, L)$ as the vector space of forms of the first kind in $\Gamma(P, j_*^m \Omega^1(L))$, and $H^{0,1}(P-S, L)$ as the complex conjugate of $H^{1,0}(P-S, L)$. It can be viewed as the space of antiholomorphic $L$-valued forms, whose complex conjugate is of the first kind. Such forms also define cohomology classes.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:2.19}
If $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ are each either in $H^{1,0}(P-S, L)$ or in $\overline{H^{0,1}(P-S, L)}$, one has, for the corresponding cohomology classes $[\omega_1]$ and $[\omega_2]$,
\[
\psi([\omega_1], [\omega_2]) = \int_{P-S} \omega_1 \wedge \omega_2.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let us assume, for definiteness, that $\omega_1$ is in $H^{1,0}(P-S, L)$. Choose $D_s$, $(u_s)_{s \in S}$, as in (2.16), and let $\varphi_s$ be the characteristic function of $D_s$. It follows from (2.16) that the class of $\omega_1$ in $H^1_c(P-S, L)$ is also the class of the compactly supported current $\omega_1 - \sum d(\varphi_s u_s)$. Hence (2.3)
\[
\psi([\omega_1], [\omega_2]) = \psi_0([\omega_1], \overline{[\omega_2]}) = \int_{P-S} (\omega_1 - \sum d(\varphi_s u_s)) \wedge \omega_2.
\]
When we let the $D_s$ shrink, the first term converges to $\int_{P-S} \omega_1 \wedge \omega_2$. We want to show that all others tend to $0$. When the radius $r$ of $D_s$ tends to zero, the size of $u_s$ on $\partial D_s$ is $O(r^{v_s(u_s)+\varepsilon})$. One has $v_s(u_s) = v_s(\omega_1)+1$. The forms $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ being of the first kind, the size is $O(r^\beta)$, with $\beta > -1$. On the other hand, the length of $\partial D_s$ is $O(r)$; the boundary term is hence $O(r^{\beta+1})$ and tends to zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:2.20}
Under the assumption (2.18) that $|\alpha_s| = 1$, $\alpha_s \neq 1$ for $s \in S$, the natural map
\[
H^{1,0}(P-S, L) \oplus \overline{H^{0,1}(P-S, L)} \to H^1(P-S, L) = H^1_c(P-S, L)
\]
is an isomorphism. The Hermitian form $(\,,\,)$ is positive definite on $H^{1,0}$, negative definite on $H^{0,1}$ and the decomposition is orthogonal.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $\omega$ is in $H^{1,0}(P-S, L)$ (resp.\ $\overline{H^{0,1}(P-S, L)}$), the integrand $\frac{1}{2\pi i} \, \omega \wedge \overline{\omega}$ is $> 0$ (resp.\ $< 0$), and vanishes only for $\omega = 0$. If $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ are one in $H^{1,0}$ and the other in $H^{0,1}$, the integrand $\frac{1}{2\pi i} \, \omega_1 \wedge \overline{\omega_2}$ vanishes. This implies that $H^{1,0} \oplus H^{0,1}$ injects into $H^1(P-S, L)$. It remains only to prove the surjectivity of $H^{1,0} \oplus H^{0,1} \to H^1$.

We will check surjectivity by counting dimensions. Let $\mu_s$ be the number between $0$ and $1$ such that $\alpha_s = \exp(2\pi i \mu_s)$. From the definitions it follows that $H^{1,0}(P-S, L) = \Gamma(P, \Omega^1(\sum \mu_s \cdot s)(L))$. By (2.8), $\deg \Omega^1(\sum \mu_s \cdot s)(L) = -2 + \sum \mu_s$. It is an integer $> -2$ and hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.20.1}
\dim H^{1,0}(P-S, L) = \deg \Omega^1(\sum \mu_s \cdot s)(L) + 1 = -1 + \sum \mu_s.
\end{equation}
Let us apply this to $\overline{L}$. One has to replace $\alpha_s$ by $\overline{\alpha}_s = \alpha_s^{-1}$ and $\mu_s$ by $1-\mu_s$. One gets
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.20.2}
\dim H^{0,1}(P-S, L) = -1 + \sum (1-\mu_s).
\end{equation}
The sum of the dimensions is $-2 + N = N-2$, as required (2.3.1).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:2.21}
Notation being as above ($\alpha_s = \exp(2\pi i \mu_s)$ with $0 < \mu_s < 1$), the signature of the Hermitian form $(\,,\,)$ on $H^1(P-S, L)$ is $(\sum \mu_s - 1, \sum(1-\mu_s) - 1)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}\label{rmk:2.22}
(2.20) is a special case of Hodge theory for the cohomology of a curve with values in a polarized variation of Hodge structures: a local system, provided with an horizontal positive Hermitian structure, can be viewed as a polarized variation of type $(0,0)$. The artificial counting argument above enabled us to shortcut the general theory, for which the reader may consult S. Zucker \cite{Zucker}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{}
In our applications, the $\alpha_s$ will be roots of unity. When this is the case, (2.20) can also be deduced from the Hodge theory of suitable coverings of $P$. Let $X$ be an irreducible abelian covering of $P$, with covering group $G$, ramified only at $S$. If, for definiteness, we take $P = \PP^1$ and $\infty \notin S$, this means that the function field $\CC(X)$ of $X$ is a subextension of the extension $\CC(\PP^1)((z-s)_{s \in S - \{\infty\}})^{1/d}$ of $\CC(z) = \CC(\PP^1)$, for suitable $d$.

Let $\pi$ denote the projection of $X$ onto $P$. The Galois group $G = \Aut(X/P)$ acts (by transport of structure) on $\pi_*\CC$ (by abuse of notation, we also denote by $\CC$ the constant sheaf with fiber $\CC$), and at a point $z \in P$ the representation of $G$ on $(\pi_*\CC)_z = H^0(\pi^{-1}(z), \CC)$ is a regular representation of $G$. For each character $\chi$ of $G$, let $L_\chi$ be the subsheaf of $\pi_*\CC$ on which $G$ acts by $\chi$. One has
\[
\pi_*\CC = \bigoplus_\chi L_\chi
\]
and, outside of $S$, $L_\chi$ is a rank one local system.

Let $g_s \in G$ be the natural generator of the inertial (= decomposition) group at $s$: if $x(t)$ ($0 < t < 1$) is a path in $X$, such that $\pi x(t)$ in $P$ stays near $s$ in $P-S$, and turn once around $s$, then $x(1) = g_s \, x(0)$. Let $d_s$ be the order of $g_s$. If $t$ is a local coordinate centered at $s$, near $s$, $X$ is a sum of copies of the Riemann surface of $t^{1/d_s}$. If $\chi(g_s) = 1$, $L_\chi$ is a local system at $s$. If $\chi(g_s) \neq 1$, the fibre of $L_\chi$ at $s$ is zero, and the monodromy of $L_\chi$ around $s$ is the $d_s$-th root of unity $\chi(g_s)$.

If we take $X$ to be the largest covering such that $G$ is killed by $d$, $G$ is the abelian group generated by the $g_s$, with the only relations $g_s^{d_s} = 1$ and $\prod g_s = 1$: $G = (\ZZ/d)^S/(\ZZ/d)$. If $p \in \PP^1$ and $\infty \in S$, $X$ corresponds to the extension $\CC(\PP^1)((z-s)_{s \in S - \{\infty\}})^{1/d}$ of $\CC(\PP^1)$. This shows that any system of roots of unity $(\alpha_s)$, with $\prod \alpha_s = 1$, is of the form $(\chi(g_s))_{s \in S}$ for suitable $X$ and $\chi$.

Let $H^*(X, \CC)_\chi$ be the subspace of $H^*(X, \CC)$ on which $G$ acts (by transport of structure) as $\chi$. The isomorphism $H^*(X; \CC) = H^*(P, \pi_*\CC)$ is compatible with the action of $G$, hence induces isomorphisms
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.23.1}
H^*(X, \CC)_\chi = H^*(P, L_\chi).
\end{equation}
On $P-S$, the sheaf $\mathcal{O}(L_\chi)$ is the direct factor $(\pi_*\mathcal{O})_\chi$ of $\pi_*\mathcal{O}$ consisting of the holomorphic functions on $X$ such that $f(g^{-1}x) = \chi(g)f(x)$. Similarly, $\Omega^1(L_\chi) = (\pi_*\Omega^1)_\chi$. Let $j'$ be the inclusion of $X - \pi^{-1}(S)$ in $X$. On $P$, one has again
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.23.2}
j_*^m(L_\chi) = (\pi_*j'^m\mathcal{O})_\chi \quad \text{and}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.23.3}
j_*^m\Omega^1(L_\chi) = (\pi_*j'^m\Omega^1)_\chi.
\end{equation}
Let us assume $\chi(g_s) \neq 1$ for $s \in S$, so that $H^*(P, L_\chi) = H^*_c(P-S, L_\chi)$. Differential forms of the first kind on $P-S$ correspond then, by (2.23.3), to the differential forms of the first kind on $X$, on which $G$ acts by $\chi$:
\[
H^{1,0}(P-S, L_\chi) = \Gamma(X, \Omega^1_X)_\chi = H^{1,0}(X)_\chi
\]
and (2.20) is the trace on $H^1(P-S, L_\chi) = H^1(X, \CC)_\chi$ of the Hodge decomposition of $H^1(X, \CC)$.


\section{Reformulation of Picard's Theorem}

\subsection{}
We now let the punctures $s$ move, while the monodromy remains the same. The starting data will be:
\begin{itemize}
\item[$\bullet$] $P$: a complex projective line;
\item[$\bullet$] $N$: an integer, $\geq 3$;
\item[$\bullet$] $S$: a set with $N$ elements, for instance $[1, N] \subset \NN$;
\item[$\bullet$] $\alpha = (\alpha_s)_{s \in S}$: a family of complex numbers indexed by $S$, satisfying $\prod \alpha_s = 1$, and such that none of the $\alpha_s$ is $1$.
\end{itemize}
We will be mainly interested in the case $|\alpha_s| = 1$.

For any space $X$ mapping to $\PP^S$, we will write $P_X$ for the pull back on $X$ of the universal punctured line
\[
P_0 = \{(p, m) \in \PP \times \PP^S \mid p \notin m(S)\},
\]
and $\pi$ for the projection $P_X \to X$. Suppose a group $H$ acts freely on $X$, with quotient $Y$, and that a lifting of the action to $P_X$ is given. We will then write $P_Y$ for $P_X/H$ and $\pi$ for the projection $P_Y \to Y$. The $H$-equivariant fiber space $P_X$ over $X$ is then the pull back of $P_Y$ over $Y$.

Let $M \subset \PP^S$ be the space of injective maps $m: S \to \PP$. Then,
\[
P_M = \{(p, m) \in \PP \times M \mid p \notin m(S)\}
\]
is a topologically locally trivial fiber space above $M$. On each fiber $P_m = P - m(S)$, there is a rank one local system with monodromy $\alpha$, i.e.\ with monodromy $\alpha_s$ around $m(s)$, for $s \in S$. However, this local system not being unique up to unique isomorphism, one cannot conclude that on $P_M$ there is a unique rank one local system $L$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:3.1.1}
\text{For $m$ in $M$, the local system $L_m$ induced by $L$ on $P_m$ has monodromy $\alpha$.}
\end{equation}
If $L$ satisfies (3.1.1), all local systems satisfying (3.1.1) are of the form $L \otimes \pi^*T$, for $T$ a rank one local system $M$. We will see in (3.12), (3.13) that there is an $L$ satisfying (3.1.1). If $N$ were $2$, the analogous existence assertion would be false for $\alpha \neq \pm 1$.

\subsection{}
Suppose $L$ satisfies (3.1.1). The projection $\pi$ being topologically locally trivial, the $H^1(P_m, L_m)$ ($= H^1_c(P_m, L_m)$ by (2.6.1)) organize themselves into a local system $R^1\pi_!L$ ($= R^1\pi_*L$) on $M$.

We will be interested mainly in the corresponding flat projective space bundle $B(\alpha)_M := \PP R^1\pi_!L$, the fiber space with fiber at $m \in M$ the projective space $\PP H^1(P_m, L_m) := (H^1(P_m, L_m) - \{0\})/\CC^*$, and with flat structure, that deduced from $R^1\pi_!L$. For any vector space $V$ and non-zero element $v \in V$, we will denote by $\PP V$ the projective space of one-dimensional subspaces of $V$ and by $\PP_v$ the image of $v$ in $\PP V$.

If $L' = L \otimes \pi^*T$ is another local system satisfying (3.1.1),
\[
R^1\pi_!L' = (R^1\pi_!L) \otimes T,
\]
and $\PP R^1\pi_!L'$ is canonically isomorphic to $\PP R^1\pi_!L$, i.e.\ the flat projective bundle $B(\alpha)_M$ depends only on $\alpha$. Another explanation of the same fact: the automorphisms of a local system $L_m$ on $P_m$ with monodromy $\alpha$ act trivially on $\PP H^1(P_m, L_m)$. Hence, although $L_m$ is unique up to only a non unique isomorphism, $\PP H^1(P_m, L_m)$ is defined up to unique isomorphism.

\subsection{}
Locally on $M$, the existence of $L$ satisfying (3.1.1) poses no problem: if $U$ is a contractible neighborhood of $m \in M$, a local system $L_m$ on $P_m$ with monodromy $\alpha$ extends uniquely to a local system $L_U$ on $\pi^{-1}(U)$ and this extension has, fiber by fiber, monodromy $\alpha$. The flat projective space bundle $\PP R^1\pi_!L_U$ on $U$ is independent (up to unique isomorphism) of the choice of $L_U$; hence for variable $U$, they glue into a flat projective space bundle on the whole of $M$. This enables us to define $B(\alpha)_M$ without having to assume the existence of a global $L$.

For $o \in M$, the flat structure of $B(\alpha)_M$ defines an action of $\pi_1(M, o)$ by projective transformations on the fibre $B(\alpha)_o$. A choice of $L$ enables one to lift this projective representation of $\pi_1(M, o)$ on $\PP H^1(P_o, L_o)$ to a linear representation on $H^1(P_o, L_o)$.

\subsection{}
Fix a system of complex numbers $(\mu_s)_{s \in S}$ such that $\alpha_s = \exp(2\pi i \mu_s)$, and that $\sum \mu_s = 2$. For each $m \in M$, there is then up to a factor a unique non-zero section $\omega$ of $\Omega^1(L_m)$ on $P_m$, meromorphic on $P$, and such that $v_s(\omega) > -\mu_s$ ($s \in S$) (2.12). Its cohomology class is not trivial (2.13). This construction hence defines a section $w_\alpha(m)$ of the projection $B(\alpha)_M \to M$.

The flat structure of the projective space bundle $B(\alpha)$ induces a structure of holomorphic projective space bundle. With respect to it, we have:

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:3.5}
The section $w_\alpha$ is holomorphic.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The question is local on $M$. Fix $m \in M$, and choose on the fibre $P_m$ a system of cycles $C_i$ with coefficient in $L^\vee$ giving rise to a basis of $H_1(P_m, L^\vee)$. They have support in some compact $K$ and, for $U$ a contractible neighborhood of $m$, small enough so that $K \times U$ is isomorphic to the pull back of $L|_K$ by a unique isomorphism which is the identity above $m$. This enables us, for $m' \in U$, to consider $C_i$ as a cycle with coefficients in $L^\vee$ on $P_{m'}$; and each linear form $\langle -, C_i \rangle$ on the $H_1(P_{m'}, L_{m'}^\vee)$ ($m' \in U$) is a horizontal linear form on $R^1\pi_!L$. This provides a horizontal system of projective coordinates on $\PP R^1\pi_!L$.

Fix a coordinate $z: \PP \to \PP^1$, such that, for $m'$ in a possibly smaller neighborhood $U$ of $m$, $z^{-1}(\infty) \subset P_{m'}$, and let us trivialize $L$ along $z^{-1}(\infty)$ by a section $e$. We can take (cf.\ (2.12))
\[
\omega = \prod_{s \in S} (z - z(m'(s)))^{-\mu_s} \cdot dz \cdot e,
\]
and the projective coordinates of $w$ are the
\[
\int_{C_i} \prod_{s \in S} (z - z(m'(s)))^{-\mu_s} \cdot dz \cdot e.
\]
This is clearly holomorphic in $m'$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rmk:3.6}
A more general method to get horizontal linear forms on $R^1\pi_!L$ is to start with a $C^\infty$ diffeomorphism $\varphi_{m,m'}: (P_m, L) \xrightarrow{\sim} (P_{m'}, L_{m'})$, and with $L^\vee$-valued cycle or current $C$ on $P_m$, and to take $\int_{\varphi_{m,m'}(C)}$. For instance, if $C$ is a path from $m(s)$ to $m(t)$, together with a section $e$ of $L^\vee$ on it, one deforms it with $m'$ so that $C(m')$ remains a path from $m'(s)$ to $m'(t)$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{}
The group $G$ of automorphisms of $\PP$ is isomorphic to $\PGL(2)$. Its action on $\PP$ induces an action on $M \subset \PP^S$, on the space $P_M$, and on the flat projective bundle $B(\alpha)_M$ on $M$. The section $w_\alpha$ is preserved. The action on $M$ is free. Let $Q = M/G$. The fiber bundle $P_M$, the flat bundle $B(\alpha)_M$ and $w_\alpha$ being equivariant, they descend to a fiber bundle $P_Q$, a flat projective space bundle $B(\alpha)_Q$ and a section $w_\alpha$ of $B(\alpha)_Q \to Q$.

For $\PP = \PP^1$, and for $a, b, c$ distinct in $S$, let $M_0 \subset M$ be the space of $m$ such that $m(a) = 0$, $m(b) = 1$, $m(c) = \infty$. One has $\PGL(2) \times M_0 \xrightarrow{\sim} M$. The quotient map hence induces an isomorphism $M_0 \xrightarrow{\sim} Q$, and via this isomorphism, $B(\alpha)_Q$ and $w_\alpha$ can be identified with the restriction of $B(\alpha)_M$ and $w_\alpha$ to $M_0$.

\subsection{}
Fix a base point $o \in Q$. The section $w_\alpha$ can then be viewed as a multivalued map from $Q$ to $B(\alpha)_o$. More precisely, let $\widetilde{Q}$ be the covering of $Q$ corresponding to the kernel of the monodromy action $\rho$ of $\pi_1(Q, o)$ on $B(\alpha)_o$, i.e.\ let $\widetilde{Q}$ be the quotient by $\Ker(\rho)$ of the universal covering of $(Q, o)$. The pull back of $B(\alpha)_Q$ on $\widetilde{Q}$ is $\widetilde{Q} \times B(\alpha)_o$, and $w_\alpha$ becomes a $\pi_1(Q, o)$-equivariant map $\widetilde{w}$ from $\widetilde{Q}$ to $B(\alpha)_o$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:3.9}
$\widetilde{w}: \widetilde{Q} \to B(\alpha)_o$ is \\'etale.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We may take $\PP = \PP^1$ and replace $Q$ by $M_0$, as in (3.7). The problem is local. Take $m \in M_0$, let $e$ be a trivialization of $L$---near $m$---on $\RR^+$ near $\infty$. We may take
\[
\omega = \prod_{s \in S} (z - m(s))^{-\mu_s} \cdot dz \cdot e
\]
(cf.\ (2.8)). Choose cycles $C_i$ as in (3.5, 3.6). Differentiating in $m$, one has, with $d_{M_0}\omega = \sum \frac{\partial \omega}{\partial m(s)} dm(s)$,
\[
d_{M_0}\omega = \sum_s \mu_s \frac{dm(s)}{z - m(s)} \cdot \omega.
\]
The spaces $\widetilde{Q}$ and $B(\alpha)_o$ have the same dimension $N-3$. The map $\widetilde{w}$ is hence \\'etale at $m$ if and only if $d(\widetilde{w})$ is injective, i.e.\ if and only if for no tangent vector $v \neq 0$ at $m$ is the family $\langle \partial_v \omega, C_i \rangle$ proportional to the family of integrals $\langle \omega, C_i \rangle$, i.e.\ if and only if for no $v$ the cohomology class of $\partial_v \omega$ is proportional to that of $\omega$. This means that the cohomology class of any non-trivial linear combination
\[
\sum b_s \frac{\omega}{z - m(s)} \quad (b_s \in \CC, \sum b_s = 0)
\]
should be non-trivial. If $b_s \neq 0$, $\frac{\omega}{z-m(s)}$ is of valuation exactly $v_s(\omega) - 1$ at $s$, and hence $\neq 0$. If $b_s = 0$, the valuation is $\geq v_s$. This shows that $\sum b_s \frac{\omega}{z-m(s)} \neq 0$ and that
\[
\sum_s v_s\left(\sum_t b_t \frac{\omega}{z-m(t)}\right) = \sum_{s: b_s \neq 0} (v_s(\omega) - 1) + \sum_{s: b_s = 0} v_s(\omega) \geq \sum v_s(\omega) - (N-2) > 1-N,
\]
so that the non vanishing of the class of $\widetilde{\omega}$ results from (2.14).
\end{proof}

\subsection{}
We will now assume that $|\alpha_s| = 1$ and that the numbers $\mu_s$ defined by $\alpha_s = \exp(2\pi i \mu_s)$, $0 < \mu_s < 1$, satisfy $\sum \mu_s = 2$.

Locally on $M$, $L$ admits a horizontal positive definite Hermitian form $(\,,\,)$, unique up to a positive real factor (2.18, 3.3). It induces on $R^1\pi_!L$ a horizontal Hermitian form $(\,,\,) = \frac{-1}{2\pi i}\psi$ (2.18), of signature $(1, N-3)$ (2.21). The vectors on which $(\,,\,)$ is positive define a horizontal family of complex balls $B^+(\alpha)_M \subset B(\alpha)_M$, and the section $w_\alpha$ is in $B^+(\alpha)_M$ (2.20) (for the meaning of the term ``complex ball'', see \S 5).

The family $B^+(\alpha)_M$ is equivariant, hence descends to $B^+(\alpha)_Q \subset B(\alpha)_Q$. The \\'etale map of (3.9) is a map
\begin{equation}\label{eq:3.10.1}
\widetilde{w}: \widetilde{Q} \to B^+(\alpha)_o
\end{equation}
equivariant with respect to the action of $\pi_1(Q, o)$ on $B^+(\alpha)_o$. This action is a morphism
\begin{equation}\label{eq:3.10.2}
\rho: \pi_1(Q, o) \to \PU\left(H^1(P_o, L_o), \frac{-1}{2\pi i}\psi\right).
\end{equation}

Notation being as above, our main result is the
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:3.11}
Assume that
\begin{itemize}
\item[\emph{(INT)}] The numbers $\mu_s$ defined by $\alpha_s = \exp(2\pi i \mu_s)$, $0 < \mu_s < 1$, satisfy $\sum \mu_s = 2$ and, for all $s \neq t$ in $S$ such that $\mu_s + \mu_t < 1$, $(1 - \mu_s - \mu_t)^{-1}$ is an integer.
\end{itemize}
Then, the image $\Gamma$ of (3.10.2) is a lattice in the projective unitary group
\[
\PU\left(H^1(P_o, L_o), \frac{-1}{2\pi i}\psi\right) = \PU(1, N-3).
\]
\end{theorem}

The theorem will be proved in section 11, where it is restated as theorem (11.4).

The strategy of the proof is presented in the next paragraphs.

In \S 4, we define a partial compactification $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$ of $Q$. We also define a compactification $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$, with $Q \subset Q_{\mathrm{st}} \subset Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$ and $Q_{\mathrm{sst}} - Q_{\mathrm{st}}$ finite. In \S 8, we recall a construction of R. H. Fox to define the completion $\widetilde{Q}_{\mathrm{st}}$ (resp.\ $\widetilde{Q}_{\mathrm{sst}}$) of $\widetilde{Q}$ over $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$ (resp.\ $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$). When the condition (INT) is satisfied, one can show that each point $y$ of $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$ admits open neighborhoods $U$ such that the inverse image of $U$ in $\widetilde{Q}$ is a disjoint sum of finite coverings of $U \cap Q$. The completion $\widetilde{Q}_{\mathrm{st}}$ is then a normal analytic space; it is the normal ramified covering of $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$ extending $\widetilde{Q}$.

The results of \S 6 allow us, in \S 8, to extend $\widetilde{w}$ to a map from $\widetilde{Q}_{\mathrm{st}}$ to $B^+(\alpha)_o$. We will again write $\widetilde{w}$ for the extended map. In \S 9, we show that the condition INT is tantamount to requiring this extended map to be \\'etale in codimension one. Since the projection $\widetilde{Q}_{\mathrm{st}} \to Q_{\mathrm{st}}$ is locally (on $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$) finite to one, and $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$ is a normal analytic space, it follows from \S 6 that the extended map $\widetilde{w}: \widetilde{Q}_{\mathrm{st}} \to B^+(\alpha)_o$ is locally finite to one. By the purity of the branch locus theorem, if $\widetilde{w}$ is \\'etale in codimension one, it is then \\'etale everywhere. Actually, we give two proofs of this fact in \S 10, the second proof not requiring the theorem on purity of branch locus. Additional work, relying on the compactness of $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$, shows that $\widetilde{w}: \widetilde{Q}_{\mathrm{st}} \to B^+(\alpha)_o$ is a topological covering map. The ball being simply connected, it is an isomorphism. In other words, the inverse of the multivalued map $w_\alpha$ is a single valued map $w_\alpha^{-1}: B^+(\alpha)_o \to \widetilde{Q}_{\mathrm{st}}$.

The homeomorphism $\widetilde{w}$ transforms the fibers of the projection $\widetilde{Q}_{\mathrm{st}} \to Q_{\mathrm{st}}$ into the orbits of $\Gamma$. Those orbits are hence discrete, and $\Gamma$ is a discrete subgroup of the Lie group $\PU(1, N-3)$ of isometries of $B^+(\alpha)_o$. When $Q_{\mathrm{st}} = Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$, the quotient $B^+(\alpha)_o/\Gamma \cong Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$ is compact, and $\Gamma$ is cocompact. In the general case, a local analysis near the cusps $p \in Q_{\mathrm{sst}} - Q_{\mathrm{st}}$ shows that it has finite covolume.

For $N = 3$, the group $\PU(1, 0)$ is trivial and the statement (3.11) uninteresting. For $N \geq 4$, it is not at once clear that $(1-\mu_i-\mu_j)^{-1}$ must be an integer for all $i \neq j$ under condition INT (cf.\ (14.2.1)). It is easy to see however that condition INT implies that all $\mu_i$ are rational. We show this, even though it is not used in our proof.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:3.12}
Assume that $\card S > 4$. If positive real numbers $(\nu_s)_{s \in S}$ sum up to $2$ and if for each of the pairs of elements $s \neq t$ of $S$ for which $\nu_s + \nu_t \leq 1$, $\nu_s + \nu_t$ is rational, then all $\nu_s$ are rational.
\end{lemma}

We may and shall assume that $S$ is the set of integer from $1$ to $N$, $N \geq 4$, and that $\nu_1 \leq \cdots \leq \nu_N$.

\textit{Case 1.---} $\nu_1 + \nu_2 > 1$. For any distinct $i, j < N$ one can find $k < N$ distinct from $i$ and $j$. One has $\nu_k + \nu_N \geq \nu_1 + \nu_2 > 1$, hence
\[
\nu_i + \nu_j = 2 - (\nu_k + \nu_N) < 1
\]
is rational. If three numbers $a, b, c$ are such that $a+b$, $b+c$ and $c+a$ are rational, they are rational. Applying this to the $\nu_i$ ($i < N$), we find that they are rational. So is $\nu_N$, because $\sum \nu_i = 2$.

\textit{Case 2.---} $\nu_1 + \nu_2 \leq 1$. In this case, for any $i \neq 1$, $\nu_1 + \nu_i \leq \nu_1 + \nu_2 \leq 1$ is rational. Summing, one concludes that $(N-2)\nu_1 + \sum \nu_i = (N-2)\nu_1 + 2$ is rational: $\nu_1$ is rational, and so are the $\nu_i$ ($i \neq 1$).

\subsection{}
Next, we explain how local systems $L$ with the prescribed monodromy in the fiber direction can be constructed globally above $M$ on $P_M$. Fix three distinct elements $a, b, c \in S$. For each $m$ in $M$, there is then a unique isomorphism $\PP \xrightarrow{\sim} \PP^1$ mapping $m(a), m(b), m(c)$ respectively to $0, 1, \infty$. Let $z: P_M \to \PP^1$ be the resulting map. When no ambiguity results, we will write $z(x)$ for $z(x, m)$. Let $A: M \to \RR_+$ be a large enough continuous function. What is needed is that $[A(m), \infty[$ be disjoint from $z(m(S))$. For any $U \subset M$, let $I_U$ denote the locus of $(x, m) \in \pi^{-1}(U)$ such that $z(x, m) \in [A(m), \infty[$. If $U \subset M$ is contractible, then $I_U \subset P_M$ is too. For $U \subset M$ contractible, if $L$ is a local system on $\pi^{-1}(U)$ with the prescribed monodromy $\alpha$ (cf.\ (3.3)), then $L_U$ admits an horizontal section $e_U \not\equiv 0$ on $I_U$. The pair $(L_U, e_U)$ is unique up to unique isomorphism, hence there is no problem in glueing. One gets a global local system $L$ on $P_M$, provided with an horizontal section $e$ on $I_M$.

Let $M_0 = \{m \in M \mid m(a) = 0, m(b) = 1, m(c) = \infty\}$ as in (3.7). The product decomposition $\PGL(2) \times M_0 \xrightarrow{\sim} M$ induces an isomorphism $\PGL(2) \times P_{M_0} \xrightarrow{\sim} P_M$; and if one takes $A(m)$ constant on the $\PGL(2)$ orbits, so that $I_M$ is the pullback of $I_{M_0}$, the system $(L, e)$ is the pull back of its restriction to $P_{M_0}$.

\subsection{}
We give two other descriptions of the same $L$. Fix numbers $\mu_s$ such that $\alpha_s = \exp(2\pi i \mu_s)$. Let $L_\mu$ be the subsheaf of $\mathcal{O}$ (cf.\ \S 2) consisting of those functions which are constant multiples of $\prod_{s \in S} (z - z(m(s)))^{\mu_s}$. This function is multivalued but the ratio of two determinations is a constant, so that the definition of $L_\mu$ makes sense. If $\mu'$ is another choice of $\mu_s$ with $\alpha_s = \exp(2\pi i \mu'_s)$, the multiplication by the univalued function $\prod_{s \in S} (z - z(m(s)))^{\mu'_s - \mu_s}$ is an isomorphism of $L_\mu$ with $L_{\mu'}$.

The principal determination of the multivalued function $\prod_{s \in S} (z - z(m(s)))^{\mu_s}$ on $I_M$ is defined as $\exp(\sum \mu_s \log(z - z(m(s))))$ with $|\arg \log(z - z(m(s)))| < \pi$ for $z$ real and large enough. This provides a section of $L_\mu$ on $I_M$. One easily checks that $L_\mu$ has the monodromy $\alpha$ on each fibre.

An identical but more algebraic description of $L_\mu$ is: the local system of horizontal sections of $\mathcal{O}$, provided with the connection $\nabla_\mu$ for which
\[
f^{-1}\nabla_\mu f = \frac{df}{f} - \sum_s \mu_s \frac{d(z - z(m(s)))}{z - z(m(s))}.
\]

\subsection{}
We close section 3 with some remarks about liftings of the projective representation
\[
\rho: \pi_1(Q, o) \to \PGL(H^1(P_o, L_o))
\]
to a linear representation.

As in (3.7) and (3.13), fix three elements $a, b, c$ in $S$ and denote by $M_0$ the subset of $M$ with $m(a) = 0$, $m(b) = 1$ and $m(c) = \infty$. Let $M(c)$ denote the subset of $m \in M$ with $m(c) = \infty$, and let $B$ denote the stabilizer of $\infty$ in $\PGL(2)$. One has $M_0 \xrightarrow{\sim} Q$ and $B \times M_0 \xrightarrow{\sim} M(c)$, $\PGL(2) \times M_0 \xrightarrow{\sim} M$.

As is well known,
\[
\pi_1(\PGL(2)) = \pi_1(\SO(3)) = \pi_1(\SU(2)/\{\pm 1\}) = \ZZ/2
\]
and $\pi_1(B) = \pi_1(\CC^*) = \ZZ$, so that
\begin{align}
\pi_1(M) &\cong \pi_1(Q, o) \oplus \ZZ/2, \label{eq:3.15.1} \\
\pi_1(M(c)) &\cong \pi_1(Q, o) \oplus \ZZ. \label{eq:3.15.2}
\end{align}
Both those isomorphisms depend on the choices of $a, b, c$.

For $X$ any of $Q$, $M(c)$ or $M$, each choice of a local system $L$ on $P_X$ with fiberwise monodromy $\alpha$ leads to a lifting
\[
\rho': \pi_1(X, o) \to \GL(H^1(P_o, L_o))
\]
of $\rho$. If $L$ carries a unitary structure, $\rho'$ has values in $\U(H^1(P_o, L_o), \frac{-1}{2\pi i}\psi)$. For instance, the $L$ of (3.13) carries a unique unitary structure for which $e$ is of length one, and its restriction to $M_0 \cong Q$ provides a lifting $\rho': \pi_1(Q, o) \to \U(H^1(P_o, L_o), \frac{-1}{2\pi i}\psi)$.

The local system $L$ itself provides a lifting $\rho': \pi_1(M, o) \to \U(H^1(P_o, L_o), \psi)$ which is trivial on the second factor of the decomposition (3.15.1).

Let $L(c)$ denote the local system on $P_{M(c)}$ which is given by the subsheaf of $\mathcal{O}$ consisting of those functions which are constant multiples of
\[
\prod_{s \in S - \{c\}} (z - m(s))^{-\mu_s}.
\]
Here, $z$ is a fixed coordinate on $\PP$ composed with $\PP \times M(c) \to \PP$ and not as in (3.13) and (3.13). The local system $L(c)$ provides a lifting $\rho': \pi_1(M(c)) \to \U(H^1(P_o, L_o), \frac{-1}{2\pi i}\psi)$ of $\rho$.

Inasmuch as $M(c)$ is the space of distinct $(N-1)$-uples of points in the plane ($N = \card S$), $\pi_1(M(c))$ is by definition the colored braid group on $(N-1)$ strands. For any $s, t \in S - \{c\}$, let $\gamma_{s,t}$ in $\pi_1(M(c))$ be a path in which $t$ comes near $s$, makes a positive turn around $s$ and comes back to its original position. The colored braid group is generated by such elements. The lifting $\rho'$ provided by $L(c)$ has the virtue that each $\rho'(\gamma_{s,t})$ is a pseudo-reflection (i.e.\ $\rho'(\gamma_{s,t}) - I$ is of rank one)---cf.\ (9.1), (12.3).


\section{The compactified quotient space $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$}

\subsection{}
As in section 3, we fix a complex projective line $\PP$, an integer $N \geq 3$ and a finite set $S$ with $N$ elements. We further fix a family $\mu = (\mu_s)_{s \in S}$ of real numbers with $0 < \mu_s < 1$ and $\sum \mu_s = 2$. The complex numbers $\alpha_s := \exp(2\pi i \mu_s)$ satisfy $|\alpha_s| = 1$, $\alpha_s \neq 1$ and $\prod \alpha_s = 1$.

As in section 3, $M \subset \PP^S$ is the space of injective maps $\gamma: S \to \PP$. We denote by $\PGL(2)$ the group of automorphisms of $\PP$.

These conventions will hold throughout this section as well as in sections 6 to 12. In this section, from (4.2) on, $\PP$ will be the standard projective line $\PP := \PP^1 = \CC \cup \{\infty\}$ and $M$ the space of $S$-uples of distinct points of the Riemann sphere. This is no loss of generality.

\subsection{}
The group $\PGL(2)$ operates diagonally on $\PP^S$. We shall define a compactification of the quotient space $Q := \PGL(2) \backslash M$. The definition is taken from D. Mumford's theory of quotient varieties of reductive groups (cf.\ (4.6)). The account here is self-contained.

A point $y \in \PP^S$ is called \emph{$\mu$-stable} (resp.\ \emph{$\mu$-semi-stable}) if and only if for all $z \in \PP$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:4.1.1}
\sum_{y(s) = z} \mu_s < 1 \quad \text{(resp.\ $\leq 1$)}.
\end{equation}
The set of all $\mu$-stable points (resp.\ $\mu$-semi-stable points) is denoted $M_{\mathrm{st}}$ (resp.\ $M_{\mathrm{sst}}$) and we set $M_{0\mathrm{usp}} = M_{\mathrm{sst}} - M_{\mathrm{st}}$.

For each partition $\{S_1, S_2\}$ of $S$ with $\sum_{s \in S_i} \mu_s = 1$ ($i = 1, 2$), the points $y$ in $\PP^S$ for which $y(S_1) \cap y(S_2) = \emptyset$ and $y$ constant on $S_1$ or $S_2$ are in $M_{0\mathrm{usp}}$. All points in $M_{0\mathrm{usp}}$ are obtained in this way, each from a unique partition.

On $M_{\mathrm{sst}}$ we define a relation $\sim$ via
\[
y \sim y' \text{ if and only if either}
\]
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
\item $y, y' \in M_{\mathrm{st}}$ and $y' \in \PGL(2) \, y$; or
\item $y, y' \in M_{0\mathrm{usp}}$ and the partitions of $S$ corresponding to $y$ and $y'$ coincide.
\end{enumerate}
It is clear that $\sim$ is an equivalence relation. Set
\[
Q_{\mathrm{st}} = M_{\mathrm{st}}/\PGL(2), \quad Q_{\mathrm{sst}} = M_{\mathrm{sst}}/\sim, \quad Q_{0\mathrm{usp}} = M_{0\mathrm{usp}}/\sim
\]
each with its quotient topology. The elements of $Q_{0\mathrm{usp}}$ are uniquely determined by their partitions. Thus $Q_{0\mathrm{usp}}$ is a finite set.

\begin{example}\label{ex:4.2}
The cross-ratio $c(z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4) = \frac{z_1 - z_3}{z_3 - z_2} \cdot \frac{z_1 - z_4}{z_4 - z_2}$ of four points in $\PP := \PP^1$ is defined and is a continuous map into $\PP^1$ as long as no three of $z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4$ are equal. When two of the $z_i$ are equal, the cross-ratio takes one of the values $0, 1, \infty$, the value depending only on the partition of $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ consisting of the subset $\{i, j\}$ with $z_i = z_j$ and its complement.

Suppose $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ and $\mu_s = \frac{1}{2}$ for all $s \in S$. One can show that the cross-ratio map $M_{\mathrm{sst}} \to \PP^1$ descends to $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$ and yields a homeomorphism onto the projective line.
\end{example}

\subsection{}
Let $M'$ be the space of $y \in \PP^S$ such that $y(S)$ has at least three distinct points, and let $Q' = \PGL(2) \backslash M'$ be the corresponding quotient orbit space. Fix $a, b, c$ in $S$, let $U$ be the set of all $y \in M'$ taking distinct values on $a, b, c$, and let $U_0$ be the subset of all $y \in M$ for which $y(a) = 0$, $y(b) = 1$, $y(c) = \infty$. Then $U$ is in $M$, stable under the action of $\PGL(2)$, and $\PGL(2) \times U_0 \xrightarrow{\sim} U$. Hence
\[
U_0 \xrightarrow{\sim} \PGL(2) \backslash U \hookrightarrow Q'.
\]
As one varies $a, b, c$, the images of $U_0$ in $Q'$ cover $Q'$. Consequently $Q'$ is a manifold. However, this manifold is not Hausdorff if $\card S > 3$. For $\card S = 4$ (cf.\ (4.2)) one gets the projective line with $0, 1$ and $\infty$ doubled. One infers from (4.1.1) that $M_{\mathrm{st}} \subset M'$. The quotient $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$ being open in $Q'$ is a manifold. The following Lemma implies that it is Hausdorff.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:4.3}
$Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$ is Hausdorff.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $F$ denote the set of injective maps of $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ into $S$. For any $f \in F$, let $M_f$ denote the set of all $y \in M_{\mathrm{sst}}$ such that for all $z \in \PP$, $\card(yf)^{-1}(z) < 2$ and additionally, if $y \in M_{0\mathrm{usp}}$, $\card f^{-1}(S_i) = 2$ for $i = 1, 2$ where $\{S_1, S_2\}$ is the partition of $S$ corresponding to $y$. The additional condition guarantees that $M_f \cap M_{f'}$ as well as $M_f \cap M_{f'}$ is $\sim$-saturated.

For any $f \in F$, define $c_f: M_f \to \PP$ via the cross-ratio:
\[
c_f(y) = c(yf(1), yf(2), yf(3), yf(4));
\]
the function $c_f$ is constant on $\sim$-equivalence classes of the $\sim$-saturated set $M_f$. Inasmuch as $c_f$ is continuous on $M_f$, it descends to a continuous function on $M_f/\sim$. To prove the lemma, it suffices to prove:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:4.3.1}
\text{For any $y \not\sim y'$ mod $\sim$, there is an $f \in F$ such that $y$ and $y'$ are in $M_f$, and $c_f(y) \neq c_f(y')$.}
\end{equation}

One is free to replace $y$ and $y'$ by $y_1$ and $y'_1$, with $y \sim y_1$, $y' \sim y'_1$.

To each $y$ in $M_{\mathrm{sst}}$ we attach a partition of $S$ as follows: for $y \in M_{0\mathrm{usp}}$, attach the partition defined in (4.1); for $y \in M_{\mathrm{st}}$, attach the partition $T = \{C_1, \ldots, C_k\}$ such that $y(s) = y(t)$ if and only if $s$ and $t$ lie in the same coset of $T$. If $y \sim y_1$, the same partition is attached to $y$ and $y_1$. In the proof of (4.3.1), we may and shall assume that if $y$ or $y'$ is in $M_{0\mathrm{usp}}$, then it is constant on the cosets of its partition.

We first treat the case that $y$ and $y'$ are in $M_{0\mathrm{usp}}$. Let $\{S_1, S_2\}$ and $\{S'_1, S'_2\}$ be their corresponding partitions of $S$. Since $y \not\sim y'$ mod $\sim$, $S_i \cap S'_j$ is not empty for $i, j \in \{1, 2\}$; otherwise, if say $S_1 \cap S'_2 = \emptyset$, we would have $S_1 \subset S'_1$, $S_1 \neq S'_1$ and
\[
\sum_{s \in S'_1 - S_1} \mu_s = 0,
\]
contradicting $0 < \mu_s$ for each $s$. Choose $f$ so that $\card f^{-1}(S_i \cap S'_j) = 1$ for each choice of $i, j \in \{1, 2\}$. Then for suitable choice of $f$ we have
\begin{align*}
yf(1), yf(2), yf(3), yf(4) &= a, a, b, b, \\
y'f(1), y'f(2), y'f(3), y'f(4) &= a', b', a', b',
\end{align*}
with $a \neq b$, $a' \neq b'$. Hence $c_f(y) = \infty$, $c_f(y') = 0$.

Suppose next that $y \in M_{\mathrm{st}}$ and $y' \in M_{0\mathrm{usp}}$. Let $T = \{C_i\}$ be the partition of $S$ corresponding to $y'$ by (4.2). Each coset in the partition of $S$ corresponding to $y$ must meet at least two distinct cosets of $T$. We can thus choose an $f \in F$ so that
\begin{align*}
yf(1), yf(2), yf(3), yf(4) &= a, a, b, b, \\
y'f(1), y'f(2), y'f(3), y'f(4) &= a', b', c, d,
\end{align*}
with $a \neq b$, $a' \neq b'$, and $c \neq d$. It follows at once that $y$ and $y'$ are in $M_f$, $c_f(y) = 1$, $c_f(y') \neq 1$.

It remains only to consider the case where $y$ and $y'$ are in $M_{\mathrm{st}}$; let $T$ and $T'$ be the corresponding partitions of $S$. If $T = T'$, then for any $f \in F$, $y \in M_f$ if and only if $y' \in M_f$. Replacing $y'$ by an element in its $\PGL(2)$-orbit, we can assume that there are three distinct cosets $C_1, C_2, C_3$ of $T$ with $y(s) = y'(s)$ for $s \in C_1 \cup C_2 \cup C_3$. If $c_f(y) = c_f(y')$ for all $f$ with $y \in M_f$ then, clearly $y = y'$. One is thus reduced to the case where $T \neq T'$. Then there is a $C_1 \in T$ and $C'_1 \in T'$ such that $C_1 \cap C'_1$ is not empty and $C_1 \neq C'_1$. Either $C_1 - C'_1$ or $C'_1 - C_1$ is not empty---say $C'_1 - C_1 \neq \emptyset$. Choose $C'_2 \in T'$ so that $C'_2 \cap C_1 \neq \emptyset$. Choose $C'_3 \in T'$ with $C'_3 \neq C'_2$. Next choose $C_2 \in T$ so that $C'_2 \cap C_2 \neq \emptyset$. Then $C'_3 \cup C_2 \neq S$; otherwise, since $y'$ and $y$ are in $M_{\mathrm{st}}$,
\[
2 < \sum_{s \in C'_1} \mu_s + \sum_{s \in C_2} \mu_s.
\]
Choose $f: \{1, 2, 3, 4\} \to S$ with
\[
f(1) \in C_1 \cap C'_1, \quad f(2) \in C'_2 \cap C_1, \quad f(3) \in C'_2 \cap C_2, \quad f(4) \in C'_3 \cup C_2.
\]
Any such $f$ is injective, and
\begin{align*}
yf(1), yf(2), yf(3), yf(4) &= a, b, c, d \quad \text{with } a \neq b, \, c \neq d, \\
y'f(1), y'f(2), y'f(3), y'f(4) &= a', a', b', c' \quad \text{with } a' \neq b', \, a' \neq c'.
\end{align*}
Hence $c_f(y') = 1$, $c_f(y) \neq 1$. This completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\subsection{}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:4.4.1}
Fix a Riemannian metric on $\PP$. For $y \in \PP^S$, define
\[
d(y) = \inf_{\sum_{s \in T} \mu_s > 1} \diam y(T)
\]
and
\[
a(y) = \sup_{g \in \PGL(2)} d(gy).
\]
Then there is $a > 0$ such that $\na(y) \geq a$ for all $y \in M_{\mathrm{st}}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The validity of the lemma is independent of the chosen metric. We will use the Fubini metric (for which $\PP$ is isometric to the sphere of radius 1) and prove the lemma with $a = \pi/4$.

If $y \in M_{0\mathrm{usp}}$ corresponds to the partition $\{S_1, S_2\}$ of $S$, with $y$ constant on $S_1$, let us take $g$ to fix $y(S_1)$ and the antipodal point $u$ of $\PP$ and to carry the complement of the $\varepsilon$-neighborhood of $y(S_1)$ into the $\varepsilon$-neighborhood of $u$. Taking $\varepsilon \to 0$, one gets $d(y) = \pi$.

For any $\varepsilon$ there is a compact $K \subset \PGL(2)$ such that for $g \notin K$, $g$ maps the complement of the $\varepsilon$-neighborhood of some point $u$ into the $\varepsilon$-neighborhood of some point $v$. If $y \in M_{\mathrm{st}}$ and if $\varepsilon$ is small enough, then, for all $u$, $\{s \in S \mid \dist(y(s), u) > \varepsilon\}$ is in $\mathscr{F}$. This implies $d(gy) \to 0$ for $g \to \infty$ in $\PGL(2)$. The sup defining $\na(y)$ is hence attained. Fix $y \in M_{\mathrm{st}}$ with $\na(y) = d(y)$. We have to prove $d(y) > a = \pi/4$.

Suppose to the contrary that $d(y) < a$. Fix $T_1 \in \mathscr{F}$ with $\diam y(T_1) < a$ and $t \in T_1$. For any $T_1, T_2$ in $\mathscr{F}$, $T_1 \cap T_2 \neq \emptyset$. If $S'$ is the union of all $T \in \mathscr{F}$ with $\diam y(T) < a$, $y(S')$ is hence contained in a half-sphere of radius $r < 2a = \pi/2$ with center $y(t)$. In particular, $y(S')$ is contained in an open halfsphere. Identify $\PP$ with the Riemann sphere $\CC \cup \{\infty\}$, with the unit disc the halfsphere, in such a way that $y(S')$ lies in $D = \{z \mid |z| < b\}$, $b < 1$. Take for $g$ the multiplication by $1 + \varepsilon$ ($\varepsilon > 0$). For $\varepsilon$ small enough (relative to $b$), distances in $D$ are increased by $g$. For any $T \in \mathscr{F}$ with $\diam y(T) < a$, one hence has $\diam gy(T) > \diam y(T)$. For $T \in \mathscr{F}$ with $\diam y(T) > a$, $\diam y(T)$ can decrease, but not much for $\varepsilon$ small, so that $d(gy) > d(y)$. This contradicts the choice of $y$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:4.4.2}
$Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$ is compact.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix $a > 0$ as in (4.4.1), and define $M' \subset \PP^S$ to be the set of $y$ such that $\diam y(T) > a$ for each $T \subset S$ with $\sum_{s \in T} \mu_s > 1$. The subset $M'$ of $\PP^S$ is closed, hence compact. It is contained in $M_{\mathrm{sst}}$ and, by (4.4.1), it maps onto $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$. Compactness follows.
\end{proof}

\subsection{}
By contrast with $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$ which is a manifold, the points of $Q_{0\mathrm{usp}}$ may be singular.

Let $\pi$ denote the natural projection of $M_{\mathrm{sst}}$ onto $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$. Let $\{S_1, S'_1\}$ be the partition corresponding to a point $y_0 \in M_{0\mathrm{usp}}$. We shall describe a neighborhood of $\pi(y_0)$ in $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$. We can assume that $y_0(s) = 0$ for all $s \in S_1$ and $y_0(s) = \infty$ for all $s \in S'_1$. Fix elements $a \in S_1$ and $b \in S'_1$, and define $V$ as the set of all $y \in M_{\mathrm{sst}}$ such that $y(a) = 0$, $y(b) = \infty$ and
\[
\sup_{s \in S_1} |y(s)| < \inf_{s \in S'_1} |y(s)|.
\]
Then, $\pi(V)$ is an open neighborhood of $\pi(y_0)$ in $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$ and is the quotient of $V$ by the equivalence relation on $V$ induced by $\sim$. The punctured neighborhood $\pi(V) - \pi(y_0)$ is the quotient of $V - \pi^{-1}\pi(y_0)$ by the stabilizer in $\PGL(2)$ of the points $0$ and $\infty$:
\[
\pi(V) - \pi(y_0) = (V - \pi^{-1}\pi(y_0))/\CC^*.
\]
Set $S_2 := S_1 - \{a\}$, $S'_2 := S'_1 - \{b\}$, $m = \card(S_2)$, $m' = \card(S'_2)$ and consider the composite map
\[
\Phi: V \to \CC^{S_2} \times \CC^{S'_2} = \CC^m \times \CC^{m'} \to \CC^{mm'}
\]
\[
y \mapsto ((y(s))_{s \in S_2}, (y(s)^{-1})_{s \in S'_2}) \mapsto (y(s) \cdot y(t)^{-1})_{s \in S_2, t \in S'_2}.
\]
The map $\Phi$ descends to an embedding of $\pi(V)$ into $\CC^{mm'}$, whose image is the intersection of the cone over the Veronese embedding of $\PP^{m-1} \times \PP^{m'-1}$ in $\PP^{mm'-1}$ with the polydisc (of radius $< 1$, $|x_{s,t}| < 1$).

If $m = 1$ or $m' = 1$, the Veronese embedding is an isomorphism with $\PP^{mm'-1}$. As a consequence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:4.5.1}
\text{If $\card(S_1) = 2$ or $\card(S'_1) = 2$, the cusp point with partition $\{S_1, S'_1\}$ is non singular.}
\end{equation}

One can define an algebraic structure on $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$ by using as a chart at a point $\PGL(2) \, y$ of $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$ the orbit space
\[
\PGL(2) \backslash \{y' \in M_{\mathrm{st}} \mid y'(i) \neq y'(j) \text{ if } y(i) \neq y(j)\}
\]
and at a cusp point with partition $\{S_1, S'_1\}$ the Zariski-open subset of the Veronese cone of $\PP^{m-1} \times \PP^{m'-1}$ in $\CC^{mm'}$ given by $x_{s,t} = y(s)/y(t) \neq 1$, for all $s \in S_1$, $t \in S'_1$.

\begin{example}
\textbf{1.---} $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$, $\mu_s = \frac{2}{5}$ for all $s \in S$. Here there are no cusp points: $Q_{\mathrm{sst}} = Q_{\mathrm{st}} = \PGL(2) \backslash M_{\mathrm{st}}$. Each pair $s, t$ in $S$ defines the diagonal line $L_{s,t}: y(s) = y(t)$ in $M_{\mathrm{st}}$ and under the projection to $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$, one gets 10 lines. The map of $M_{\mathrm{st}}$ to $(\PP^1)^2$ given by
\[
(y_1, y_2, y_3, y_4, y_5) \mapsto (\infty, 0, 1, c(y_1, y_2, y_3, y_4)^{-1}, c(y_1, y_2, y_3, y_5)^{-1})
\]
(where $c$ denotes cross-ratio) descends to a map $p$ of $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$ which is biregular except at $L_{0,\infty} = p^{-1}(0,0)$, $L_{0,\infty} = p^{-1}(1,1)$, $L_{\infty,\infty} = p^{-1}(\infty, \infty)$. Here, $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$ is a blowup of $\PP^1 \times \PP^1$. Each of the curves $p^{-1}(0,0)$, $p^{-1}(1,1)$, $p^{-1}(\infty, \infty)$ are exceptional, that is, each has self-intersection $-1$. Inasmuch as the set of lines $\{L_{ij}, i \neq j, i, j \in S\}$ are permuted transitively by permutation of coordinates in $M_{\mathrm{st}}$, their images in $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$ are also permuted transitively and thus each of the 10 lines is exceptional. The 10 lines of $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$ consist of $p^{-1}(0,0)$, $p^{-1}(1,1)$, $p^{-1}(\infty, \infty)$, and the 7 lifts of the seven lines on $\PP^1 \times \PP^1$:
\[
x = 0, \, x = 1, \, x = \infty, \quad y = 0, \, y = 1, \, y = \infty, \quad x = y
\]
where $x = c(y_1, y_2, y_3, y_4)^{-1}$, $y = c(y_1, y_2, y_3, y_5)^{-1}$.

\textbf{2.---} $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$, $\mu = (\frac{2}{3}, \frac{2}{3}, \frac{2}{3}, \frac{2}{3}, \frac{2}{3})$. Here there are four points in $Q_{0\mathrm{usp}}$ corresponding to the partitions $\{S_i, S^c_i\}$ where $S_i = \{i, 5\}$, $1 \leq i \leq 4$. $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$ is a non-singular manifold by (4.5.1). This $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$ can be obtained from the $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$ of Example 1 by blowing down the four lines $L_{i,5}$ ($1 \leq i \leq 4$). It can be obtained from $\PP^1 \times \PP^1$ by blowing up the point $(\infty, \infty)$ and then blowing down the lines $x = \infty$ and $y = \infty$. The resulting space is $\PP^2$.
\end{example}

\subsection{}
We conclude this section by relating our $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$ to Mumford's when all the $\mu_s$ are rational.

Let $\mathcal{O}(n)$ be the line bundle on $\PP$ of degree $n$, and let $\Omega$ be the canonical line bundle on $\PP$; i.e.\ $\Omega \cong \mathcal{O}(-2)$. For any line bundle $\mathscr{L}$ we write $\mathscr{L}^{\otimes n}$ for the dual line bundle and $\mathscr{L}^{\otimes n} = (\mathscr{L}^{\otimes(-1)})^{\otimes(-n)}$ for $n < 0$.

Let $D$ be the common denominator for $\{\mu_s, s \in S\}$. On $\PP^S$ define the line bundle
\[
\bigotimes_{s \in S} \pr_s^*(\Omega^{\otimes \mu_s \cdot D} \otimes \mathcal{O}(2)^{\otimes D}).
\]
It admits a $\PGL(2)$-action coming from the action on the tangent bundle $\Omega^{\otimes(-1)} \cong \mathcal{O}(2)$. The stable and semi-stable points of $\PP^S$ defined in (4.1) are the same as those defined in Mumford's ``Geometric Invariant Theory'' for the action of $\PGL(2)$ on the linear space of holomorphic sections of this line bundle; $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$ is the underlying topological space of Mumford's ``quotient variety'' for $\PGL(2) \backslash \PP^S$ \cite{Mumford}.


\section{The complex ball}

\subsection{}
Let $V$ be a complex vector space, and $(\,,\,)$ be a Hermitian form on $V$, of signature $(1, \dim V - 1)$. The complex ball $B^+$ in $\PP(V) := \{\text{1-dimensional subspaces of } V\}$ corresponding to $(\,,\,)$ is the set of lines in $V$ which are spanned by a vector $v$ with $(v, v) > 0$. The form $(\,,\,)$ is determined, up to a positive real factor, by $B^+$. The closure $\overline{B^+}$ of $B^+$ in $\PP(V)$ is the set of lines spanned by a non zero vector $v$ such that $(v, v) \geq 0$.

\subsection{}
The hyperbolic angle $\theta \in \RR_+$ between lines $\ell_1, \ell_2 \in B^+$, spanned by vectors $v_1, v_2$, is defined by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:5.2.1}
|(v_1, v_2)| = \cosh(\theta) (v_1, v_1)^{1/2} (v_2, v_2)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
It remains unchanged when $(\,,\,)$ is replaced by a positive multiple. The angle $\theta(\ell_1, \ell_2)$ is a Riemannian distance on $B^+$. It is invariant by the action of the unitary group $\U(V)$ on $B^+$ and renders $B^+$ a Hermitian symmetric space. By continuity we extend the function $\theta(\ell_1, -)$ to a function $\theta(\ell_1, -)$ from $\overline{B^+}$ to $\RR_+ \cup \{\infty\}$: $\theta(\ell_1, \ell) = \infty$ for $\ell \in \partial B^+$.

\subsection{}
Fix $\ell \in \partial B^+$. For $v \neq 0$ in $\ell$, we define a ``distance from $\ell$'' function on $B^+$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:5.3.1}
d_\ell(\ell_1) = |(v, v_1)| / (v_1, v_1)^{1/2} \quad \text{for } \ell_1 \in B^+, \text{ any } v_1 \neq 0 \text{ in } \ell_1;
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:5.3.2}
d_\ell(\ell) = 0, \quad d_\ell(\ell') = \infty \text{ for } \ell' \in \partial B^+, \, \ell' \neq \ell.
\end{equation}
The function $d_\ell$ is continuous and $\geq 0$ on $\overline{B^+} - \{\ell\}$. One has $d_{\lambda \ell}(\ell_1) = |\lambda| \, d_\ell(\ell_1)$, and the family of functions $d_\ell$ ($v \in \ell - \{0\}$) is stable by the stabilizer of $\ell$ in $\U(V)$.

\subsection{}
On $\overline{B^+}$, we will use the coarsest topology for which the functions $d_\ell$ and $\theta(\ell_1, -)$ are continuous. A fundamental system of neighborhoods of $\ell \in B^+$ (resp.\ of $\ell \in \partial B^+$---on which we choose $v \neq 0$) consists of the $V_\varepsilon$ ($\varepsilon > 0$) defined by $V_\varepsilon = \{\ell_1 \in B^+ \mid \theta(\ell, \ell_1) < \varepsilon\}$ (resp.\ $V_\varepsilon = \{\ell_1 \mid \ell_1 = \ell \text{ or } \ell_1 \in B^+ \text{ and } d_\ell(\ell_1) < \varepsilon\}$).

This topology is finer than the one induced by the topology of $\PP(V)$. It induces the usual topology on $B^+$, and the discrete one on $\partial B^+$. It is respected by $\U(V)$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:5.5}
Fix $\ell \in \partial B^+$ and let $A$ be a discrete subgroup of the projective unitary group $\PU(V)$ stabilizing $\ell$ and respecting a ``distance to $\ell$'' function $d_\ell$. If $\ell$ has a neighborhood $V$ (in the topology 5.4) such that $V/A$ is compact, the volume of $(V \cap B^+)/A$ is finite.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The subgroup of $\PU(V)$ respecting $\ell$ and $d_\ell$ is the isomorphic image of the stabilizer $H$ of $v$ in $\U(V)$. It is an extension of the unitary group $\U(v^\perp/v)$ by the unipotent subgroup $N$ of $H$ consisting of the $n \in \U(V)$ respecting the filtration $V \supset v^\perp \supset \CC v \supset 0$ and acting trivially on the successive quotients. The extension is split: $H$ and $N$ act transitively on each horocycle $d_\ell(\cdot) = C$ and the stabilizer of $\ell_1$ is a lifting of $\U(v_1^\perp/v_1)$.

Fix an isotropic vector $v'$ such that $(v, v') = 1$, and let $v_1 = v \cdot t^{-2} - v'$. The line $\ell_1$ spanned by $v_1$ is in $B^+$ on the horocycle $d_{\ell_1} = t$. Let $V'$ be spanned by $v$ and $v'$, and let $V''$ be the orthogonal complement ($\supset v^\perp/v$). Any $h \in H$ fixing $\ell_1$ fixes each point of $V'$; hence the stabilizer of $\ell_1$ in $H$ is $\U(V'') \subset \U(V)$. Let $A \subset \U(V') \subset \U(V)$ be the group of unitary transformations $a(\lambda): v \mapsto \lambda^{-1} v$, $v' \mapsto \lambda v'$ ($\lambda \in \RR_+$) and fixing each point of $V''$. It normalizes $H$: one has $d_\ell \circ a(\lambda) = d_\ell \cdot |\lambda|^{-1} = \lambda d_\ell$. Let $A_R = \{a(\lambda) \mid \lambda < R\}$. The map $g \mapsto g\ell_1$ maps isomorphically $A_R H/\U(V'') = A_R N$ onto $\{\ell_1 \in B^+ \mid d_\ell(\ell_1) < R\}$. The horocycles are closed in $\overline{B^+}$ (for the topology 5.4). The compactness assumption implies that for $R$ small enough, $\{\ell_1 \mid d_\ell(\ell_1) = R\}/A$ is compact. One has
\[
A \backslash a(R) H / \U(V'') \xrightarrow{\sim} \{\ell_1 \mid d_\ell(\ell_1) = R\}/A
\]
and $A$ is hence cocompact in $H$. It remains to evaluate the volume of
\[
A \backslash A_R H / \U(V'') \xrightarrow{\sim} \{\ell_1 \in B^+ \mid d_\ell(\ell_1) < R\}/A
\]
for a volume element on $A_R H / \U(V'')$ which is $A$-$H$-invariant, hence a multiple of the image of $da \cdot dh$ on $A_R H$.
\[
\int_{A \backslash A_R H} da' \, dh = \int_{\lambda \leq R} \int_{A \backslash a(\lambda) H} d(a(\lambda)^{-1} h a(\lambda)) \, d\lambda
\]
and
\[
\int_{A \backslash a(\lambda) H} dh = \int_{A \backslash H} d(a(\lambda)^{-1} h a(\lambda)) = |\det(\Int a(\lambda)^{-1}, \Lie H)| \int_{A \backslash H} dh.
\]
This leads us to evaluate $|\det(\Int a(\lambda)^{-1}, \Lie H)|$. One checks that the character $\det(\Int a(\lambda)^{-1}, \Lie H)$ of $\lambda \in \RR_+^*$ tends to zero for $\lambda \to 0$, and the integral is hence finite.

The computation above is of course exactly the one used to prove that Siegel domains have finite volume (\cite{Borel}, Lemma 1.9).
\end{proof}


\section{Stable points}

\subsection{}
Notation and assumptions (4.0) are in force in this section as well as the assumption $N \geq 4$. Our aim is to investigate the asymptotic behaviour of the section $w_\alpha$ of $B(\alpha)_M$, for $y \in M$ converging to a stable point $m \in M_{\mathrm{st}}$.

Fix $m \in M_{\mathrm{st}}$. To make a local study of $w_\alpha$ near $m$, it will be convenient to identify $\PP$ with $\PP^1$, in such a way that $\infty \notin m(S)$. Let $U$ be an open neighborhood of $m$ in $M_{\mathrm{st}}$, such that $\infty \notin y(S)$ for $y \in U$. On the inverse image $P_U$ of $U$ in $P_M$ (3.1), there is a rank one local system, trivialized at $\infty$ by a section $e$ over $\{\infty\} \times U$, and with monodromy $\alpha$ on $P_U$ for $y \in U \cap M$. A model of it is: the local system of constant multiples of $\prod_{s \in S} (z - y(s))^{\mu_s}$; the $\mu_s$ summing up to an integer, there is no ramification at $\infty$. A trivialisation for large positive real $z$ is provided by the principal determination of $\prod_{s \in S} (z - y(s))^{\mu_s}$; it extends by continuity to a trivialisation $e$ at $\infty$. We normalize the Hermitian form $(\,,\,)$ on $L$ so that $(e, e) = 1$.

For a general $y \in U$, the monodromy of $L_U$ on $P_y$ around $p \in y(S)$ is $\prod_{y(s) = p} \alpha_s = \exp(2\pi i \sum_{y(s) = p} \mu_s)$. The stability of $y$ ensures that $0 < \sum_{y(s) = p} \mu_s \leq 1$, so that this local monodromy is never trivial.

Fiber by fiber, we take as section $\omega$ of $\Omega^1(\sum \mu_s \cdot y(s))(L)$
\[
\omega_y = \prod_{s \in S} (z - y(s))^{-\mu_s} \cdot e \, dz.
\]
On $U \cap M$, it depends holomorphically on $y$. The assumption of stability of $y$ amounts to saying that this form is of the first kind.

\subsection{}
The sheaf $R^1\pi_! L_U$, for $\pi$ the projection $P_U \to U$, is no longer a local system if $m \notin M$. Its fiber at $m$ is $H^1_c(P_m, L_m)$ ($\xrightarrow{\sim} H^1(P_m, L_m)$ by (2.6.1)). For each $h \in H^1_c(P_m, L_m)$, there is hence a unique germ $h^\sim$ of a section of $R^1\pi_! L$ inducing $h$ on $P_m$. The vector space $H^1_c(P_m, L_m)$ being finite dimensional, this provides us on $U$ suitably small, with a map
\begin{equation}\label{eq:6.2.1}
\text{(constant sheaf $H^1_c(P_m, L_m)$)} \hookrightarrow R^1\pi_! L_U.
\end{equation}
This map is compatible with the non degenerate Hermitian form $(\,,\,)$, and hence injective. Compatibility can be checked on the description given below. In particular, each $h \in H^1_c(P_m, L_m)$ defines a horizontal section $h^\sim$ of $R^1\pi_! L$ on $U \cap M$.

We describe $h^\sim$, for $h$ the class of a $L_m$-valued compactly supported $C^\infty$ form $\eta$, whose support is contained in a connected compact $K$ containing $\infty$, and for $U$ connected and small enough for $K$ to be disjoint from $y(S)$ for $y \in U$. On $K \times U \subset P_U$, $L$ is uniquely isomorphic to $\pr_1^*(L_m)$, by an isomorphism which is the identity above $m$. It hence make sense, in each fibre $P_y$, to take the same form $\eta$; it defines $h^\sim$.

\subsection{}
If we apply this to $\omega_m$, we get an horizontal section $\cl(\omega_m)^\sim$ of $R^1\pi_! L_U$ on $U \cap M$. As $(\omega_m, \omega_m) > 0$ (2.20), the same positivity holds for $\cl(\omega_m)^\sim$, and, projecting from $R^1\pi_! L_U$ to $B(\alpha)$, we get a section $\Pcl(\omega_m)^\sim$ of $B^+(\alpha)_M$. We will compare it with the holomorphic section $w_\alpha$, using fiber by fiber the metric (5.2).

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:6.4}
$\theta(w_\alpha, \Pcl(\omega_m)^\sim)$ tends to $0$ for $y \in M$ tending to $m$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
This will result at once from the two statements
\begin{align}
(\omega_y, \omega_y) &\to (\omega_m, \omega_m) \quad \text{for } y \to m; \label{eq:6.4.1} \\
(\omega_y, \cl(\omega_m)^\sim) &\to (\omega_m, \omega_m) \quad \text{for } y \to m. \label{eq:6.4.2}
\end{align}

If it made sense, we would like to say that $\omega_y \to \omega_m$ in the $L^2$-norm, for $y \to m$, but $\omega_y$ and $\omega_m$ do not live in the same space, having different ramification. Let $K_R$ be the complement in $\PP^1$ of discs of radius $R$ around each point of $m(S)$. One assumes $R$ small enough for these discs to be disjoint; on a connected neighborhood $U$ of $y$, small enough for $y(S)$ to stay disjoint from $K_R$, one can compare $\omega_y|_{K_R}$ and $\omega_m|_{K_R}$: on $K_R \times U$, $L \xrightarrow{\sim} \pr_1^* L_m$. There, one has a $C^\infty$ isotopy of $\omega_y$ to $\omega_m$. To prove (6.4.1), it hence suffices to obtain a bound $r$ uniform in $y$, close to $m$, for the $L^2$-norm of the restriction of $\omega_y$ to a disk $D_R$ of radius $R$ around $p \in m(s)$, for any $s \in S$, this bound being such that $r \to 0$ for $R \to 0$.

We may assume $R$ and $U$ small enough so that $y(s) \subset D_{2R}$ if $m(s) \neq p$. This ensures
\[
\prod_{m(s) \neq p} |z - y(s)|^{-\mu_s} < \text{constant} \cdot \prod_{m(s) \neq p} |z - y(s)|^{-\mu_s}
\]
and the required estimate follows from the following lemma.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:6.5}
Let $D$ be a disc of radius $R$, $(a_i)_{i \in I}$ be a family of points of $D$ and $(\mu_i)_{i \in I}$ a family of real numbers, such that $0 < \mu_i < 1$ and $\sum \mu_i < 1$. Then, writing $z = x + iy$,
\[
\int_D \prod_i |z - a_i|^{-2\mu_i} \, dx \, dy < \int_{|z| < R} |z|^{-2\sum \mu_i} \, dx \, dy = \frac{2\pi R^{2(1 - \sum \mu_i)}}{2(1 - \sum \mu_i)}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We may and shall assume $D$ centered at $0$. If the real numbers $p_i$ are such that $1 > \frac{1}{p_i} > \mu_i$ and $\sum \frac{1}{p_i} = 1$, H\"older's inequality gives
\[
\int_D \prod_i |z - a_i|^{-2\mu_i} \, dx \, dy \leq \prod_i \left[ \int_D |z - a_i|^{-2\mu_i p_i} \, dx \, dy \right]^{1/p_i}.
\]
One has that the integral of $|z - a_i|^{-2\mu_i p_i} \, dx \, dy$ is bigger when taken on the disc $D_R(a_i)$ of radius $R$ and center $a_i$ than when taken on $D_R(0)$: results from the fact that $|z - a_i|^{-2\mu_i p_i}$ is bigger on $D_R(a_i) - D_R(0)$ than on $D_R(0) - D_R(a_i)$: one has $|z - a_i| < R$ on $D_R(a_i) - D_R(0)$, and $|z - a_i| \geq R$ on $D_R(0) - D_R(a_i)$.

Using that $\sum 1/p_i = 1$, we have
\[
\int_D \prod_i |z - a_i|^{-2\mu_i} \, dx \, dy \leq \prod_i [2\pi \cdot \ldots]^{1/p_i} = 2\pi \cdot R^{2(1 - \sum \mu_i)} \cdot C^{-1}
\]
with $C = \prod_i (2 - 2\mu_i p_i)^{1/p_i}$. Taking the $p_i$ such that the $\mu_i p_i$ are all equal, i.e.\ $p_i = \mu_i^{-1} \sum \mu_i$, one gets the result.
\end{proof}

\textit{Proof of (6.4.2).---} For each $p \in m(S)$, let $D_p$ be a disc of radius $R$ around $p$, $u_p$ the solution on $D_p^* = D_p - \{p\}$ of $\omega_m = du$ and $\varphi_p$ the characteristic function of $D_p$. The current with compact support $\omega_m - \sum d(\varphi_p u_p)$ can be used to compute $\cl(\omega_m)^\sim$. One gets
\[
(\omega_y, \cl(\omega_m)^\sim) = \int_{P - \bigcup D_p} \omega_y \wedge \overline{\omega_m} - \sum_p \int \omega_y \wedge \overline{d(\varphi_p u_p)}.
\]
For $y \to m$, and $R \to 0$ in such a way that the disc of radius $2R$ around $p$ contains all $y(s)$ for $m(s) = p$, the first integral converges to $(\omega_m, \omega_m)$, while the others each are $O(R^{2\beta})$, $\beta = 1 - \sum_{m(s)=p} \mu_s$. This concludes the proof.
\end{proof}

\subsection{}
In the neighborhood of a stable point $y \in Q_{\mathrm{st}}$, the projection of $\widetilde{Q}_{\mathrm{st}}$ to $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$ has a section. Taking a pull-back by such a section, we get the following:

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:6.8}
For $y \in Q_{\mathrm{st}}$, there exists a neighborhood $V$ of $y$ in $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$ and a horizontal section $b_V$ of $B(\alpha)$ on $Q \cap V$ such that $\theta(b_V(y'), w_\alpha(y')) \to 0$ for $y' \to y$ ($y' \in Q$).
\end{corollary}

For $y \in Q$, the corollary just tells once more that the section $w$ is continuous at $y$.

\subsection{}
A partition $T$ of $S$ is \emph{stable} if for each $C \in T$, $\mu(T)_C := \sum_{s \in C} \mu_s < 1$.

Define $\alpha(T)_C = \exp(2\pi i \mu(T)_C) = \prod_{s \in C} \alpha_s$. For $T$ stable, let $M_T \subset M_{\mathrm{st}}$ be the space of maps $y$ from $S$ to $\PP$, such that $y(s) = y(t)$ if and only if $s$ and $t$ are in the same coset of $T$. It is the inverse image in $M_{\mathrm{st}}$ of a subspace $Q_T$ of $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$. The $M_T$ (resp.\ $Q_T$) for $T$ stable form a partition of $M_{\mathrm{st}}$ (resp.\ $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$) and each $M_T$ (resp.\ $Q_T$) can be identified with the analogue $M(T)$ of $M$ (resp.\ $Q(T)$ of $Q$) for $T$, $\mu(T)$, and its closure with the analogue $M_{\mathrm{st}}(T)$ (resp.\ $Q_{\mathrm{st}}(T)$) of $M_{\mathrm{st}}$ (resp.\ $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$).

Fix $m, U, L$ as in (6.1), with $m \in M_T$. On $M_T \cap U$, the $H^1_c(P_y, L_y)$ form a local system. By (6.2.1), this local system, which is trivial as $U$ is small enough, extends as a trivial sublocal system of $R^1\pi_! L_U$. The $\omega_y$ ($y \in M_T \cap U$) define a non-zero holomorphic section of this local system on $M_T \cap U$. The corresponding ray can be identified with the section $w_{\alpha(T)}$ of $B(\alpha(T))$ on $M(T)$.

On $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$, this gives us in a neighborhood $V$ of $y \in Q_T$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
\item A horizontal sub-ball-bundle of $B(\alpha)$, which extends as a constant horizontal bundle on $V$. Its restriction to $Q_T$ can be identified with $B(\alpha(T))$ on $Q(T)$.
\item Via this identification, the horizontal section $b_V$ of (6.8) is the horizontal section through $w_{\alpha(T)}(y)$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:6.10}
If $\mu$ satisfies condition INT of (3.11): $0 < \mu_s < 1$, $\sum \mu_s = 2$ and for all $s \neq t$ in $S$ such that $\mu_s + \mu_t < 1$, $(1 - \mu_s - \mu_t)^{-1}$ is an integer, then any subset $C \subset S$ for which $\sum_{s \in C} \mu_s < 1$ has at most three elements, and for any such subset with at least two elements, $(1 - \sum_{s \in C} \mu_s)^{-1}$ is an integer.
\end{proposition}

The condition INT implies that $\mu_s + \mu_t$ is always $> 1/2$ for $s \neq t$. Averaging over the pairs in $C \subset S$, we get that for $\card(C) \geq 2$, the mean of the $\mu_s$ ($s \in C$) is $> 1/4$. From $\sum_{s \in C} \mu_s < 1$ there results $\card(C) < 4$.

The case $\card(C) = 2$ is trivial by INT. Suppose $C = \{a, b, c\}$. The relations
\[
1 - \mu_a - \mu_b = \frac{1}{n_{ab}}, \quad 1 - \mu_b - \mu_c = \frac{1}{n_{bc}}, \quad 1 - \mu_c - \mu_a = \frac{1}{n_{ca}}
\]
sum up to $3 - 2(\mu_a + \mu_b + \mu_c) = \frac{1}{n_{ab}} + \frac{1}{n_{bc}} + \frac{1}{n_{ca}}$, hence $\frac{1}{n_{ab}} + \frac{1}{n_{bc}} + \frac{1}{n_{ca}} > 1$.

We now use the elementary fact that a triple of integers whose reciprocals have their sum strictly greater than 1 must be one of
\[
(2, 2, n), \quad (2, 3, 3), \quad (2, 3, 4), \quad (2, 3, 5).
\]
The excesses of the sums over 1 are respectively $1/n$, $1/6$, $1/12$, $1/30$.

It follows at once that $(1 - \mu_a - \mu_b - \mu_c)^{-1}$ is an integer.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:6.11}
If $\mu$ satisfies INT and $T$ is a stable partition of $S$ (cf.\ (6.9)), the family $\mu(T)$ defined by
\[
\mu(T)_C = \sum_{s \in C} \mu_s \quad (C \in T)
\]
again satisfies INT.
\end{corollary}


\section{Semi-stable points}

\subsection{}
The notation and assumptions (4.0) are in force in this section; we assume also $N \geq 4$. Let $(S(1), S(2))$ be a partition of $S$, such that $\sum_{s \in S(i)} \mu_s := 1$. We want to investigate the asymptotic behavior on $Q$ of $w_\alpha$, for $y$ converging to a point in $Q_{0\mathrm{usp}}$ of type $(S(1), S(2))$.

Fix $a \in S(1)$ and $b \in S(2)$, identify $\PP$ with $\PP^1$, and let $W$ be the space of $y \in \PP^S$ such that $y(a) = 0$, $y(b) = \infty$, and
\[
\sup \{|y(s)| \mid s \in S(1)\} < \inf \{|y(s)| \mid s \in S(2)\}
\]
(as in section 4 where we wrote $V$ instead of $W$). We put $A(y) := \sup \{|y(s)| \mid s \in S(1)\}$, $B(y) = \inf\{|y(s)| \mid s \in S(2)\}$, $C(y) = A(y)/B(y)$. The quotient map $\pi: W \to Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$ sends a point $y$ with $C(y) = 0$ to the semistable point $J$ of type $(S(1), S(2))$ and identifies $(W - \pi^{-1}(J))/\CC^*$ with a punctured neighborhood of $J$. One has convergence of $\pi(y)$ to $J$ if and only if $C(y)$ converges to $0$.

On $P_W$, there is, up to a unique isomorphism, a unique rank one local system $L$, trivialized on the annulus $A(y) < |z| < B(y)$ by a section $e$, and which for $y \in M$, has fiberwise monodromy $\alpha$ (hence trivial monodromy around the annulus, as $\prod_{s \in S(i)} \alpha_s = 1$). A model of it is the local system of constant multiples of
\[
\prod_{s \in S(1)} (z - y(s))^{\mu_s} \cdot \prod_{s \in S(2)} \left(1 - \frac{z}{y(s)}\right)^{\mu_s}.
\]
We normalize the Hermitian form $(\,,\,)$ on $L$ by $(e, e) = 1$.

For any $y$, fix $R$ such that $A(y) < R < B(y)$, and let $\gamma$ be the $L$-valued homology class represented by the cycle $|z| = R$ in $P_y$, positively oriented and provided with $e$, the trivializing section of $L$. It can also be viewed as a class in $H^{\mathrm{lf}}_1(P_y, L_y)$. For $y = j$, defined by $j(S(1)) = 0$, $j(S(2)) = \infty$, it is a generator of this $H^{\mathrm{lf}}_1$. The $\gamma$ provide a horizontal section of $R^1\pi_! L$. Since $(\omega_j, \omega_j) = 0$, the line $(\omega_j)$ spanned by $\omega_j$ is in the boundary of the ball $B^+(\alpha)$ and the functions $d_{\omega_j}$ of (5.3) provide a horizontal family of ``distance to $\PP \omega_j$'' functions. We will use them to compare $\PP \omega_j$ with the holomorphic section $w_\alpha$ of $B^+(\alpha)$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:7.2}
For $y$ variable in $W$, if $C(y)$ tends to $0$, the $d_{\omega_j}(w_\alpha(y))$ tend to $0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fiber by fiber $w_\alpha$ is the line spanned by the cohomology class of
\[
\omega_y = \prod_{s \in S(1)} (z - y(s))^{-\mu_s} \cdot \prod_{s \in S(2)} \left(1 - \frac{z}{y(s)}\right)^{-\mu_s} \cdot dz \cdot e.
\]
The form $\omega_y$, as a function of $y$, is invariant by the action of $\CC^*$ on $(W, L)$. It hence suffices to treat the case where $A(y) \to 0$, $B(y) \to \infty$. In that case, the numerator of $d_{\omega_j}(w_\alpha)$ clearly tends to $1$: it is the absolute value of
\[
\frac{1}{2\pi i} \int_{|z|=R} \prod_{s \in S(1)} (z - y(s))^{-\mu_s} \cdot \prod_{s \in S(2)} \left(1 - \frac{z}{y(s)}\right)^{-\mu_s} \cdot \frac{dz}{z}
\]
which tends to $1$.

We have to prove that $(\omega_y, \omega_y) \to \infty$. Indeed,
\[
(\omega_y, \omega_y) = \int_{\CC} \prod_{s \in S(1)} |z - y(s)|^{-2\mu_s} \cdot \prod_{s \in S(2)} \left|1 - \frac{z}{y(s)}\right|^{-2\mu_s} |dz \wedge d\overline{z}|
\]
and this integral is bounded below by
\[
\int_{A(y) < |z| < B(y)} |z|^{-2} |dz \wedge d\overline{z}| = 2\pi \cdot 2 |\log C(y)|.
\]
\end{proof}

On $Q$, the proposition (7.2) has the following corollary:
\begin{corollary}\label{cor:7.3}
For each semi-stable point $y$ of $Q_{0\mathrm{usp}}$, there is on a neighborhood $V$ of $y$ a horizontal family of functions $d$ on the balls $B(\alpha)_{y'}$ ($y' \in V \cap Q$)---each a ``distance to a point of $\partial B(\alpha)_y$'' function---such that $d(w_\alpha(y')) \to 0$ for $y' \to y$.
\end{corollary}


\section{Extending $\widetilde{w}$ by continuity}

Let $X, Y$ be complex algebraic varieties which are separated as algebraic varieties, i.e.\ which are Hausdorff in their complex topology.

\subsection{}
One knows that any quasi-finite map $f: X \to Y$---i.e.\ a map whose fibers are finite---admits a factorization $X \xrightarrow{j} \mathcal{X} \xrightarrow{\bar{f}} Y$, with $j$ an open embedding, and $\bar{f}$ a finite map (= quasi-finite and proper). If $X$ and $Y$ are connected and normal, of the same dimension, there is an unique such factorization for which $\mathcal{X}$ is normal. One calls this $\mathcal{X}$ the normalization of $Y$ in $X$.

For more general spaces, and for some maps with totally disconnected but possibly infinite fibers, a simple topological generalization of this construction has been given by R. H. Fox \cite{Fox}.

The spaces $X$ and $Y$ are taken to be Hausdorff and locally connected. A continuous map $f: X \to Y$ is called a \emph{spread} if, for any $x$ in $X$, one obtains a fundamental system of neighborhoods of $x$ by taking, for each neighborhood $V$ of $f(x)$, the connected component of $f^{-1}(V)$ containing $x$. A spread $f$ is called \emph{complete} if, for any $y \in Y$,
\[
f^{-1}(y) \cong \varprojlim \pi_0(f^{-1}(V)),
\]
the projective limit being taken on the neighborhoods $V$ of $y$.

Fox proves that each spread $f: X \to Y$ can be embedded in a complete spread $\bar{f}: \bar{X} \to Y$ with the following universal property: any commutative diagram of solid arrows
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
X \arrow[r] \arrow[d, "f"'] & X' \arrow[d, "f'"] \\
Y \arrow[r, equal] & Y \arrow[ul, phantom, "\circlearrowleft"]
\end{tikzcd}
\]
with $f'$ a complete spread, can be completed as shown. For any $y \in Y$, one has
\[
\bar{f}^{-1}(y) \cong \varprojlim \pi_0(f^{-1}(V)),
\]
the projective limit being taken on the neighborhoods $V$ of $y$. It is clear from the universal property that $\bar{X}$ is unique up to unique isomorphism, and functorial. We will call it the \emph{completion of $X$ over $Y$}.

It contains $X$ as a dense subspace, and the topology of $X$ is induced by that of $\bar{X}$. The space $\bar{X}$ is locally connected.

\subsection{}
We define a space $X$ to be connected if it is non-empty and has no proper open and closed subset.

A map $f: X \to Y$ is called a \emph{covering map} if each $y \in Y$ has a neighborhood $V$ for which there exists a discrete set $D$ (possibly empty), and a commutative diagram
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
D \times V \arrow[r] \arrow[dr] & f^{-1}(V) \arrow[d] \\ & V
\end{tikzcd}
\]
If $Y$ is connected and $X$ non-empty, such a map is onto.

A map $f: X \to Y$ is called a \emph{local homeomorphism} at $x \in X$ if $x$ has a neighborhood $U$ which maps homeomorphically onto a neighborhood of $f(x)$. The map $f$ is called a local homeomorphism if this holds at all points $x \in X$.

Assume that $X$ is a connected covering space of a connected locally simply connected open subset $U$ of $Y$. The composed map $X \to U \hookrightarrow Y$ is then a spread. Assume further that each $y$ in $Y$ has a fundamental system $\mathscr{V}_y$ of open neighborhoods such that
\begin{align}
&\text{for $V$ in $\mathscr{V}_y$, $V \cap U$ is connected;} \label{eq:8.2.1} \\
&\text{for $V' \subset V''$ in $\mathscr{V}_y$, $\pi_1(V' \cap U) \to \pi_1(V'' \cap U)$.} \label{eq:8.2.2}
\end{align}
As $f$ is a local homeomorphism, $X$ is open in $\bar{X}$. As $X$ is a covering of $U$, one has further $\bar{X} = f^{-1}(U)$.

Fix a base point $o \in U$. One knows that the functor $(g: \widetilde{U} \to U) \mapsto g^{-1}(0)$ is an equivalence of the category of coverings of $U$ with that of discrete sets on which $\pi_1(U, o)$ acts. In particular, the connected components of $\widetilde{U}$ correspond to the orbits of $\pi_1(U, o)$ on $g^{-1}(o)$. For $W$ open in $U$, connected and containing $0$, the same applies to $W$ and the restriction functor $(g: \widetilde{U} \to U) \mapsto (g^{-1}(W) \to W)$ from coverings of $U$ to coverings of $W$ corresponds to the functor: restriction of the action of $\pi_1(U, o)$ to $\pi_1(W, o)$.

In particular, if $\widetilde{U}$ corresponds to $\pi_1(U, o)/K$, $\pi_0(g^{-1}W) = \pi_1(W, o) \backslash \pi_1(U, o) / K$. This applies to $X$. If a lifting $\tilde{o}$ of $o$ is chosen in $X$, the map $\pi_1(U, 0) \to f^{-1}(o): \sigma \mapsto \sigma \cdot \tilde{o}$ identifies $f^{-1}(o)$ with a suitable homogeneous space $\pi_1(U, o)/K$ of $\pi_1(U, 0)$.

Fix $y \in Y$, and a neighborhood $V \in \mathscr{V}_y$. Let us assume at first that $o \in V$. The decomposition group $D_V$ is then defined as the image of $\pi_1(V \cap U, 0)$ in $\pi_1(U, 0)$ and one has
\[
\pi_0(f^{-1}(V \cap U)) = D_V \backslash \pi_1(U, o) / K.
\]
For $V' \subset V$ in $\mathscr{V}_y$, (8.2.1) and (8.2.2) imply that $\pi_0(f^{-1}(V' \cap U)) \to \pi_0(f^{-1}(V \cap U))$ hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:8.2.3}
f^{-1}(y) = D_V \backslash \pi_1(U, o) / K.
\end{equation}
For a $\sigma \in \pi_1(U, o)/K$, $\sigma \cdot \tilde{o}$ is a lifting of $o$ in the connected component $f^{-1}(V)_\sigma$ of $f^{-1}(V)$ corresponding to the double coset $D_V \sigma K$. If we use it as a base point, the fibre at $0$ of the covering $f^{-1}(V)_\sigma$ of $V \cap U$ is identified with $D_V \sigma K / K \cong D_V / D_V \cap \sigma K \sigma^{-1}$. To the component $f^{-1}(V)_\sigma$ there corresponds a point $\tilde{x}$ in $f^{-1}(y)$. The $f^{-1}(V)_\sigma \cap f^{-1}(V')$, for $V'$ a neighborhood of $y$, are the trace on $X$ of a fundamental system of neighborhood of $\tilde{x}$ in $\bar{X}$.

For $o$ not necessarily in $V$, one chooses $o'$ in $V \cap U$ and a path $p$ from $o$ to $o'$ in $U$. This path lifts as a path from $\tilde{o}$ to some lifting $\tilde{o}'$ of $o'$. It defines an isomorphism of $\pi_1(U, 0)$ with $\pi_1(U, o')$ via which the above constructions, which make sense for $o'$, can be pushed back into $\pi_1(U, o)$. For instance, the decomposition group $D_V \subset \pi_1(U, o)$ is defined as the image of the decomposition group $D_V \subset \pi_1(U, o')$. It depends on the path $p$. One still has (8.2.3).

(8.2.4) A covering map $f: X \to U$ is normal if the $\pi_1(U, o)$ homogeneous space $f^{-1}(o)$ is isomorphic to a homogeneous space $\pi_1(U, o)/K$, with $K$ a normal subgroup. If $f: X \to U$ is a normal covering map, the group $\pi_1(U, 0)$ acts on $X$ by deck-transformations, and this action extends to $\bar{X}$ by functoriality. We leave to the reader to check that, under the conditions (8.2.1), (8.2.2), one has
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\pi_1(U, o) \backslash \bar{X} \xrightarrow{\sim} Y$;
\item for each $y \in Y$, the stabilizer in $\pi_1(U, o)$ of a point $\tilde{x} \in \bar{f}^{-1}(y)$ is the conjugate of the decomposition group $D_V$ determined by a path in $U$ corresponding to $\tilde{x}$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}
Let $K$ denote the kernel of the action of $\pi_1(U, o)$ on $X$. Then $\pi_1(U, o)/K$ acts properly discontinuously on $X$ but not necessarily on $\bar{X}$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{}
Let $w$ be a continuous function from $X$ to a topological space $B$. We assume $B$ regular, i.e.\ that any neighborhood of any point contains a closed neighborhood. The function $w$ then extends as a continuous function on $\bar{X}$ if and only if, for any $\tilde{y} \in \bar{Y}$, $w(x)$ has a limit for $x \to \tilde{y}$ in $X$ (convergence of $w$ on the filter of traces on $X$ of neighborhoods of $\tilde{y}$ in $\bar{X}$).

In the situation (8.2) (with $o \in V$ for simplicity), one can treat $w$ as a multivalued function on $U$. If $\tilde{x}$ in $f^{-1}(y)$ corresponds to the connected component $f^{-1}(V)_\sigma$ of $f^{-1}(V)$, the restriction of $w$ to $f^{-1}(V)_\sigma$ is a multivalued function $w_\sigma$ on $V \cap U$. That $w(x)$ has a limit for $x \to \tilde{x}$ means that there is $b$ in $B$, and for each neighborhood $W$ of $b$ a neighborhood $V'$ of $y$, such that all determinations of $w_\sigma(u)$, for $u \in V' \cap U$, are in $W$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:8.4}
If we take for $Y$ the unit disk $D \subset \CC$, for $U$ the punctured unit disk $D^*$ and for $X$ the universal covering of $D^*$, the completion of $X$ over $D$ is deduced from $X$ by adding one point above $0$: $\bar{X} = X \cup \{0\}$. For $\alpha$, real $> 0$, the function $z^\alpha$ has a limit for $z \to 0$ in $X$, but it has no limit for non real $\alpha$, with $\re(\alpha) > 0$. Similarly, $z^\alpha \log z$ has no limit.
\end{example}

\subsection{}
With the assumptions (8.2.1) and (8.2.2) on $(Y, U)$, let $\mathscr{B}$ be the flat fiber space on $U$ defined by an action $\theta$ of $\pi_1(U, o)$ on a regular topological space $B$, and let $(X, o)$ be the smallest covering of $(U, o)$ on which $\mathscr{B}$ becomes trivial: one has $f^{-1}(o) = \pi_1(U, o)/\Ker(\theta)$. A continuous section $w$ of $\mathscr{B}$ defines by pull-back to $X$ an equivariant continuous map from $X$ to $B$ (and conversely). We write $w$ for this map. When does it extend to $\bar{X}$? Unravelling (8.3), we find that $w$ has a limit for $x \to \tilde{x}$ above $y$ if and only if there is a horizontal section $b$ of $\mathscr{B}$ over the trace on $U$ of a neighborhood $V \in \mathscr{V}_y$ of $y$, toward which $w$ converges for $y' \to y$ ($y' \in U$), in the following sense. For $y_1 \in V \cap U$ and $W_1$ a neighborhood of $b(y_1)$ in the fibre $\mathscr{B}_{y_1}$ of $\mathscr{B}$ at $y_1$, there is a neighborhood $V'$ of $y$ such that for any $y_2 \in V' \cap U$, and for $W_2$ a neighborhood of $b(y_2)$ in the fiber of $\mathscr{B}$ at $y_2$, obtained by horizontal transport of $W_1$ along any path in $V \cap U$ from $y_1$ to $y_2$, one has $w(y_2) \in W_2$. This holds if $b$ has a system of horizontal neighborhoods $W$ which are fiber arbitrarily small, such that $w(y_1)$ is in $W$ for $y_1$ close enough to $y$. Of course, $b$ defines the limit of $w$.

In the rest of section 8, the notation and assumptions (4.0) as well as the assumption $N \geq 4$ are in force.

\subsection{}
As in (3.8), fix a base point $o \in Q$, and let $p: (\widetilde{Q}, o) \to (Q, o)$ be the smallest covering of $(Q, o)$ on which the flat projective space bundle $B(\alpha)_Q$ becomes trivial. For $Q \subset Y \subset Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$ and $Y$ open in $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$, the composed map $\widetilde{Q} \to Y$ is a spread. We will write $\widetilde{Y}$ for the completion of $\widetilde{Q}$ over $Y$. The assumptions (8.2.1) and (8.2.2) are satisfied by $(Y, Q)$.

It follows from (8.2.4) that
\begin{align}
\pi_1(Q, o) &\text{ acts on } \widetilde{Q}_{\mathrm{sst}}; \label{eq:8.6.1} \\
\pi_1(Q, o) \backslash \widetilde{Q}_{\mathrm{sst}} &= Q_{\mathrm{sst}}. \label{eq:8.6.2}
\end{align}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:8.7}
The map $\widetilde{w}$, of (3.8) extends as a continuous map from $\widetilde{Q}_{\mathrm{sst}}$ to the closed ball $\overline{B^+(\alpha)_o}$, provided with the topology (5.4). The inverse image of $\partial B^+(\alpha)_o$ consists of the stable points.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We are in the situation envisioned in (8.5), if we take for $\mathscr{B}$ the flat fiber space with fiber $\overline{B^+(\alpha)}$, completing $B^+(\alpha)$. That the extendability criterion of (8.6) applies is (6.8) for $y$ stable and (7.3) for $y$ semi-stable. Further, the limit occurring in criterion (8.5) lies in $\partial B^+(\alpha)_o$ if and only if $y$ is in $Q_{0\mathrm{usp}}$.
\end{proof}

The extended map $\widetilde{Q}_{\mathrm{sst}} \to \overline{B^+(\alpha)_o}$ is also denoted $\widetilde{w}$. It is $\pi_1(Q, o)$-equivariant.

\subsection{}
For $T$ a stable partition of $S$ (cf.\ (6.9)), the system of the $Q_{T'}$, for $T'$ finer than $T$, is topologically constant along $Q_T$, and the inverse image $\widetilde{Q}_T$ of $Q_T$ in $\widetilde{Q}_{\mathrm{sst}}$ is hence a covering of $Q_T$ ($\cong Q_*(T)$). By (6.9), on each connected component $\widetilde{Q}_T^c$ of $\widetilde{Q}_T$, the flat ball bundle $B(\alpha(T))^+$ trivializes, and can be identified with a flat sub-ball bundle of the constant bundle $B(\alpha)^+$, in such a way that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:8.8.1}
\widetilde{w}|_{\widetilde{Q}_T^c} = w_{\alpha(T)}.
\end{equation}
From (3.9), we hence get:
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:8.9}
For any component $\widetilde{Q}_T^c$ of $\widetilde{Q}_T$, $\widetilde{w}|_{\widetilde{Q}_T^c}$ is an \\'etale map from $\widetilde{Q}_T^c$ to a sub-ball of $B(\alpha)^+$.
\end{proposition}


\section{Codimension 1 \\'etaleness}

In this section, the notation and assumptions (4.0) as well as the assumption $N \geq 4$ are continued.

Let $T$ be a stable partition of $S$ (cf.\ (6.9)), with $\card T = \card S - 1$; one coset of $T$ has two elements and all others only one. Our aim in this section is to describe the flat bundle $B(\alpha)_Q$ over $Q$ (cf.\ (3.7)) and the holomorphic section $w_\alpha$ in the neighborhood of a point in $Q_T \subset Q_{\mathrm{st}}$ (cf.\ (6.9)).

\subsection{}
The subspace $Q_T$ of $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$, defined in (6.9), is a locally closed purely one codimensional complex submanifold of the complex manifold $Q_{\mathrm{sst}}$ and $Q \cap Q_T$ is open in $Q_{\mathrm{st}}$. The monodromy around $Q_T$ is the following conjugacy class in $\pi_1(Q)$.

Let $\varphi: D \to Q \cup Q_T$ be an embedding of the unit disc with $\varphi(D)$ transversal to $Q_T$ and $\varphi^{-1}(Q_T) = \{0\}$; $\varphi$ induces an embedding of $D^* := D - \{0\}$ in $Q$. The fundamental group $\pi_1(D^*)$ is canonically isomorphic to $\ZZ$, generated by the loop $[0, 1] \to D^*: t \mapsto z_0 \cdot \exp(2\pi i t)$. The decomposition group at $\varphi(0)$ is the image of $\pi_1(D^*)$ in $\pi_1(Q)$ and the monodromy around $Q_T$ is defined as the image of the positive generator of $\pi_1(D^*)$. Both are well defined up to conjugacy and independent of $\varphi$. Let us fix a base point $o$ in $Q$. The fundamental group $\pi_1(Q, o)$ acts on $B(\alpha)_o$. We will compute the action of the monodromy around $Q_T$.

Let $\{s_1, s_2\}$ be the two element coset of $T$ and choose additional elements $s_3, s_4 \in S$. We assume that $\PP$ is the standard projective line $\PP^1$ and we choose in it two distinct points $b, c \neq 0$. Let $M_0$ be the space of injective maps $y: S \to \PP$ with $y(s_1) = 0$, $y(s_2) = b$, $y(s_4) = c$. Let $M_{0T}$ be the space of maps $S \to \PP$ with $y(s) = y(t)$ if and only if $s$ and $t$ are in the same coset of $T$, and $y(s_1) = y(s_2) = 0$, $y(s_3) = b$, $y(s_4) = c$. The quotient map $M_{\mathrm{st}} \to Q_{\mathrm{st}}$ induces isomorphisms $M_0 \xrightarrow{\sim} Q$, $M_{0T} \xrightarrow{\sim} Q_T$ and $M_0 \cup M_{0T} \xrightarrow{\sim} Q \cup Q_T$. We will work on $M_0 \cup M_{0T}$, rather than on $Q \cup Q_T$.

The monodromy around $Q_T$ is represented by the following loop in $M_0$: it starts from a point $y_0$ with, for some $r$, $y_0(S_1)$ in the disc $D_r$ of radius $r$ around $0$ and the $y_0(s_i)$ ($i \neq 1, 2$) outside of this disc; it is given by the map from $[0, 1]$ to $M_0$, $t \mapsto y_t$, with $y_t(s_i)$ constant for $i \neq 2$, and $y_t(s_2) = y_0(s_2) \cdot \exp(2\pi i t)$.

Above this loop, one can construct on $P_{y_t}$ a local system $L$ with fiberwise monodromy $\alpha$, as follows: we fix a base point $d$ on $\PP$, outside of $y_0(S)$ and of $D_r$, and take for $L$ the local system trivialized on the constant section $d$ and with the required monodromy. The action of monodromy on $B(\alpha)_{y_0} = \PP H^1(P_{y_0}, L)$ is deduced from the action of monodromy on $H^1(P_{y_0}, L)$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:9.2}
In a suitable basis of $\PP H^1(P_{y_0}, L)$, the above action of the monodromy around $Q_T$ is given by a diagonal matrix $\diag(\alpha_1 \alpha_2, 1, \ldots, 1)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Set $S_1 = \{s_1, s_2\}$ and $S_2 = S - S_1$. Let $T_1, T_2$ be trees as in (2.5) with the vertices of $T_i$ in $S_i$ ($i = 1, 2$) and let $\beta: T_1 \amalg T_2 \hookrightarrow \PP$ be an embedding with $\beta|_S = y_0$. We may and shall assume that $\beta|_{T_1}$ is the straight line segment from $y_0(s_1) = 0$ to $y_0(s_2)$ and that $\beta(T_2)$ is outside of the disc $D_r$. For each (open) edge $a$ of $T_1$ or $T_2$, we choose one of its orientations, and a non zero section $t(a)$ of $\beta^*L$ above it. As in (2.4), (2.5), each edge then defines a closed $L$-valued current $t(a) \cdot \beta|_a$, and the cohomology classes of those currents form a basis of $H^1(P_{y_0}, L)$. It is the basis we will use.

To transport our basis elements around the loop $y_t$ ($0 \leq t \leq 1$) horizontally, it suffices to deform $\beta$ with $\beta_t|_S = y_t$, dragging along the $t(a)$. We will keep $\beta_t|_{T_2}$ fixed and take for $\beta_t|_{T_1}$ the family of straight line segments from $y_t(s_1) = 0$ to $y_t(s_2) = y_0(s_2) \cdot \exp(2\pi i t)$. Outside of $D_r \subset \PP$, the fibers $P_{y_t}$ and $L$ remain constant, and the $t(a) \cdot \beta_t|_a$ for $a$ an edge of $T_2$ are, as currents, independent of $t$. It follows that the monodromy is trivial on the $t(a) \cdot \beta_t|_a$ for $a$ an edge of $T_2$.

Let $a$ be the unique edge of $T_1$. When $\beta_t(a)$ ($0 \leq t \leq 1$) has made a complete turn around $0$, $t(a)$ is multiplied by $\alpha_1 \alpha_2$. Indeed, if $p_t(x): [0, 1] \to \PP$ is a path from the base point $d$ on $\PP$ to a point $d_t$ on $\beta_t(a)$, and if this path deforms with $t$ so as to always avoid $\beta_t(S)$, one can take $t(a)$ to be obtained at $d_t$ by transporting along $p_t$ the trivialising section $e$ of $L$ at $d$. As the following picture shows, $p_1$ can be chosen to differ from $p_0$ by a loop around $y_0(s_1)$ and $y_0(s_2)$. Around this loop, $L$ has monodromy $\alpha_1 \alpha_2$, and the claim follows.

\[
\begin{array}{cc}
\text{$t = 0$:} & \begin{matrix} y_0(s_1) & y_0(s_2) & d \\ \bullet & \bullet & \bullet \\ ) & | & / \end{matrix} \\[2em]
\text{$t$ small:} & \begin{matrix} & y_t(s_2) & \\ & \bullet & \\ y_0(s_1) & | & d \\ \bullet & | & \bullet \end{matrix} \\[2em]
\text{$t = 1$:} & \begin{matrix} y_0(s_1) & y_0(s_2) & d \\ \bullet & \bullet & \bullet \\ ) & | & / \end{matrix}
\end{array}
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rmk:9.3}
In the computation (9.2), we have made use of the assumption $\mu_1 + \mu_2 < 1$ only in the weaker form: each $\mu_i < 1$. In the case $\mu_1 + \mu_2 > 1$, the monodromy when $s_2$ turns around $s_1$ would be given by the same formula, but the basis $e$ in which it holds would have $(e_1, e_1) > 0$ instead of $(e_1, e_1) < 0$: it would be a pseudo-reflection with respect to a hyperplane not meeting the ball $B^+(\alpha)$.

More precisely, one checks that if the section $t(a)$ of $L$, for $a$ the only edge of $T_1$, is chosen of length one: $(t(a), t(a)) = 1$, then
\[
(e_1, e_1) = 2\pi i \cdot \frac{2}{1 - \alpha_1 \alpha_2} (1 + \mu_1 + \mu_2 - 1)
\]
We will not need this formula.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{rmk:9.4}
For later computations, it will be more convenient to work with the basis of the dual space $H^{\mathrm{lf}}_1(P - y_0(S), L^\vee)$ to $H^1(P - y_0(S), L)$ given by a choice of non zero sections $t^\vee(a)$ of $\beta^*L^\vee$ above the edges of $T_1$ or $T_2$. The same arguments as in (9.2), show that on such a basis of the dual space, monodromy around $Q_T$ acts by $\diag((\alpha_1 \alpha_2)^{-1}, 1, \ldots, 1)$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{}
Fix $y \in Q_T$. We will describe the section $w_\alpha$ of $B(\alpha)_Q$ near $y$. A neighborhood of $y$ in $Q \cup Q_T$ and local coordinates are chosen as follows. First, one replaces $Q \cup Q_T$ by $M_0 \cup M_{0T}$, as in (9.1). It is convenient to choose $b$ and $c$, in the definition of $M_0$, so that $\infty \notin y(S)$ and that the discs of radius 1 around the points in $y(S)$ are disjoint. We do so. The chosen neighborhood $U$ is then identified with the space of $m \in M_0 \cup M_{0T}$ such that $|m(s) - y(s)| < 1$. The chosen coordinates are the $m(s) - y(s)$ ($s \neq s_1, s_2, s_4$). If $S' := S - \{s_1, s_2, s_3, s_4\}$, this system of coordinates identifies $U$ with $D \times D^{S'}$, and $U \cap M_0$ with $D^* \times D^{S'}$.

We choose $L$ as in (9.1), using a base point $d$ on $\PP$ at distance $> 1$ from $y(S)$---for instance $d$ a large positive real number. In $U \cap M_0$, we choose a base point $m_0$---for instance with $m_0(s_2) = -1$ and $m_0(s) = y(s)$ for $s \neq s_2$. We view $w_\alpha$ as a multivalued map from $U \cap M_0$ to $B(\alpha)_{m_0} = \PP H^1(\PP - m_0(S), L)$, and on $H^1(\PP - m_0(S), L)$ we use the coordinates provided by a basis (9.4) of the dual space. The multivalued map $w_\alpha$ then lifts to the multivalued map with values in $\CC^{N-3}$ having as coordinates (up to multiplicative constants)
\[
\int_{C_{s_1}} t^\vee(z) \cdot \prod_{s \in S} (z - m(s))^{-\mu_s} \cdot dz
\]
as first coordinate and as the other coordinates the integrals
\[
\int \prod_{s \in S} (z - m(s))^{-\mu_s} \cdot dz
\]
taken along a path remaining outside the disc $|z| < 1$ from one $m(s)$ to another ($s \neq s_1, s_2$). When $m(s_2)$ turns once around $m(s_1)$, the first coordinate gets multiplied by $(\alpha_1 \alpha_2)^{-1} = \exp(2\pi i (1 - \mu_1 - \mu_2))$ while the others regain their value (9.4). This allows us to write the first coordinate as $(m(s_2) - m(s_1))^{1 - \mu_1 - \mu_2} I(m)$, with $I(m)$ an ordinary function on $U \cap M_0$. When $m$ tends to $m_1 \in M_{0T}$, $I(m)$ tends to the limit
\[
\int_{C_{s_1}} \prod_{s \neq s_1, s_2} (z - m(s))^{-\mu_s} \cdot z^{-\mu_1 - \mu_2} \, dz,
\]
which is non zero (2.17.1), hence $I(m)$ extends as a holomorphic function on $U$, invertible on $U \cap M_{0T}$. The other coordinates extend as holomorphic functions on $U$, having as restriction to $U \cap M_{0T}$ integrals
\[
\int z^{-\mu_1 - \mu_2} \cdot \prod_{s \neq s_1, s_2} (z - m(s))^{-\mu_s} \, dz.
\]
By (3.9) applied to $\mu(T)$, these functions on $U \cap M_{0T}$ are the projective coordinates of an \\'etale map from $U \cap M_{0T}$ to the projective $[(N - 2) - 3]$-space (cf.\ (8.9)).

\subsection{}
In suitable local coordinates, the multivalued map $w_\alpha$ from $U \cap M_0$ to $B(\alpha)_{m_0}$ has the form
\[
(z_0, z) \mapsto (z_0^{1 - \mu_1 - \mu_2} I(z_0, z), J(z_0, z));
\]
here, $(z_0, z)$ are local coordinates on $U$ coming from the product decomposition above, $U \cong D \times D^n$, $U \cap M_0 \cong D^* \times D^n$; the local coordinates on the image of $U$ in $B(\alpha)_{m_0}$ are selected on an open set $V$ with $V \cong D \times D^n$ and $\{0\} \times D^n$ lying in a hyperplane; and finally, $I: D \times D^n \to \CC$ and $J: D \times D^n \to D^n$ are holomorphic maps, with $I(0, z) \neq 0$ and $J(0, z): D^n \to D^n$ \\'etale.

At a point of $\{0\} \times D^n \subset D \times D^n$, the holomorphic functions $z_0 \cdot I(z_0, z)^{1/(1 - \mu_1 - \mu_2)}$ and $J(z_0, z)_i$ ($1 \leq i \leq n$) form a system of coordinates. In those coordinates, the multivalued map $w_\alpha$ is
\[
(z_0, z_1, \ldots, z_n) \mapsto (z_0^{1 - \mu_1 - \mu_2}, z_1, \ldots, z_n).
\]
Suppose $1 - \mu_1 - \mu_2$ is rational, set $1 - \mu_1 - \mu_2 = k/\ell$, in reduced terms. If $\widetilde{D}$ is another copy of the disc, with coordinate $u$, mapping to $D$ by $u \mapsto u^\ell$, the pull back to $\widetilde{D} \times D^n$ of the multivalued map above is the holomorphic map
\[
(u, z_1, \ldots, z_n) \mapsto (u^k, z_1, \ldots, z_n).
\]
This map is \\'etale in a neighborhood of $\{0\} \times D^n \subset \widetilde{D} \times D^n$ if and only if $k = 1$, i.e.\ if and only if $(1 - \mu_1 - \mu_2)^{-1}$ is an integer.
