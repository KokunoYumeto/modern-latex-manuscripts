completely settled. We divide all numbers not divisible by a modulus p, where p is a prime of the
form 4n + 1, among four complexes A, B, C, D according as they become congruent modulo p to

(mod p), then +2 belongs to A, B, C, D according as 12 b has the form 4n, 4n + 1, 4n + 2, 4n + 3,
respectively.

The special theorems obtained by induction in this way are confirmed as far as the induction is
continued, and they exhibit a very beautiful form of criteria. When they are compared in order
to draw general conclusions from them, the following observations occur at once.
The criteria for deciding to which complex the prime number ±q is to be referred, taking the
upper or lower sign according as q has the form 4n + 1 or 4n + 3, depend on the forms of a, b
compared with one another modulo q.
First, when a ≡ 0 (mod q), ±q belongs to a fixed complex: namely to A for q = 7, 17, 23,
and to C for q = 3, 11, 13, 19. This suggests that the former case holds in general when q is of
the form 8n ± 1, and the latter when q is of the form 8n ± 3. Moreover, B and D are excluded
without induction when a is divisible by q, since then p ≡ b2 (mod q), so that p is a quadratic
residue of q, and therefore by the fundamental theorem ±q must be a quadratic residue of p.
Second, when a is not divisible by q, the criterion depends on the value of ab (mod q). This
expression√admits q different values, 0, 1, 2, . . . , q − 1; but when q is of the form 4n + 1, the two
values of −1 (mod q) must be excluded, since they plainly cannot be values of ab under the
standing assumption that p = a2 + b2 is a prime distinct from q. Thus the number of admissible
values of ab (mod q) is q − 2 for q ≡ 1 (mod 4), while it remains q for q ≡ 3 (mod 4).
These values are distributed into four classes. Let certain values, denoted indefinitely by α,
correspond to A; others, denoted by β, to B; others, γ, to C; and the remaining values, δ, to
D. Thus ±q is assigned to A, B, C, D according as b ≡ αa, b ≡ βa, b ≡ γa, b ≡ δa (mod q).
The law of this distribution appears more hidden, although certain general facts can be noticed
at once. Three of the classes have the same number of values, 14 (q − 1) or 14 (q + 1), while in
one class, namely the class corresponding to the complex with criterion a ≡ 0, the number is
smaller by one. Hence the total number of distinct criteria for each complex is the same, namely
1
(q − 1) or 14 (q + 1).

We also observe that 0 is always in the first class, among the α, and that the complements
to q of the numbers α, β, γ, δ lie respectively in the first, fourth, third, and second classes.
Finally, the values of 1/α, 1/β, 1/γ, 1/δ (mod q) are seen to belong to the first, fourth, third,
and second classes when the criterion a ≡ 0 corresponds to A; but to the third, second, first,
and fourth classes, respectively, when that criterion is referred to C. This is about the limit of
what induction can provide, unless we boldly anticipate results that will below be drawn from
their genuine sources.
