237.
In Chapter III we showed that the congruence xn ≡ 1, when the modulus is a prime number
p, has µ roots when µ is the greatest common divisor of n and p − 1, and that these roots
coincide exactly with the roots of the congruence xµ ≡ 1. It is therefore enough to consider the
case in which n is a divisor of p − 1. Moreover, that the solution not only of the congruence
xn − 1 ≡ 0 but of any other congruence, for arbitrary moduli, can be derived from the solution
for prime moduli has already been shown in various places and will be taught more fully below,
in Chapter VIII.


238.
Nor is it necessary to stop here. In the same Chapter III we showed that the solution of xn ≡ 1
depends on the resolution of similar congruences xa ≡ 1, xb ≡ 1, etc., where a, b, etc. are
primes or powers of primes and n is their product. Namely, if A, B, etc. are arbitrary respective
roots of xa ≡ 1, xb ≡ 1, etc., then the product AB . . . will be one of the roots of xn ≡ 1. Our
investigations will therefore be restricted to the solution of xn ≡ 1 (mod p), where p is prime,
n is a prime or a power of a prime, and at the same time n is a divisor of p − 1.


239.
It follows further from Chapter III that among the roots of xn ≡ 1 there are always some
through whose powers all the others can be exhibited. Thus, if r denotes such a root (we called
it primitive above when n = p − 1, and we shall retain this expression here, although in a
broader sense), all roots of the proposed congruence will be

                                  1,   r,    rr,   r3 ,   ...,   rn−1 .

Such roots must therefore be sought with particular care, since once they have been found
the remaining roots become evident of themselves. For brevity we denote any power of r by
enclosing its exponent in parentheses, so that (0) denotes unity, (1) denotes any primitive root
of xn ≡ 1, (2) the square of (1), and so on. Thus the series (0), (1), (2), (3), . . ., (n − 1) contains

                                                    1
all the roots. It is known, moreover, that (k) will always be such a primitive root whenever k is
prime to n; that is, in our case, where n is a power tν of a prime t, whenever t does not divide
k. The signs (1), (2), etc. are plainly indeterminate in themselves; but once a determinate
value has been assigned to (1), all the others become determined.


240.
Since the aim is to investigate primitive roots before the others, they must first be separated
from the rest. This is done by removing from the series (0), (1), (2) . . . (n − 1) all terms (k) for
which k is divisible by t; but if n is prime, or ν = 1, only (0) is removed. Before proceeding
to the investigation of the remaining roots, we urge the reader to make several examples,
so that everything which without examples might perhaps appear too general may be seen
concretely. We add one example, but it will not on that account be superfluous to work out
others independently.
    Let p = 29 and n = 7. The seven roots of x7 ≡ 1 (mod 29) are 1, 7, 16, 20, 23, 24, 25.
Since n is prime, all these roots except 1 are primitive. If therefore 7 = (1), the signs have the
following meanings:
                                (0) (1) (2) (3) (4) (5) (6)
                                 1    7 20 24 23 16 25.
The reader will remember that the signs (n) and (0), (n + 1) and (1), and in general (a) and
(b), are equivalent whenever a ≡ b (mod n).

242.
So that the reader may know where the following investigations are headed, it will be useful to
state the theorem which we propose to prove and explain.



                                                   2
   If the number λ, which is tν−1 (t − 1), has the distinct prime factors a, b, c, d, etc., and if
λ = aα bβ cγ . . ., then the resolution of the congruence xn − 1 ≡ 0 depends on the resolution of
α + β + · · · lower congruences, of which α are of degree a, β of degree b, γ of degree c, etc.
   Thus in our example the resolution of x7 ≡ 1 depends on one congruence of the second
degree and another of the third degree; and in general it is clear that the degree of these
congruences never depends on the modulus p. In order to arrive at the proof of this theorem,
we must first put forward some propositions concerning the connection between congruences
and their roots, although these investigations properly belong to Chapter VIII, where they will
be pursued further.


243.
Theorem. Suppose that the congruence

                      xm + Axm−1 + Bxm−2 + · · · + N ≡ 0        (mod prime)

is so constituted that, when the product of the m factors x − r, x − r′ , x − r′′ , x − r′′′ . . . is
formed, and is xm + axm−1 + bxm−2 + · · · + n, one has A ≡ a, B ≡ b, C ≡ c, etc. modulo p.
Then the quantities r, r′ , r′′ . . . are roots of the proposed congruence, and it has no others.
   Proof. I. We always have

             xm + Axm−1 + Bxm−2 + · · · ≡ xm + axm−1 + bxm−2 + · · ·         (mod p).

The latter expression becomes 0 on putting x = r, x = r′ , x = r′′ , etc.; hence, for these values
of x, the former expression becomes 0 (mod p). This proves the first assertion.
   II. If some further value ϱ, congruent to none of r, r′ , etc., satisfied the proposed congruence,
then
                             0 ≡ ϱm + Aϱm−1 + Bϱm−2 + · · ·
                               ≡ ϱm + aϱm−1 + bϱm−2 + · · ·
                               ≡ (ϱ − r)(ϱ − r′ )(ϱ − r′′ )(ϱ − r′′′ ) · · · .
But none of the factors ϱ − r, ϱ − r′ , ϱ − r′′ , etc. is 0 modulo p; hence, since p is prime, it is
absurd that their product should be 0. Therefore there are no roots besides r, r′ , etc. This
proves the second assertion.

Problem. Let r, r′ , r′′ . . . be unknown quantities whose number is m, whose sum is α, whose
sum of squares is β, whose sum of cubes is γ, and so on, and whose sum of m-th powers
is µ. Suppose that these numbers themselves, again m in number, are not given, but rather
other numbers α′ , β ′ , γ ′ , etc. respectively congruent to them modulo a prime p > m. Find the
congruence of the m-th degree whose roots are r, r′ , r′′ , etc.
   Solution. Regard r, r′ , r′′ , etc. as the roots of an equation

                            xm + Axm−1 + Bxm−2 + Cxm−3 + · · · = 0

and determine its coefficients A, B, C, etc., using congruence in place of equality, by the known
method, namely by setting
                                    −A ≡ α′ ,
                                   −2B ≡ β ′ + Aα′ ,
                                   −3C ≡ γ ′ + Aβ ′ + Bα′ ,
                                   −4D ≡ δ ′ + Aγ ′ + Bβ ′ + Cα′ ,
                                        etc.
                                  −mN ≡ µ′ + Aλ′ + etc.
These coefficients cannot be indeterminate, since all the numbers 1, 2, 3 . . . m are less than p. I
say that the required congruence is
                               xm + Axm−1 + Bxm−2 + · · · + N ≡ 0.

   Proof. Suppose that the equation whose roots are r, r′ , r′′ , r′′′ , etc. is
                                  xm + axm−1 + bxm−2 + · · · = 0.
Then
                                      −a = α,
                                     −2b = β + aα,
                                     −3c = γ + aβ + bα,
                                     −4d = δ + aγ + bβ + cα,
                                          etc.
It is therefore evident that
                         a ≡ A,       b ≡ B,       c≡C      etc. (mod p).
Thus, by the preceding section, the numbers r, r′ , r′′ , etc., which are roots of
                                  xm + axm−1 + bxm−2 + · · · = 0,
are also roots of the congruence
                          xm + Axm−1 + Bxm−2 + · · · ≡ 0.          Q. E. D.
We leave examples for the readers to construct.


245.
We return to our purpose. Keeping the notation of §§242 and the preceding sections, we set out
to show that, if λ is the product of any factors ef g, etc., the primitive roots of xn ≡ 1, whose
number is λ, can be divided into e classes, so that the sums of the roots put into the same class
are given by a congruence of degree e; and, once these are supposed known, any class can be
subdivided into f orders, so that the sums of the terms in each order are given by a congruence
of degree f . These orders can then again be subdivided, and so on, until one arrives at the
individual roots.

period, or simply a period. Here e denotes a divisor of λ, α any given number, and k all integers
from 0 up to λe − 1. For brevity we denote such a period by (e ∗ α). Thus in our example the
terms

                                 (2), (5)                        (3 ∗ 2).
If all the terms are distributed in any way into periods, and each period is again distributed
into smaller periods, and so on, we say that what was promised in the preceding section has
been obtained.

of such a period depends in a certain way on two arbitrary quantities r and ρ, it nevertheless
contains nothing vague: however these quantities are chosen, the same terms always come
together into the same period, provided that the number of terms which the period is to contain
has been prescribed.

such a form (ρke+α ), that is,

above.

Theorem. The product of two similar periods can be composed, independently of the number p,
by the addition of similar periods and given numbers.
   We call periods similar when they contain the same number of terms, or equivalently when
the number e is the same.
    Example. Let n = 7. The product of the periods (1) + (6) and (2) + (5) is, since (a) × (b) =
(a + b), equal to (3) + (6) + (8) + (11), that is, it consists of the periods (3) + (4) and (1) + (6).

   Proof. Let λe = f , and let the given periods (e ∗ α) and (e ∗ δ), or sums, be

                           (ρα ) + (ρα+e ) + (ρα+2e ) + · · · + (ρα+(f −1)e ) = P,
                              (ρδ ) + (ρδ+e ) + (ρδ+2e ) + · · · + (ρδ+(f −1)e ) = Q.
The product P Q consists of f 2 terms. These must be arranged as follows. Form f series, each
consisting of f terms. The first contains the product of P by (ρδ ), the second the product
P (ρδ+e ), and so on. In the first series the first place is occupied by the product arising from
the part (ρα ), the second by the product arising from (ρα+e ), and so on. In the second series
the first place is assigned to the product arising from (ρα+e ), the second to the product arising
from (ρα+2e ), and so on, the last to that arising from (ρα ). The third begins with the product
arising from (ρα+2e ), and so forth; after the product from the last part follow the products from
the first and second parts, etc. Thus, if the successive parts of the period P are denoted by
1, 2, 3 . . . z and those of the period Q by I, II, III, . . . Z, the parts of P Q are
                                   1 · I + 2 · I + 3 · I + 4 · I + · · · + z · I,
                                   2 · II + 3 · II + 4 · II + · · · + 1 · II,
                                   3 · III + 4 · III + · · · + 1 · III + 2 · III,
                                   etc.
Now collect into f orders all terms occupying the same place in the several series. I say, first,
that if one term is ≡ 1, then all the others of the same order are also ≡ 1; and second, that every
order in which no term is ≡ 1 forms a period. Once these have been proved, the proposition is
plainly obtained.
   The general form of such an order is
       (ρα+ke + ρδ ),   (ρα+(k+1)e + ρδ+e ),      (ρα+(k+2)e + ρδ+2e ), . . . , (ρα+(k+f −1)e + ρδ+(f −1)e ).
For ρα+(k−1)e one may also write ρα+(k+f −1)e , since ef = λ and ρλ ≡ 1 (mod n), and similarly
for the preceding terms. Put ρα+ke + ρδ ≡ ρχ (mod n), which is permitted unless perhaps
ρα+ke + ρδ is divisible by n. 1 Then the order can be exhibited as
                                 (ρχ ),   (ρχ+e ),     (ρχ+2e ), . . . , (ρχ+(f −1)e ),
which is plainly the period (e ∗ χ). If, on the other hand, ρα+ke + ρδ is divisible by n, all terms
of the order are ≡ (0), that is, ≡ 1. Q. E. D.

another below which does not have this advantage, but which is not to be despised on account
of its simplicity.

the periods
                  (ef ∗ α),     (ef ∗ f + α),        (ef ∗ 2f + α), . . . , (ef ∗ (e − 1)f + α)

the numbers placed after the sign ∗, here α, f + α, 2f + α, proceed in an arithmetic progression

are similar.
    Theorem. If the periods of two similar properly ordered systems are multiplied with one
another, the first by the first, the second by the second, the third by the third, etc., the sum of
all these products can be composed of periods similar to the larger period and of given numbers.
   Proof. Let the systems be
                           (ef ∗ α), (ef ∗ α + f ), (ef ∗ α + 2f ), . . . ,
                           (ef ∗ δ), (ef ∗ δ + f ), (ef ∗ δ + 2f ), . . . .
The products of each period of the first system with the corresponding periods of the second
consist, by the preceding section, of integers and similar periods. A little attention to the
genesis of these periods shows that if

                                          (ef ∗ α) × (ef ∗ δ)

consists of an integer N and of the periods (ef ∗ A), (ef ∗ B), (ef ∗ C), etc., then the products

  (ef ∗ α + f ) × (ef ∗ δ + f ) consist of N and of (ef ∗ A + f ), (ef ∗ B + f ), (ef ∗ C + f ), etc.,
(ef ∗ α + 2f ) × (ef ∗ δ + 2f ) consist of N and of (ef ∗ A + 2f ), (ef ∗ B + 2f ), (ef ∗ C + 2f ), etc.,
and in general

(ef ∗ α + µf ) × (ef ∗ δ + µf ) consists of N and of (ef ∗ A + µf ), (ef ∗ B + µf ), (ef ∗ C + µf ), etc.

It follows at once that the sum of all the products is

                        eN + (f ∗ A) + (f ∗ B) + (f ∗ C) etc.           Q. E. D.

This proof also supplies a method for finding that sum.

It is easy to make this theorem still more general. If any number of similar properly ordered
systems are given and one forms the products of all first periods, of all second periods, etc.,
then the sum of all these products consists of numbers and larger periods. If all these systems
are taken equal, the sum of any powers whatever of all the periods will consist of numbers and
periods similar to the larger one. It is now clear what this is aiming at. Let λ = ef gh . . ., and

In applying these rules, however, a difficulty occurs which must be removed. Since every
congruence has several roots, we must see which sign is to be assigned to each so that they
can be properly distinguished from one another. Since the designation of periods depends on
the numbers r and ρ, which may be chosen arbitrarily, something arbitrary must necessarily
cling to the designation as well. The number ρ must of course be fixed at the beginning. The
essential character of our method consists chiefly in deriving smaller periods from larger ones.
But this cannot be done without the proper order of the periods, which we have achieved by
the signs. We must therefore strive to distinguish all periods by their signs as soon as they have
been found.

be designated. It is clear that each is contained in a form (ef ∗ ke + α); but I say that for one
of them, say B, the number k may be taken arbitrarily, and the placement of the others can
then be derived.

etc. Let µ1 ρke+α ≡ ν1 (mod n). Since the value of r is arbitrary, provided only that A obtains
