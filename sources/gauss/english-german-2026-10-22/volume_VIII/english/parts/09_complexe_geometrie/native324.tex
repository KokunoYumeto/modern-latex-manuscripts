If (0), (1), (2), (3) are the complex numbers that represent the positions of the points 0, 1, 2, 3 (I can no doubt assume that you have the essentials of the theory of complex numbers in mind, but for good measure I note that $x+iy$, i.\,e. $x+y\sqrt{-1}$, is the complex number that represents the position of the point whose coordinates are $x$, $y$), and if $A$, $B$, $C$ are the points represented by the complex numbers
\[
(0)(1)+(2)(3),\qquad (0)(2)+(1)(3),\qquad (0)(3)+(1)(2)
\]
then the sides of the triangle $ABC$ are as follows.
\[
BC=(01).(23),\qquad AC=(02).(13),\qquad BC=(03).(12), \]
The exterior angles of the triangle, or the differences of the angles $A$, $B$, $C$ from $180^0$, are the angles whose sines occur in the equations above. I leave to you the very slight trouble of seeing this directly for yourself. The same theorem can also be proved \textls[120]{geometrically} almost as elegantly. —

To visualize the theorem itself geometrically, i.\,e. its content, one must consider separately the two cases in which the four points are arranged
\[
\begin{array}{c@{\quad}ccc@{\qquad}c@{\quad}ccc}
 & * & & * & & * & & *\\
\text{thus} & & * & & \text{or thus} & & &\\
 & & * & & & * & & *
\end{array}
\]
i.\,e. where one of them lies inside the triangle formed by the other three, or where all four form a quadrilateral without re-entrant angles.

In the first case the theorem can be stated as follows.

\Needspace{13\baselineskip}Theorem. In a triangle whose sides are $AD\times BC$, $BD\times AC$, $CD\times AB$, the angles opposite them are respectively $\alpha-A$, $\beta-B$, $\gamma-C$.
