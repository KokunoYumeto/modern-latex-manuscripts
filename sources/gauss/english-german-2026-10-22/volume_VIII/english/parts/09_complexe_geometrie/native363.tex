\begin{center}\bfseries ON THE THEORY OF ROTATION OF THE SPHERICAL SURFACE INTO ITSELF.\end{center}

The problem of combining two rotations into one can be solved in two ways.

I. Combine the two rotations, the first $a$ about the point $A$ and the second $\beta$ about the point $B$, into one.

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}{4.4cm}\input{figure363a.tex}\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}{8.1cm}\raggedright
Join $AB$ and set
\[
\begin{aligned}
BAC&=-\tfrac12a\quad\text{or}\quad CAB=+\tfrac12a,\\
ABC&=+\tfrac12\beta,
\end{aligned}
\]
and construct the triangle $ABC$. Then $2ACD$ is the rotation obtained by combining the two rotations.
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

\begin{center}\input{figure363b.tex}\end{center}
II. Instead of rotations about points, consider displacements along great circles.

Then the displacement $2AC$ is composed of $2AB$ and $2BC$.

\begin{center}\bfseries REMARKS.\end{center}

The preceding notes [I], [II] and [III] are in a handbook. The following note [IV] is on an individual undated slip of paper. It shows that \textsc{Gauss} had recognized the connection between linear substitutions of a complex variable and rotations of the spherical surface into itself. For this reason note [V] has been added, which deals with the same subject in a purely geometrical way. It is found in another handbook and was probably written down before 1819.
