Further, if we denote, for\,example, the angle that (I, II) makes with the fixed axis by [I, II],
we have
\[
\begin{array}{rl}
\text{[I, II]} & = [4,1] + [3,2] \\
\text{[II, III]} & = [2,1] + [4,3] \\
\text{[III, I]} & = [3,1] + [4,2].
\end{array}
\]
It follows that the angles are
\[
\begin{array}{rl}
\text{II, I, III} & = [4,1] + [3,2] - [3,1] - [4,2] \\
\text{III, II, I} & = [2,1] + [4,3] - [4,1] - [3,2] \\
\text{I, III, II} & = [3,1] + [4,2] - [2,1] - [4,3].
\end{array}
\]

Now apply the theorem that the sides of a triangle are proportional to the sines of the
opposite angles. For the original quadrilateral this gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{\sin\{[4,1]+[3,2]-[3,1]-4,2]\}}{(2,1)(4,3)}\\
&=\frac{\sin\{[2,1]+[4,3]-[4,1]-[3,2]\}}{(3,1)(4,2)}\\
&=\frac{\sin\{[3,1]+[4,2]-[2,1]-[4,3]\}}{(4,1)(3,2)}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Our proposition can be proved just as simply by geometry, so we also give that
proof here, although strictly speaking it does not belong in this lecture.

First suppose that point 4 lies inside triangle 1, 2, 3. If we then draw
(4,1), (4,2), (4,3) and extend these lines in their directions to I, II, III so that
we have

\begin{center}\input{figure339a.tex}\end{center}

\[
\begin{array}{rl}
(4,1) : (4,3) & = (4,\text{III}) : (4,\text{I}) \\
(4,1) : (4,2) & = (4,\text{II}) : (4,\text{I}),
\end{array}
\]
then it follows directly that
\[
(4,3) : (4,2) = (4,\text{II}) : (4,\text{III}).
\]

We therefore have
\[
\triangle\ (4,1,3) \sim \triangle\ (4,\text{III},\text{I}),
\]
