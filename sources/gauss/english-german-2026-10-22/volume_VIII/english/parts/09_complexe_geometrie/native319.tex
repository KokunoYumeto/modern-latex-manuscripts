\textls[120]{With this understood}, all previous treatments of the problem are incomplete in that they give no general rule by which one can tell at once from the given data whether they are mutually compatible. Even without taking a purely theoretical point of view, remedying this deficiency seems desirable in practice. Data intended to refer to a real case in concreto can be distorted by gross copying errors and mistakes, even when there is no prank involved. It must be worthwhile even for someone concerned only with practice to be able to recognize such errors at once in many cases.

By a general rule I mean that one is to dispense with making a separate rough drawing for each particular case. For if one is willing to do that, it will be easy enough each time to judge whether the case is possible or impossible.

What is required, however, is precisely that all cases be covered by a single general rule.

You seemed to think that such physical impossibility or incompatibility affects only isolated, perhaps rare, cases. The situation is otherwise. If one sets aside any known correct correspondence to a real case in concreto, there are three times as many impossible combinations as possible ones.

This is precisely what is contained in the general proposition on the slip of paper. It reads as follows.

\begin{quote}“If the prescribed data are possible, they immediately become impossible when one of the three directions ($XA$, $XB$, $XC$) is changed to the opposite one (differing by $180^0$)”.\end{quote}
