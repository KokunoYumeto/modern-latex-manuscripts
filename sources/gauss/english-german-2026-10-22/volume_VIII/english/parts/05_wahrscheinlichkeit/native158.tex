\SourceLeaf{158}determination of the altitude of the pole from $p$ lower and $q$ upper culminations is assigned the weight $\dfrac{4pq}{p+q}$, which is quite correct. But the altitude of the pole is not the sum, only \textls{half} the sum of the zenith distances at upper and lower culmination. The case is therefore quite different. It can be shown in general, \textls{without much effort} from the principles of my Theoria and \textls{with the greatest ease} using my new formulation of its foundations, that, --- if a quantity $w$ arises from a combination of the quantities $x,y,z$ etc., so that $w=\alpha x+\beta y+\gamma z+\text{etc.}$, and if the values of $x,y,z$ etc. are further determined independently of one another so that the respective weights $p,q,r$ etc. are assigned to those determinations, --- the resulting value of $w$ has a reliability whose weight is expressed by $\frac{1}{\dfrac{\alpha\alpha}{p}+\dfrac{\beta\beta}{q}+\dfrac{\gamma\gamma}{r}+\text{etc.}} $.

(Weight, incidentally, is always directly proportional to the square of the accuracy, or inversely proportional to the square of the so-called probable error. This is no theorem. It is simply the \textls{definition} of the word weight.)
