\begin{center}\textbf{REMARKS ON THE FRAGMENTS\\ ON ELLIPTIC MODULAR FUNCTIONS.}\end{center}

The approach provided by the algorithm of the arithmetic-geometric mean, by which \textsc{Gauss} opened up a path to the theory of elliptic functions and their associated modular functions, led \textsc{Gauss} to place modular functions before the general elliptic functions (contrary to the order of development usually followed in more recent times). This is the natural standpoint insofar as modular functions are functions of one variable, or two homogeneous variables, whereas the general elliptic functions depend on two arguments, or three homogeneous arguments.

The algorithm in question is defined in its proper generality in Art. 12\footnote{Here and in what follows the articles referred to are those of the treatise on the arithmetic-geometric mean published among the posthumous papers (ges. Werke III p. 361--403).}. From three initially real \SourceLeaf{110}quantities $a$, $b$, $c$, the two means $M(a,b)$ and $M(a,c)$ are derived, and their dependence on the given triple $a$, $b$, $c$ is studied.

Two points then become fundamental to \textsc{Gauss}'s development. The first is the idea of \emph{inversion}, so important also in the later development of the theory of elliptic functions, i.\,e. the problem of viewing the originally given $a$, $b$, $c$ in their dependence on $M(a,b)$ and $M(a,c)$. On the other hand, in developing this view it becomes natural or necessary to take $a$, $b$, $c$, or $M(a,b)$, $M(a,c)$, as \emph{complex variables}.

With regard to the first point, it is an important result that \textsc{Gauss} already knew, in the nineties of the preceding century, the series expansions for the values at zero of the three even $\vartheta$-functions. The reasoning used here (see Art. 16) is highly remarkable both for its grasp of the true goal of the investigation and for the individual arguments it employs.

The admission of complex variables appeared necessary in particular because of the developments in Art. 17. There the fundamental formulas for the linear transformation of the $\vartheta$-values at zero are established. From this starting point \textsc{Gauss} recognized a number of important principles of the theory of modular functions that have become generally accessible only in more recent times.

In this connection, compare first the statements of Art. 17 and then the fragments printed above. The quantity $t$ in fragment [3] is related to the period ratio $\omega$ of the more recent theory by the equation $\omega = it$. The generators of the group of all linear period transformations then become

\[
t' = t+i, \qquad t' = \frac1t.
\]

\textsc{Gauss} repeatedly considered the composition of the other substitutions of this group from these generators. In addition to the statements in fragment [1], mention should be made of the formula

\[
\frac{[\alpha,\beta,\ldots,\nu]\theta+[\beta,\gamma,\ldots,\nu]i}{-i[\alpha,\beta,\ldots,\mu]\theta+[\beta,\gamma,\ldots,\mu]},
\]
which occurs in a notebook entitled »Cereri Palladi Junoni sacrum, Febr. 1805«. The continued fraction expansions of the following two substitutions are given there as examples

\[
\frac{128\theta+37i}{-45i\theta+13}, \qquad \frac{121\theta+36i}{-84i\theta+25}.
\]

Both to elucidate the continued fraction expansion of the substitutions and to draw conclusions in function theory, \textsc{Gauss} used the geometrical mode of representation that has become the basis of the more recent theory of modular functions. In the notebook just mentioned, \textsc{Gauss} drew the figure reproduced here. Since the aforementioned continued fraction expansions of substitutions occur beside it, \textsc{Gauss} presumably used the figure to illustrate those expansions. Indeed, here we have the beginning of the familiar network of circular-arc triangles that underlies the theory of modular functions.

That \textsc{Gauss} understood the relevant »principle of symmetrical multiplication of circular-arc triangles« in general, and that ev\SourceLeaf{111}en the character of the »natural boundary« of a network of triangles obtained in this way did not escape him, is also evident from the second drawing reproduced here, which was found among the posthumous papers on a

\begin{center}\resizebox{12.3cm}{!}{\input{figures/figure104.tex}}\end{center}

\noindent separate sheet. It concerns circular-arc triangles with angles $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$, $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$, $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$, for which the orthogonal circle emphasized in the drawing provides the natural boundary. Beside the drawing are the following statements in \textsc{Gauss}'s hand

\emph{»Centre of the first circle $\sqrt[4]{2}$,}

\emph{Radius $\BareRoot{(\sqrt{2}-1)}$,}

\SourceLeaf{112}
\emph{Centre of the second circle $\frac12\{\BareRoot{(\sqrt2+1)}+\BareRoot{(\sqrt2-1)}\}$,}

\emph{Radius $\frac12\{\BareRoot{(\sqrt2+1)}-\BareRoot{(\sqrt2-1)}\}$.«}

The figures briefly indicated in vol.~III p.~477 and following of the collected works are more closely concerned with function theory. They are reproduced here again in somewhat greater detail. \textsc{Gauss} characterizes the region drawn in the upper figure by the condition that the imaginary components of $t$ and $\dfrac1t$ lie between $-i$ and $+i$. This is the »domain of discontinuity of the congruence group of level two« in the sense of the more recent theory of modular functions. \textsc{Gauss} recognized that the points of this region are uniquely mapped to all those complex values of the function

\begin{center}\resizebox{8.3cm}{!}{\input{figures/figure105.tex}}\end{center}

\[
\left(\frac{qt}{pt}\right)^{2}
\]
that have a positive real part. This is the function $k'$ of the more recent theory, which gives the so-called complementary modulus of the elliptic integral of the first kind as a function of the period ratio. In the equation given at the cited place by \textsc{Gauss},

\[
\left(\frac{qt}{pt}\right)^{2} = A
\]
$A$ is to be as\-sumed to be a complex number with absolute value $\leqq 1$. Otherwise the region of the $t$-plane would have to be doubled in a suitable way.

The statements at the beginning of fragment [3] are directly connected with this. One need only add that one obtains even all the solutions of the equation

\[
\left(\frac{qt}{pt}\right)^{2} = A^{2}
\]
if, in the manner indicated, one admits all integers satisfying the condition $\alpha\delta - 4\beta\gamma = 1$, namely $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta$.

The remarks added towards the end of fragment [3] on the computation of $B = \dfrac{q(\frac13 t)}{p(\frac13 t)}$ from $A$ are not directly connected with this. \textsc{Jacobi} subsequently rediscovered the equation given here as a modular equation for a transformation of degree three. It is well known that it also occurs in a particularly elegant (irrational) form in \textsc{Legendre}.

The numerical data appended to the fragment are, incidentally, inaccurate in several of the last decimal places.

\textsc{Gauss} recognized early the connection between the theory of modular functions and the arithmetic of binary quadratic forms of negative determinant. Besides Art.~17, fragment [2] is particularly important for the reduction theory of forms. Note especially the concluding line. There \textsc{Gauss} stated that reduction theory is in no way tied to the assumption of integral or rational coefficients. \hfill \textsc{Fricke}.
