In Euclidean geometry nothing is absolutely large, whereas in non-Euclidean geometry there is something absolutely large. That is its essential character, and anyone who does not admit this already assumes the whole of Euclidean geometry eo ipso. As I said, however, I am convinced that this is mere self-deception.

In the case in question, suppose the points $A$, $B$ and the direction $AC$ are given and $C$ can grow without restriction. Then, even though $DBC$ keeps approaching $DAC$, there is nothing contradictory in the difference never being reducible below a certain finite amount.
