[\emph{Proposition 1} = Euclid I. 13: The angles that one straight line makes with another
on which it stands are both right angles or together equal
to two right angles.

\emph{Proposition 2} = Euclid I. 5: In every isosceles triangle, the
angles at the base are equal to one another.

\emph{Proposition 3} = Euclid I. 6: If two angles of a triangle are
equal, the sides opposite the equal angles are also equal to one another.

\emph{Proposition 4}: A triangle cannot have two angles equal to right
angles.]

\emph{5. Theorem.} No angle of a triangle can equal the supplementary angle adjacent to
another angle in the same triangle.

It is impossible that $ACB = ABD$.

\textls{Proof.} Suppose $ACB = ABD$ and distinguish three
cases.

I. Let $AB = AC$. Fig. 1. Then (Proposition 2) $ABC = ACB$, hence
$ABC = ABD$. Thus $ABC, ACB$ are right angles [Proposition 1], which is
impossible (Proposition 4).

II. Let $AB$ be less than $AC$. (Fig. 2.) Thus $B$ lies inside
a spherical surface with centre $A$ and radius $=AC$. The straight line
$CB$, extended beyond $B$, must therefore meet the spherical surface. Let this
occur at $E$. Thus $AE=AC$, hence $AEB=ACB$ (Proposition 2), then
$AEB=ABD=ABE$, and further (Proposition 3) $AB=AE=AC$, contrary to the assumption.
