\begin{center}\textsc{Cubature of Tetrahedra.}\end{center}
In the tetrahedron $1234$ the faces $124$ and $134$ are perpendicular. The volume $=\Delta$.
\begin{center}\input{figure235.tex}\end{center}
\[
\partial\Delta=-24.\partial341,
\]
provided the angles at $3$ remain constant, in which case
\[
\alpha\alpha\operatorname{cotg}341^{2}-\beta\beta\left(\frac{\operatorname{tg}i.24}{i}\right)^{2}=1
\]
and
\[
\begin{aligned}
\alpha&=\operatorname{cotg}431\\
\beta&=\operatorname{cotg}234.
\end{aligned}
\]

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\begin{center}\textsc{Remarks.}\end{center}
\begingroup\small
Notes [I] and [III] occur in immediate succession in a notebook and were very likely written when \textsc{Gauss} was drafting the letter reprinted on pages 221—224 of this volume, addressed to \textsc{Bolyai}, that is, in March 1832. Note [II] dates from later and is recorded in the same notebook.

While notes [I] and [II] need no explanation, an explanation is all the more necessary for note [III], since in the formula for $\partial\Delta$ the factor $\frac{1}{2}$ is missing.

\textsc{Gauss} imagines a tetrahedron $1234$ constructed so that the faces $124$ and $134$ are perpendicular to one another and the edges $13$ and $24$ also meet the line of intersection $14$ of these two faces at right angles. Then $24$ is perpendicular to $14$ and $34$, and $13$ to $12$ and $14$, and the tetrahedron has the four right triangles
\[
123,\qquad134,\qquad412,\qquad423,
\]
as faces, with the right-angle vertex always listed first.

In the right triangle $134$ we have
\[
\cos(i.34)=\cot(431).\cot(341),
\]
and in the right triangle $423$,
\[
\sin(i.34)=\cot(234).\operatorname{tg}(i.24),
\]
Eliminating the side $34$ common to the two triangles gives the relation
\[
\cot(431)^{2}.\cot(341)^{2}-\cot(234)^{2}.\left(\frac{\operatorname{tg}i.24}{i}\right)^{2}=1,
\]
