There are many such things, even in elementary geometry, that require a rigorous
proof, for example the \textls{possibility of the plane}, whose definition already
involves a theorem. For example, if $ABD$, $AFG$, $ACE$, $BFC$ are straight lines,
the straight line determined by $DE$ cannot pass above or below $G$ in its
course.

The proof is not very difficult, though it is not altogether easy either, and in any

\begin{center}ESTABLISHING THE PLANE.\end{center}


1. By a \textls{plane} we mean the surface containing every straight line through a given point $A$,
such as $AD$, that makes a right angle with the given straight line $AB$.
Such a plane is thus described when $AD$ rotates about $AB$
as its axis.

2. If $AB$ is extended backward to $C$, then $AC$ also makes with each $AD$
a right angle.

\begin{center}
\input{figure201b.tex}
\end{center}

3. Now let $AD$ and $AD'$ be two straight lines in the plane and let $AD = AD'$. Let
$AC = AB$ as well. The four-point configuration $ACD'D$ then coincides with the
four-point configuration $ABDD'$, and hence the triangle $CD'D$ coincides with the triangle $BDD'$.
But the triangle $BD'D$ also coincides with $BDD'$, so $CD'D$ coincides with $BD'D$. If
$T$ is any point on the straight line $DD'$, then $BDT$ and
$CDT$ also coincide, and so do $BT$ and $CT$. Thus $BCT$ is isosceles, so
$BCT$ and $CBT$ coincide, as do $BAT$ and $CAT$. Consequently the angles $BAT$ and $CAT$
are right angles, and $T$ lies in the plane.
