Thus for $a$ in the plane there must be \textls{one} symmetric point $a'$, and for $b$ there must be a corresponding $b'$. And $ab$ must coincide with $a'b'$ when I \textls{fold it over}, and generally \textls{only} by folding it over. What is symmetric to a symmetric figure becomes congruent here. If the third dimension is now added and, on \textls{both sides}, $c$ and $c'$ lie above $a$ and $a'$, then in folding over $c$ and $c'$ also fall on different sides of the plane, and so on. --- I think that in this way one could gain an intuitive conviction.

To my knowledge there is still no appropriate technical term for figures that can be divided into two symmetric halves by a line or plane drawn through them. Examples are the isosceles triangle and the triangular pyramid with an isosceles base and faces equally inclined along its two equal sides, and so on. I would very much like a term for describing these things concisely.

I made diligent use of \textsc{Legendre}'s Geometry in preparing the first edition of \textsc{Lorenz}, and to my knowledge [found] several points of solid geometry presented more rigorously than in earlier books. For Euclid XII. 5, however, I have so far found nothing better than my § 330, where I could not dispense with the method of exhaustion. \mbox{.\ .\ .\ .\ .\ .\ .}\}
