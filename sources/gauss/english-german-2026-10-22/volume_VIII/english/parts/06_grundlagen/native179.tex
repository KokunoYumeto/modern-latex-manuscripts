\SourceLeaf{179}A large part of the work revolves around the contention, directed against \textsc{Kant}, that
the certainty of geometry rests on definitions and on the
»Principium identitatis« and the »Principium contradictionis« rather than on intuition. That these
logical aids are continually used to give form and connection to the truths of geometry
is something \textsc{Kant} can hardly have wished to deny. But that
they are powerless in themselves and put forth only barren blossoms unless the
living, fertilizing intuition of the object itself is active throughout can hardly escape anyone
familiar with the nature of geometry. Mr. \textsc{Schwab}'s objection
seems, incidentally, to rest in part on a misunderstanding. At least it seems to us, in view of
the 16th paragraph of his work, which from beginning to end calls upon the very
faculty of intuition and is ultimately meant to prove »postulata Euclidis in
generaliora resolvi posse, non sensu et \textls{intuitione} sed \textls{intellectu} fundata«, that Mr.
\textsc{Schwab} must have understood these names for different branches of the faculty of cognition
in a sense different from that of the Königsberg philosopher.

Although the author of the second work has treated his subject in an altogether different and
genuinely mathematical manner, we can judge his result
no more favorably. We do not intend to set out the whole course of his
attempted proof here and shall confine ourselves to bringing out the point on which
everything ultimately depends.
