\emph{6. Theorem.} No angle of a triangle can be greater than the supplementary angle adjacent to
another angle in the same triangle.

$ACB$ cannot be greater than $ABD$.

\textls{Proof.} Describe a conical surface with axis $CB$, vertex $C$ and an angle equal to $ABD$.
If $ABD$ were smaller than $ACB$, the line $CA$ would lie outside the
cone (definition), and since $B$, as a point on the axis, lies inside,
the straight line $AB$ must meet the conical surface. Let it do so at $E$. It follows that
$ECB = EBD$, which is impossible (Proposition 5).

\begin{center}\textsc{Remark.}\end{center}


The principle of placing congruent triangles in succession, on which the proof in
note [1] rests, was written down by \textsc{Gauss} on the back of the title page of his copy
of \textsc{Baermann}, Elementorum Euclidis Libri XV, Leipzig 1769. It had already been used
by \textsc{Legendre} in 1798 (Éléments de géométrie, 2\textsuperscript{ième} édition,
Proposition XIX). Exactly the same proof also occurs in \textsc{Lobatschefskij}'s
treatise \gausscyr{Новыя начала геометріи} (New Principles of Geometry), Chap. VI,
\S\ 90, from 1836.

Note [2] is on a single undated slip. The
\emph{Theorem 6} it contains is equivalent to Euclid I. 17.
