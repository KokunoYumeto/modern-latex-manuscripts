As for your proof of 1), I object first of all to using an infinite quantity as a \textls{completed} one. Mathematics never permits this. The infinite is merely a façon de parler, since what we are actually speaking of are limits that certain ratios approach as closely as one wishes, while others are allowed to grow without restriction. In this sense non-Euclidean geometry contains no contradiction whatsoever, even though those [who become acquainted with it] are bound at first to find many of its results paradoxical. To regard them as contradictory would be merely to deceive oneself, having acquired early on the habit of holding Euclidean geometry to be \textls{strictly} true.

In non-Euclidean geometry there are no similar figures without congruence. E.\,g., the angles of an equilateral triangle differ both from $\frac{2}{3}R$ and [in different triangles] from one another, according to the length of the sides, and can become as small as one wishes if the side grows beyond all bounds.
