\noindent If one now sets a right angle $=1$, then $C,A,B$ are numbers, so $c$ must drop out of the function, for otherwise
\[
c=\psi(C,A,B)=\text{a number},
\]
q[uod] e[st] a[bsurdum]. Thus in a right triangle the sum of the two acute angles $=1R$, from which the rest follows.

I had already read this proposition in \textsc{Legendre} when I was studying in G[öttingen], and on my last return from Göttingen I was not a little vexed that it had not come to mind when I had the pleasure of hearing you speak of it then. Now, on reading it again, I find that in reply to the objection about spherical triangles, »which had been put to him«, he says that in their case it is not $C=\varphi(A,B,c)$ but $C=\varphi(A,B,c,\mathrm{rad.})$ »ou seulement
\[
C=\varphi\!\left(A,B,\frac{c}{r}\right)
\]
en vertu de la loi des homogènes«. --- This last point is not quite clear to me, and I should very much like you to say a few words about the whole matter on some occasion.\}

\begin{center}\textsc{Gauss} to \textsc{Gerling}. Göttingen, 11 April 1816.\end{center}

.\ .\ .\ .\ .\ .\ .\ You wish to have my judgment on \textsc{Legendre}'s proof of parallels. I confess that I find no demonstrative force whatever in his inference. He concludes that $c=\psi(A,B,C)$, \textls{therefore} $=$ a number, which is absurd. But this \textls{therefore} does not follow. For the equation $c=\psi(A,B,C)$ says only that $c$ is determined as soon as $A,B,C$ are determined, and leaves open the possibility that a \textls{constant} line also occurs in the form $\psi$. The equation $C=\varphi(A,B,c)$ does not require $c$ to drop out. It can stand perfectly well provided that in the function $\varphi$ a constant line $=m$ also occurs, so that in fact
\[
C=\varphi\!\left(A,B,\frac{c}{m}\right).
\]
