\SourceLeaf{202}4. \emph{Theorem.} A straight line drawn through two points of the plane $P$, namely $T, U$,
lies wholly in that plane.

\textls{Proof.} I) If $A$ lies on the straight line $\ldots TU \ldots$, the proposition follows at once from
the definition of the plane. If $A$ does not lie on the straight line $TU$, then
$AT$ and $AU$ form an angle with one another.

II) If $AT = AU$, the proposition follows from 3.

III) If $AT$ and $AU$ are unequal, let $AT > AU$. Extend $AT$ backward
until $AK = AT$. The circle that $T$ describes by rotating about $A$
then passes through $K$, and the disk, which is part of the plane $P$,
is divided by $KT$ into two parts, one of which contains $U$.

Now let a straight line $TK$ move so that one endpoint remains at $T$ and
the other on the circumference $KVW$. It sweeps out the plane,
covering more and more of the figure and filling it completely when $W$ reaches $T$.
Thus in one of its positions the moving straight line passes through $U$.
Let this be the position $TV$. The straight line $TU$ is therefore only part
of the straight line $TV$, which lies wholly in the plane. The same thus holds
for $TU$.



That the area of the semicircle is filled entirely in the manner just described
can readily be proved with complete rigor.
Suppose some part cannot be reached and let $U$ be a point in
that part. With a radius smaller than $TU$, draw [about $T$] a
spherical surface that divides the semicircle into the parts $M$ and $M'$,
with $M$ outside and $M'$ inside the spherical surface. Clearly
$U$ then lies in $M$. Let $W$ also be the point on the circumference of the semicircle
where it is cut by the spherical surface. The straight line $TW$ then lies
inside $M'$, and thus $U$ lies inside the figure $KTWK$. But this figure is described
as the moving straight line rotates from the position $TK$ to the
position $TW$. The assumption that $U$ is never reached is therefore
absurd.
