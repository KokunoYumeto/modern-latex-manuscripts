All this gives a fully consistent theory that is indispensable in a general treatment of such subjects. It can also be applied to curved surfaces, though it still requires several modifications or further specifications when the curved surface is closed. You see that even this chapter alone covers a great deal. Yet this and much else must be included in order, for example, to give a satisfactory account of the \textls{broadest generalization} of \textsc{Legendre}'s method (for calculating spherical triangles), in which the sides are taken proportional to the sines of the angles reduced by $\frac13$ of the spherical excess. I have this generalization, and it is needed as part of Higher Geodesy.

Although the mathematical side of a work usually interests me most, I cannot deny that, if I am to take pleasure in so extensive a work, I must in the end see an orderly whole emerge, free from an excessively patchwork appearance.
