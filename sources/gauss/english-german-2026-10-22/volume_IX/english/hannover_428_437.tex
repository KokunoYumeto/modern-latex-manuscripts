\par\noindent\hspace*{\parindent}The plan of the work on the trigonometric measurements in Hanover that \textsc{Gauss} intended to write is on a small slip of paper. The introduction and the unfinished first section are taken from 2 sheets that had been folded together in book form. It is unclear when \textsc{Gauss} began drafting them. The remark on the Electoral Hessian measurements at the top of p. 403, however, shows that this must have been before 1835 (since in that year work on the Electoral Hessian triangulation, which had been suspended since the spring of 1824, was resumed. \textsc{Gerling}, Beiträge zur Geographie Kurhessens etc. pp. V and VIII). Of the reports to the Hanoverian Cabinet Ministry, the first two, dating from 1820 and 1821 and dealing with the instruments required and the preparations for the arc measurement, have already been printed (in extracts) in vol. IV, pp. 485/486 and pp. 487/489. In all, the Gauss Archive holds about 40 reports on the progress of the triangulation work, most of them very lengthy and detailed and some with overview maps. Extracts from some of them on the general part of the triangulation are given under [2]. The original records of the official correspondence on the arc measurement and the land survey are also in the Gauss Archive. Including the reports on the work, about 90 documents are in \textsc{Gauss}'s hand.

\par\noindent\hspace*{\parindent}In 1816 \textsc{Schumacher} had been commissioned by King \textsc{Friedrich} VI of Denmark to measure an arc on the meridian from Skagen to Lauenburg and an arc on the parallel from Copenhagen to the west coast of Jutland. The meridian arc to be measured extended over $4\frac12^\circ$. Extending the arc through Hanover, which would bring its length to $6\frac12^\circ$, was an idea \textsc{Schumacher} immediately put to \textsc{Gauss}. Although \textsc{Gauss} was keenly interested in the »splendid great undertaking«, he felt that he could not yet raise the matter with the Hanoverian government (see p. 345), probably because his observatory had only recently been completed and its equipment still called for expenditure. It was then \textsc{Schumacher} who approached Minister \textsc{von Arnswaldt} in Hanover in person in July 1817 and secured an initial request to \textsc{Gauss} for a »Memoire« on extending the Danish meridian arc measurement through Hanover. When \textsc{Schumacher} measured his southernmost triangles in 1818, he asked \textsc{Gauss} to make connecting observations in Lüneburg, whose Michaelisthurm was visible from the Danish triangulation stations Hamburg (Michaelisthurm), Hohenhorn, and Lauenburg. \textsc{Gauss} was reluctant, however, to seek permission from his government because a decision on the Hanoverian arc measurement had yet to be made. In a letter to \textsc{Schumacher} of 12 August 1818 he says:

\par\noindent\hspace*{\parindent}»As early as last autumn, immediately after receiving your notes, I prepared a Memoire on your arc measurement and brought out as clearly as possible the manifold advantages of extending it through Hanover in the future, so I can think of nothing further to add. I sent in this Memoire, but nothing further has come of it to date. Of all the difficult arts, that of pressing one's case is the one for which I --- to my great disadvantage, of course --- have the least talent and am least suited. In the prevailing circumstances, therefore, I can hardly return to the matter quaestionis in writing.«

\par\noindent\hspace*{\parindent}\textsc{Schumacher} again approached Minister \textsc{von Arnswaldt}, and \textsc{Gauss} was commissioned to make the measurements at Lüneburg needed to link the Hanoverian and Danish triangulations (see the top of p. 347). \textsc{Gauss} made them jointly with \textsc{Schumacher} in the first half of October 1818. On that occasion a sunlit window in the Michaelisthurm at Hamburg, which interfered with his observations, also gave him the first idea for the heliotrope that he invented in the autumn of 1820.

\par\noindent\hspace*{\parindent}Still no decision had been made on carrying out a Hanoverian triangulation. In a letter to \textsc{Schumacher} of 25 November 1818, \textsc{Gauss} says:

\par\noindent\hspace*{\parindent}\textsc{Schumacher} now used his stay in England in April 1819, where he meant to collect the \textsc{Ramsden} zenith sector used for the English triangulation and lent to him for his astronomical measurements, to win the support of influential figures in London, such as Sir \textsc{Joseph Banks}, for the Hanoverian arc measurement. He also induced the Danish envoy to take the matter up with Count \textsc{Münster}, the minister for Hanoverian affairs in London. At \textsc{Schumacher}'s suggestion, \textsc{Gauss} then addressed two reports to Count \textsc{Münster} and \textsc{v. Arnswaldt}. Both are printed in vol. IV, the former on pp. 482/483, the latter on pp. 484/485. It was also through the mediation of \textsc{Schumacher}, who again made representations to \textsc{v. Arnswaldt} in Hanover in June 1819, that \textsc{Gauss} was able to take part in the observations with the zenith sector in Lauenburg in late June and early July 1819. The following year \textsc{Gauss} wished to attend the baseline measurement in Holstein. On 18 January 1820 he wrote to \textsc{Schumacher}: »It would give me great pleasure if it were possible for me once again to spend a few weeks in your company and working with you this year. But it would depend in part on when you would be back in those parts, and I confess I am somewhat concerned about making a nuisance of myself if I apply in Hanover for the third time to make a journey that can only appear to bear some relation to a \textls[120]{possible}, but perhaps still far-off, operation in our kingdom.«\enspace .\ .\ .\ .\enspace In the same letter, referring to \textsc{Schumacher}'s request to \textsc{Gauss} for a review of the Danish arc measurement, he goes on to say:

\par\noindent\hspace*{\parindent}»Please communicate to me the \textls[120]{results} (even if only provisional as yet) of the sector observations that you make in Copenhagen in January and February of this year, principally those for which I can still make corresponding observations here, either with \textsc{Reichenbach}'s circle or, if the new clamp arms should unexpectedly be delayed into February, for the time being with \textsc{Repsold}'s. I shall then publish these results in our Gelehrten Anzeigen and take the opportunity to give a proper account of your arc measurement as a whole. For the reason given above I must ask you to send me a concise outline of its main points, all the more so as there may be one circumstance or another that you would prefer to leave unmentioned for now. A few weeks earlier or later will surely make no difference. That the whole thing should appear as \textls[120]{uncontrived} as possible matters to \textls[120]{me} too, because I would by no means want to give the impression that I was trying in this roundabout way to remind our government of the matter. For as willing as I am to carry the triangles on to Göttingen, etc., if the requisite means are provided in an appropriate manner, it is the interest of science alone, and quite apart from any interest of my own. Personally, I see it as a sacrifice, which, on the condition just stated, I am very glad to make.«

\par\noindent\hspace*{\parindent}On 9 May 1820 the Cabinet order of \textsc{Georg} IV, King of Great Britain etc. and Hanover, was finally issued to the Ministry, entrusting \textsc{Gauss} with »the continuation of the Danish arc measurement« through Hanover (see p. 347). \par\noindent\hspace*{\parindent}The Danish-Hanoverian meridian arc was capable of considerable extension southwards. It could be carried as far as the island of Elba, extending over about $16^\circ$ (see p. 402). The Hanoverian arc measurement was linked through Brocken and Inselsberg to the triangles of the Prussian General Staff. Along the side Hohehagen-Inselsberg lay the Electoral Hessian triangles, the measurement of which had been entrusted to \textsc{Gerling} in 1822. South of these, in Hesse-Darmstadt, Württemberg, Bavaria, and Austria, geodetic operations were partly in progress and partly planned. Separate from this system of triangles was the great Anglo-French arc on the meridian of Paris, which continued along the east coast of Spain to the island of Formentera (see p. 419). To the north, the French arc was joined by the triangles measured in 1801--1811 by Lieutenant General \textsc{von Krayenhoff} in Belgium, the Netherlands, East Frisia, and Oldenburg (Précis historique des opérations géodésiques et astronomiques, faites en Hollande etc. La Haye 1815). In its southeastern part, between Nederweert and Nimwegen, \textsc{Krayenhoff}'s network of triangles was joined to \textsc{Tranchot}'s, which in turn was joined on the side Nürburg-Fleckert to \textsc{Müffling}'s chain of triangles »from Berlin to the Rhine«. Nothing, however, had been published about the latter two networks (see p. 365), nor was anything published later. Moreover, \textsc{Tranchot}'s triangles were intended only as the basis for a map. The resulting cross-connection between the two great triangulation systems could therefore not be considered adequate. In a memorandum to the Bremen Senate, which it forwarded to the Hanoverian government, \textsc{Olbers} proposed towards the end of 1823, probably in agreement with \textsc{Gauss}, to link the Danish-Hanoverian arc from the sides Hamburg-Wilsede and Wilsede-Falkenberg through Bremen and Oldenburg to \textsc{Krayenhoff}'s side Varel-Jever and thereby to the Anglo-French arc measurement. In a fuller statement of 7 January 1824, held in the Gauss Archive along with \textsc{Olbers}'s memorandum, \textsc{Gauss} declared his agreement with extending the arc measurement to Jever. Its execution was then ordered by a rescript from Count \textsc{Münster} of 15 February 1824 and entrusted to him by the Cabinet Ministry on 8 March of that year (see p. 419). The connection could also have been made farther south to \textsc{Krayenhoff}'s side Bentheim-Kirchhesepe. Although the more northerly route was shorter but presented greater difficulties of terrain, \textsc{Gauss} preferred it because the triangles on that route could be connected to the North Sea, allowing the relative heights of his triangulation stations to be converted to absolute heights above sea level (see p. 419). As further justification for extending the arc measurement to Varel-Jever, \textsc{Gauss} states the following in the

\par\noindent\hspace*{\parindent}The observations for the extension of the arc measurement to Jever took place in 1824 and 1825.

\par\noindent\hspace*{\parindent}The side Varel-Jever linked the survey to \textsc{Krayenhoff}'s triangles. Their northern portion proved very inaccurate, however, as a direct check by remeasuring angles at Jever showed and an adjustment by \textsc{Gauss}, Suppl. theor. comb. Art. 23, confirmed (see the letters to \textsc{Bessel} of 12 March and 20 November 1826, pp. 360/362, to \textsc{Olbers} of 4 July 1824, p. 370, and 14 May 1826, pp. 321/322, and to \textsc{Gerling} of 12 September and 14 November 1838, pp. 391 and 393). This connection between the two great meridian arcs was therefore also of little value. On the return journey from the measurements, in late July 1825, \textsc{Gauss} therefore (as he relates in his report of 11 March 1827 on the trigonometric work in 1825) consulted \textsc{Olbers} in Bremen on how this might be remedied. As the southern triangles of \textsc{Krayenhoff}'s network were more accurate than the northern ones (see p. 361), \textsc{Olbers} proposed »starting a new series of triangles running southwest from Bremen through the Osnabrück territory, establishing a new connection to \textsc{Krayenhoff}'s triangles at Bentheim. He added the assurance that the Bremen Senate, out of interest in scientific undertakings, would gladly make the assistant \textsc{Klüver} [whom the city of Bremen had already assigned to \textsc{Gauss} the previous year to assist with the measurements] available for the remainder of the year for this purpose«. \textsc{Gauss} hesitated to accept this proposal, however, as it »would have exceeded his commission«, and moreover »the terrain from Bremen to Bentheim was quite unknown to him«. This connection, from the side Bremen-Steinberg to the side Kirchhesepe-Bentheim, was later carried out in 1829 by his son, Lieutenant \textsc{Joseph Gauss} (see the letter to \textsc{Bessel} of 9 April 1830, p. 363, and also p. 422), though with lower accuracy (see p. 342). The plan that still remained (according to the report on the work in 1824) to join the island of Helgoland to the triangulation in collaboration with \textsc{Schumacher}, via Wangeroog, Neuwerk, and a Danish triangulation station on the Schleswig-Holstein coast, was never carried out. The island's difference in longitude with Greenwich, Altona, and Bremen had been determined chronometrically in 1824 (see vol. VI, pp. 455/459). A proposed new connection between the Danish and Hanoverian arc measurements via the side Litberg-Hamburg likewise never materialised.
