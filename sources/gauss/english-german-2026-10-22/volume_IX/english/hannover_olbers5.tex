\begin{center}
\textsc{Gauss} to \textsc{Olbers}.\quad Göttingen, 19~February 1825.
\end{center}

\par\noindent\hspace*{\parindent}I am sending you my complete list of heights[\textsuperscript{*})].
A small change of \(\pm\,2{,}7\) feet has been made for Hamburg, since \textsc{Schumacher}
gives me the difference in height between the ball and the windows of the
chamber, which I formerly took to be 47,9 feet from \textsc{Benzenberg}'s
copper[plate engraving], as 53,3 from \textsc{Sonnin}'s copper[plate engraving].
For the time being I have therefore put the ball (my target) 2,7 higher
and the windows 2,7 [feet] lower than before.
This also places \textsc{Schumacher}'s barometer 2,7 [feet] lower than
before, hence \(-\,357{,}8\) [feet] relative to Göttingen and \(+\,70{,}4\)
[feet] \(= 11{,}73\) toises relative to your barometer.
The difference from your barometric determination is therefore now quite insignificant.
This will still be modified somewhat, however, since at some stations I also used the
floor of the lantern as a target, and \textsc{Schumacher} has not told me how far
it is below the ball. In any event, the relative heights of the three points
really ought to be measured properly by trigonometry (from a nearby station).

\par\smallskip\noindent\rule{.24\linewidth}{.4pt}\par\smallskip
{\footnotesize
\noindent\hspace*{2.75em}[\textsuperscript{*}) The original of this list is preserved among Gauss’s papers. The following is appended to it:]\par
\noindent\hspace*{2.75em}“The heights at the principal points, except for Göttingen, Lüneburg, and Hamburg,
for which the particulars are noted separately, always refer to the surface on which the repeating circle stood.
At most places this is a stone masonry pedestal 3\(\tfrac{1}{2}\) feet above the ground,
sometimes a couple of inches more, sometimes less.
Only at the meridian mark is this height somewhat greater, namely 5 feet”.\par
}
