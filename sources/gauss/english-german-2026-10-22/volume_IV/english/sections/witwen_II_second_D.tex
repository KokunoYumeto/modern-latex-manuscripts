If we consult experience, it shows a very large increase in this element in recent times. For the first eighty years of the institution's existence, the average number joining annually may be taken as nearly \(1\tfrac{1}{2}\); but in the last 20 years there have been 64 entries in all\footnote{These include G., whose widow receives a pension, although he himself had not, strictly speaking, been admitted. For the last 10 years alone this mean is \(4\tfrac{1}{10}\), and for the last 5 years alone, during which 27 new members joined, even \(5\tfrac{2}{5}\). It must not be overlooked that the principle of discounting on which all the calculations rest is valid only insofar as all interest is received at the proper time and immediately used again. In practice, however, this cannot be carried out; some part of the assets, now smaller, now larger, is always idle. For brevity, one could call the mean amount of this sum of money the working fund, although, strictly speaking, this name fits only that part of the cash value which must be kept ready so that the fund can meet its own payment obligations punctually. But one must also include money lying temporarily idle when an opportunity for safe and advantageous investment does not immediately present itself, and outstanding arrears. How much all this amounts to on an actual average can be gathered only imperfectly from the annual accounts, since they state only \textit{what} was received and spent in the course of the year, but not on what date (that this \textit{could} be supplied \textit{in part} from the \textit{supporting documents}, and even from these not without uncertainty, does not enter into consideration here).
