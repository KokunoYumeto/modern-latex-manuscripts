\SourceLeaf{32} or, more accurately, that this sum can never be positive. Suppose then that $\gamma,\gamma',\gamma''$ and so on are positions different from $c,c',c''$ and so on but compatible with the conditions of the system, and that $\theta,\theta';\theta''$ and so on are the angles that $c\gamma,c'\gamma',c''\gamma''$ and so on make with $cb,c'b',c''b''$ and so on. Then $\Sigma m.cb.c\gamma.\cos\theta$ is always either $0$ or negative. Now since
\[
\gamma b^2=cb^2+c\gamma^2-2cb.c\gamma.\cos\theta
\]
it is clear that
\[
\Sigma m.\gamma b^2-\Sigma m.cb^2=\Sigma m.c\gamma^2-2\Sigma m.cb.c\gamma.\cos\theta
\]
will consequently always be positive. Thus $\Sigma m.\gamma b^2$ will always be greater than $\Sigma m.cb^2$, so that $\Sigma m.cb^2$ will be a minimum. W. Z. B. W.

It is very remarkable that, when the free motions are incompatible with the necessary conditions, nature modifies them in just the same way as a mathematician uses the method of least squares to adjust observations of quantities that are linked to one another by necessary relations. This analogy could be pursued further, but that lies outside my present purpose.
