and it is well known that $T,U,V$ are respectively proportional to the cosines of the angles that a line normal to the surface makes with lines parallel to the coordinate axes $x,y,z$, that is, the angles $QX,QY,QZ$. From this we find
\[
\begin{aligned}
\cos QX&=\frac{\pm T}{\BareRoot{(TT+UU+VV)}}\\
\cos QY&=\frac{\pm U}{\BareRoot{(TT+UU+VV)}}\\
\cos QZ&=\frac{\pm V}{\BareRoot{(TT+UU+VV)}}
\end{aligned}
\]
but it remains uncertain whether to take the upper or the lower signs. To decide this, let us take on the line $PQ$, which is normal to the surface at $P$ and directed \textit{outward}, a point $P'$ infinitely close to $P$. Let the distance be $PP'=\mathrm{d}w$. The coordinates of point $P'$ will then be, respectively,
\[
\begin{aligned}
x+\mathrm{d}w.\cos QX&=x+\mathrm{d}x\\
y+\mathrm{d}w.\cos QY&=y+\mathrm{d}y\\
z+\mathrm{d}w.\cos QZ&=z+\mathrm{d}z
\end{aligned}
\]
and hence the increase in the value of the function $W$ from point $P$ (where it is $=0$) to point $P'$ is
\[
\begin{aligned}
&=\mathrm{d}w.(T\cos QX+U\cos QY+V\cos QZ)\\
&=\pm\mathrm{d}w.\BareRoot{(TT+UU+VV)}
\end{aligned}
\]
This shows that the upper signs apply if, on moving away from the solid part of the body, the function $W$ takes a positive value and hence takes a negative value on entering the solid part of the body. The lower signs apply in the opposite case. Indeed, our surface separates the solid part of the body from the rest of empty space and also separates those regions of space where $W$ takes a positive value from those where the function $W$ takes a negative value. Generally speaking, \textit{either} the value of the function $W$ is positive outside the body and negative inside, in which case the upper signs are to be taken, \textit{or} the function $W$ is negative outside and positive inside the body, in which case the lower signs apply.
