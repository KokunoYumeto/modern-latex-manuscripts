\begin{center}
{\small TABLE FOR THE CORRECTION TO NOON.\par}
\medskip\hrule\medskip
{\small Monatliche Correspondenz zur Beförderung der Erd- und Himmels-Kunde,\par
edited by Baron \textsc{von Zach}. April 1811.\par}
\medskip\hrule
\end{center}\medskip
\begin{flushright}Göttingen, March 1811,\end{flushright}
The excellent tables for the correction to noon that Mr.~\textsc{von Zach} published in the \emph{January issue of the Monatl.\ Corresp.} prompt me to send you similar tables, which I calculated some time ago for my own use. I myself had calculated mine only at intervals of 5 minutes and for half intervals from 0 to 3 hours, since I very rarely observe equal altitudes at longer intervals. But an able student of mine, Mr.~\textsc{Gerling}, who pursues astronomy with good results, has taken great care to extend them tenfold. Mr.~v.~\textsc{Zach} seems to have introduced his auxiliary angle because the logarithms of their tangents, which are all one really needs, or rather the logarithm of the tangent of one of them, varies too sharply when the half interval is 6 hours. If we consider only the cases that occur most often, however, the tables can be made even easier to use by leaving out the auxiliary angles and keeping the declination and its change in place of the longitude of the Sun, since the former are just as easy to obtain from the ephemerides as the latter. I therefore use the following procedure.
\SourceLeaf{172}%

Let
\begin{list}{}{\setlength{\leftmargin}{1.8em}\setlength{\labelwidth}{1.2em}\setlength{\labelsep}{.6em}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}\setlength{\parsep}{0pt}}
\item[$\delta$] be the declination of the Sun at noon,
\item[$\mu$] twice its daily change, that is, the change from the preceding day to the following day, taken as positive or negative according as the Sun moves from south to north or from north to south,
\item[$\varphi$] the altitude of the pole,
\item[$h$] half the interval (measured in apparent solar time).
\end{list}
Then, as is well known, the correction to noon in apparent solar time is
\[
-\mu\operatorname{tang}\varphi\frac h{15.48^{\mathrm{st.}}\sin15h}+\mu\operatorname{tang}\delta\frac h{15.48^{\mathrm{st.}}\operatorname{tang}15h}
\]
I now set
\[
\frac h{720^{\mathrm{st.}}\sin15h}=A,\qquad\frac h{720^{\mathrm{st.}}\operatorname{tang}15h}=B
\]
and include only $\log A$ and $\log B$ in my tables for the argument $h$. The correction to noon thus becomes
\[
-A\mu\operatorname{tang}\varphi+B\mu\operatorname{tang}\delta
\]

Three further points are worth noting.

1) One might wonder why I introduce the change over 48 hours. Strictly speaking, the change over 24 hours must be taken as the mean of the change since the preceding noon and the change up to the following noon. My procedure therefore saves a division by 2.

2) The correction to noon is given in apparent solar time. If the clock runs on mean time, one can safely use that correction as it stands. If it runs on sidereal time, the correction to noon must first be converted to sidereal time. It is enough to multiply it by $\frac{366}{365}$, or to add the constant 0.0012 to $\log A$ and to $\log B$.

3) For a given altitude of the pole, one could also enter $\log A+\log\operatorname{tang}\varphi$ directly in the table and save an addition.

Here is an example.

On 20 March I observed several pairs of equal solar altitudes for which the mean half interval was $1^{\mathrm{st.}}\ 43'$.
\SourceLeaf{173}%

Here we have
\[
\mu=+2739'';\qquad\delta=-7^0\ 26'\ 4'';\qquad\varphi=51^0\ 31'\ 54''
\]
This gives
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr@{\qquad\qquad}lr}
$\log\mathrm{const.}$ & $0.0012$ & $\log\mathrm{const.}$ & $0.0012$\\
$\log A$ & $7.7394\,\mathrm n$ & $\log B$ & $7.6940$\\
$\log\mu$ & $3.4376$ & $\log\mu$ & $3.4376$\\
$\log\operatorname{tang}\varphi$ & $0.0999$ & $\log\operatorname{tang}\delta$ & $9.1156\,\mathrm n$\\\cline{2-2}\cline{4-4}
& $1.2781\,\mathrm n$ & & $0.2484\,\mathrm n$\\
Value & $-18''97$ & & $-1''77$\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The correction to noon is therefore $-20''74$.

Mr.~\textsc{von Zach}'s tables require 11 lookups and mine only 7. If $\log\operatorname{tang}\varphi$ is taken as given, his require 10 and mine 6.
