\begin{center}[\textsc{Gauss} to \textsc{Schumacher.}]\quad Göttingen, 17 January 1844.\end{center}

I enclose an ephemeris of the \emph{comet}, which Dr. \textsc{Goldschmidt} calculated from his second set of elements. I have no doubt that the ephemeris will always make the comet easy to find, unless it grows too faint to be seen.

\Needspace{3\baselineskip}

Dr. \textsc{Goldschmidt} will promptly finish comparing this second set of elements with all the observations that have come to his attention.

My own observation of 11 January reads as follows:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|l|l|l}
1844 January 11 & $9^{\rm u}23^{\rm m}41^{\rm s}.8$ & $77^\circ8'49''4$ & ---\quad ---\quad --- \\
 & $9\;23\;47.5$ & ---\quad ---\quad --- & $+3^\circ32'26''7$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The declination remains somewhat uncertain. The small difference between the times to which the right ascension and the declination apply arises because the former is based on three observations and the latter on two, and because the third observation, usable only for right ascension, lies \emph{near}, but \emph{not exactly} at, the midpoint between the other two.

I now have the chronometers of a craftsman from Hanover here for testing, but they have been here only 4 or 5 days. Their rate has been exceptionally uniform in that time, although such a short period can tell us little as yet.

Finally, I should mention that my observation of 13 December changed slightly in the new reduction, solely because of an error in the \emph{reduction of the star}. It now reads as follows:
