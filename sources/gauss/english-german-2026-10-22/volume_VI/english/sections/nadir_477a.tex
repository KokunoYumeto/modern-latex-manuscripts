When determining the nadir point from the image of the wires reflected by the mercury horizon, one particularly wants the field of view to be lit somewhat uniformly. Previously, this had never been the case when using a lamp. All that appeared was a somewhat indistinct image of the flame in an otherwise extremely dim field.

I used to get around this by screwing the eyepiece a little to one side, so that the middle wire appeared off centre, with roughly enough shift to bring the next wire to the centre of the field of view. This had the advantage that the image of the flame no longer interfered much with the observations. It worked better than with the eyepiece centred, but still fell short of what one might wish for. It was therefore only a palliative. The following remedy gets to the root of the problem.

Experiments soon convinced me that the indistinct image of the flame arises solely from the reflection of the flame by lens B. But this image does nothing at all to make the wires or their image reflected by the mercury visible. It only makes them much harder to see. So I remove lens B altogether. The eyepiece then gives somewhat less magnification, to be sure, but still enough, and produces splendidly sharp images. The eyepiece I use on the meridian circle, for example, magnifies 134 times with A and B and 96 times with A alone.

This gives me a small but still adequate, uniformly lit field, whether I use a lamp or daylight (in the latter case, perhaps with nothing more than a piece of mirror held in the hand). The essential point is that there must be no glass between the mirror inclined at $45^\circ$ and the system of wires.
