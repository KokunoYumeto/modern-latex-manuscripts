His Royal Highness the Duke of \textsc{Clarence} has shown his gracious regard for the university by presenting its library with a valuable collection of nautical charts in 182 sheets. The charts are issued by the Hydrographical Office in London and bear its stamp. They are intended solely for the Royal Navy and are unavailable through booksellers. The collection covers the waters of Europe as well as most of the coasts of Africa, America, and the East and West Indies, many of which the Duke, himself a seaman, knows firsthand. This will suffice to indicate both their value and our gratitude, since a list of the individual charts would be out of place in these pages.

The university has also received a token of the gracious regard of His Royal Highness the Duke of \textsc{Sussex}, who has made two gifts to the observatory. They are a clock for measuring fractions of a second, made by \textsc{Hardy} in London, of a distinctive design and exceptionally fine workmanship, and an apparatus that might be called an inverted pendulum, which demonstrates the practical use of an ingenious idea. An ordinary pendulum is fixed at the top and \emph{hangs} from the spring. This one is fixed at the bottom and \emph{stands} on the comparatively strong spring. The pendulum rod is a cylinder 5 inches long with a small spherical weight screwed onto the top. The rod ends at the top in a fine point that moves along a one-inch graduated arc divided into 20 parts. Each division thus actually amounts to a little over 34 minutes. Tests at the observatory showed, however, that a tilt of only 57 seconds produces a deflection of one division, or that this pendulum is as sensitive as an ordinary one 15 feet long. Since the little sphere can be screwed up or down, it is easy, if desired, to adjust it so that a deflection of one division corresponds to exactly one minute. This shows that, with the necessary adjusting screws and due care in its use, the apparatus can in some cases usefully take the place of a spirit level or a plumb line.
