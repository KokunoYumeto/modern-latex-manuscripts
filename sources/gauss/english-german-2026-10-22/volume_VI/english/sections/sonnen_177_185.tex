\fancyhead[L]{\small TABLE FOR THE SUN’S COORDINATES}
\begin{center}
{\small TABLE FOR THE SUN’S COORDINATES.\par}
\medskip\hrule\medskip
{\small Monatliche Correspondenz zur Beförderung der Erd- und Himmels-Kunde,\par
edited by Baron \textsc{von Zach}. January 1812.\par}
\medskip\hrule
\end{center}\medskip
I first proposed in 1804, and later perfected in the \emph{Theoria motus corporum coelestium}, a method for calculating the geocentric positions of celestial bodies by referring them \emph{directly} to the equator using three rectangular coordinates. It makes the work so much easier whenever \emph{many} positions have to be calculated that astronomers familiar with the subject have welcomed it and already used it widely. There are two cases in which \emph{many} geocentric positions have to be calculated. Either one wants to compare many observations of a celestial body made over a moderate interval with its elements, in order to determine the mean error of those elements as precisely as possible, or one wants to calculate the geocentric motion of a celestial body in advance to facilitate observations or for other purposes. In the latter case there is no need for very high precision. One does not aim to guarantee arcseconds, and perhaps cannot, but is content with whole arcminutes. It is here that an auxiliary table can make the method much easier still. If, as is permissible \emph{here}, one neglects the perturbations of the Sun’s longitude and of its distance from the Earth, and initially adopts
\SourceLeaf{178}%
fixed values for the eccentricity of the Earth’s orbit, the longitude of perigee and the obliquity of the ecliptic, the Sun’s three coordinates depend only on the mean solar longitude and are therefore easy to tabulate. I calculated such a table for my own use several years ago, taking the elements of the Sun’s orbit for 1800 as a basis and, for convenience, increasing the mean solar longitude by one decimal degree at a time. All that then remained was to add a table of the epochs of the argument at the beginning of each year and of its change for individual days, etc. The table was thus sufficiently accurate for a number of years, as long as the arguments of the Sun’s orbit still differed little from those of 1800. To make the table usable for more distant times as well, I calculated a further table of the secular changes in the coordinates brought about by the secular changes in the elements of the Sun’s orbit. In this way my table could serve for several centuries.

The simplest methods do not always suggest themselves first, and so it was here. If I had increased the arguments of the table by the Sun’s mean daily motion instead of by one decimal degree, the table would have been considerably easier to use. The coordinates given \emph{directly} in the table would then (apart from the secular changes) each have applied to a particular date in any given year, and \emph{all to the same hour}. The preliminary calculations for obtaining the arguments would thus have been almost entirely dispensed with. I therefore had the skilled Mr.\ \textsc{Wachter} recalculate the entire table on this principle. Since the year 1800 is now receding further and further from us, I also advised taking the elements for 1820 as the basis, so that for the time being there would be no need at all to take the table of secular changes into account for a number of years (approximately from 1810 to 1830). The arguments of the table thus proceed in increments of $59'\ 8''33$, but are chosen (for ease of calculation) to start at a point other than 0 and to include the longitude of perihelion itself as one of their terms. I publish here this table, which Mr.\ \textsc{Wachter} has calculated with great care, and I believe that in doing so I am rendering a service to astronomers who carry out these calculations. It remains only for me to add a few words on how to use it.
\SourceLeaf{179}%

Table I gives the epoch, that is, the hours, etc., referred to the Paris meridian, for which Table II applies at the date given as its argument. One must therefore add the epochs in Table I to $0^{\mathrm h}$ on the date in question (only in the years 1792, 1793, 1796 and 1797 does addition become subtraction) to obtain the times for which Table II applies directly. When calculating ephemerides for planets and comets, it makes little difference which hours one chooses, so one can always arrange to use Table II directly without interpolation. Alternatively, to make the interpolation at least \emph{easy}, one can choose times $\frac12$, $\frac13$ or $\frac14$ of a day away from those for which the table applies directly.

If, on the other hand, one wants the Sun’s coordinates for a specified hour, one first subtracts the amount given by the table of epochs (which thus becomes an addition in the years 1792, 1793, 1796 and 1797) and enters Table II with the remainder.

\emph{Example.} The Sun’s coordinates are wanted for 1811 April 11 at $7^{\mathrm h}47^{\mathrm m}18^{\mathrm s}$ Paris mean time (the time of Mr.\ \textsc{von Zach}’s first observation of the comet, corrected for aberration). Thus we have
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
& April $11.\ 7^{\mathrm h}\ 47^{\mathrm m}18^{\mathrm s}=$ April $11.3245$\\
Epoch for 1811 & $.\quad.\quad.\quad1\quad5\quad7\quad26\quad.\quad.\quad.\quad.\quad.\quad.\quad1.2135$\\\cline{2-2}
& April $10\quad2\quad39\quad52=$ April $10.1110$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Mr.\ \textsc{Wachter}’s table then gives
\[
x=+9362.5,\qquad y=+3299.4,\qquad z=+1432.1
\]

The signs in Table III, which gives the secular changes in the coordinates, must be understood algebraically. They apply to the signed values rather than to their magnitudes. Thus, for a year \emph{after} 1820, the secular change increases the magnitude of a negative $x$. To take this into account in our example, we must also add the secular change multiplied by $-\frac9{100}$, giving
\[
\mathrm dx=+0.5,\qquad\mathrm dy=-0.1,\qquad\mathrm dz=-0.1
\]
hence
\[
x=+9363.0,\qquad y=+3299.3,\qquad z=+1432.0
\]
\SourceLeaf{180}%

Furthermore, the table gives all values for a mean Earth–Sun distance $=10000$, so in the usual notation
\[
x=+0.93630,\qquad y=+0.32993,\qquad z=+0.14320
\]
Mr.\ \textsc{von Lindenau} calculated
\[
x=+0.936308,\qquad y=+0.329822,\qquad z=+0.143148
\]

Clearly, one cannot vouch for more than four decimal places, and it would have been superfluous to give Table II to more decimal places, since the periodic perturbations can affect even the fourth decimal place by one or two units. But for \emph{the purpose} for which the table is intended, this accuracy is entirely sufficient, and the table cannot and should not apply when accuracy to within arcseconds is required.
