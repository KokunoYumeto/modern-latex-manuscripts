\section{Jacobson preschemes}
\label{section:IV.10}

\oldpage[IV-3]{95}

We have already had occasion to observe \sref{IV.5.2.5} that even \emph{excellent} preschemes \sref{IV.7.8.5} do not always behave like the ``varieties'' of classical Algebraic Geometry, in particular concerning dimension questions; thus if $X$ is the spectrum of a complete discrete valuation ring, the set of closed points of $X$ (reduced to a single element) is not everywhere dense in $X$, and its complement (also reduced to a single element) is an open set everywhere dense in $X$ whose dimension is zero, hence ${<}\dim(X)$.
We examine in this paragraph general conditions under which such phenomena do not occur; it turns out that, under such conditions, the behaviour is much more satisfactory in many respects for the relations between dimension, codimension, depth, and coprofundity in these preschemes (\sref{IV.10.6} and \sref{IV.10.8}).
Furthermore, in the preschemes considered, the fact that the set of closed points is everywhere dense (and even ``very dense'' \sref{IV.10.1.3}) allows many demonstrations to be simplified; one thus recovers the classical viewpoint of ``algebraic varieties'' which, from our point of view, are the sets of closed points of \emph{algebraic preschemes} over a field, and one makes the link between the language of schemes and that of ``varieties'' or ``algebraic spaces'' of Serre (\sref{IV.10.9} and \sref{IV.10.10}).

\subsection{Very dense subsets of a topological space}
\label{subsection:IV.10.1}

\begin{env}[10.1.1]
\label{IV.10.1.1}
We say that a subset of a topological space $X$ is \emph{quasi-constructible} if it is a finite union of locally closed parts of $X$.
We say that a subset $T$ of $X$ is \emph{locally quasi-constructible} if for every $x\in X$, there exists an open neighbourhood $V$ of $x$ such that $T\cap V$ is quasi-constructible in $V$.
It is clear that every quasi-constructible part of $X$ is locally quasi-constructible: the converse is true if $X$ is quasi-compact.
The reasoning of $(\mathbf{0_{III}}$, 9.1.3), where one omits the word ``retrocompact'', shows that the set of quasi-constructible (resp. locally quasi-constructible) parts of $X$, which we will denote by $\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{c}(X)$ (resp. $\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{qc}(X)$), is stable under finite union, finite intersection, and passage to complements.
If $f:X\to Y$ is a continuous map, it follows immediately from these definitions that, for every quasi-constructible (resp. locally quasi-constructible) part $Z$ of $Y$, $f^{-1}(Z)$ is quasi-constructible (resp. locally quasi-constructible) in $X$.

The constructible (resp. locally constructible) parts of $X$ are evidently quasi-constructible (resp. locally quasi-constructible); the converse is true when $X$ is Noetherian (resp. locally Noetherian).

In what follows, we will denote respectively by $\mathfrak{D}(X)$, $\mathfrak{F}(X)$, $\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{f}(X)$, $\mathfrak{C}(X)$, $\mathfrak{L}\mathrm{c}(X)$ the set of subsets of $X$ that are respectively open, closed, locally closed, constructible, locally constructible.
\end{env}

\begin{proposition}[10.1.2]
\label{IV.10.1.2}
\oldpage[IV-3]{96}
Let $X$ be a topological space, $X_0$ a subset of $X$.
The following conditions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\emph{\alph*)}]
  \item For every non-empty locally closed part $Z$ of $X$, we have $Z\cap X_0\neq\emp$.
  \item[\emph{a')}] For every closed part $Z$ of $X$, we have $Z=\overline{Z\cap X_0}$.
  \item For every non-empty locally quasi-constructible part $Z$ of $X$, we have $\overline{Z}\cap X_0\neq\emp$.
  \item[\emph{b')}] For every locally quasi-constructible part $Z$ of $X$, we have $Z\subset\overline{Z\cap X_0}$ (equivalently, $Z\cap X_0$ is dense in $Z$).
  \item The map $U\mapsto U\cap X_0$ from $\mathfrak{D}(X)$ to $\mathfrak{D}(X_0)$ is injective (hence bijective).
  \item[\emph{c')}] The map $F\mapsto F\cap X_0$ from $\mathfrak{F}(X)$ to $\mathfrak{F}(X_0)$ is injective (hence bijective).
  \item[\emph{c'')}] The map $Z\mapsto Z\cap X_0$ from $\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{c}(X)$ to $\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{c}(X_0)$ is injective (hence bijective).
  \item[\emph{c''')}] The map $Z\mapsto Z\cap X_0$ from $\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{qc}(X)$ to $\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{qc}(X_0)$ is injective.
\end{enumerate}
Furthermore, when these conditions are satisfied, the map $Z\mapsto Z\cap X_0$ from $\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{c}(X)$ to $\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{qc}(X_0)$ is bijective.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Note that the surjectivity assertions in \emph{c)} and \emph{c')} are trivial; they imply that every locally closed part of $X_0$ is the trace on $X_0$ of a locally closed part of $X$, and hence the map $\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{c}(X)\to\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{c}(X_0)$ defined in \emph{c'')} is also surjective.

We will prove the implications
\[
  c''')\Rightarrow c'')\Rightarrow c')\Rightarrow c)\Rightarrow b)\Rightarrow b')\Rightarrow a')\Rightarrow a)\Rightarrow c''').
\]

The first three are trivial.
To see that \emph{c)} implies \emph{b)}, note first that \emph{c)} implies that $X_0$ is \emph{dense} in $X$.
Replacing $X$ and $X_0$ by a suitable open set $U$ of $X$ and $U\cap X_0$ respectively, we can reduce to the case where $Z$ is locally closed in $X$, hence $Z=V\cap\complement W$, where $V$ and $W$ are open in $X$; the hypothesis $Z\neq\emp$ means that $V\not\subset W$, equivalently $V\cup W\neq W$.
By virtue of \emph{c)}, we have $(V\cup W)\cap X_0\neq W\cap X_0$, hence $V\cap X_0\not\subset W$, and therefore $(V\cap\complement W)\cap X_0\neq\emp$.

To see that \emph{b)} implies \emph{b')}, it suffices to apply \emph{b)} to $Z\cap U$, where $U$ is an arbitrary open neighbourhood of a point of $Z$.
As $\overline{Z}\cap X_0\subset\overline{Z}$, it is trivial that \emph{b')} implies \emph{a')}.
To show that \emph{a')} implies \emph{a)}, note that if $Z$ is locally closed in $X$, we can write $Z=F-F'$ where $F$ and $F'$ are closed in $X$ and $F'\subset F$; hence $Z\cap X_0=(F\cap X_0)-(F'\cap X_0)$.
If $Z\cap X_0=\emp$, we would deduce that $F\cap X_0=F'\cap X_0$, hence $F=F'$ by \emph{a')}, that is to say $Z=\emp$.

To see that \emph{a)} implies \emph{c''')}, it suffices to show that if $Z\neq\emp$ is locally quasi-constructible, the relation $Z'\cap X_0=Z''\cap X_0$ is equivalent to $((Z'\cup Z'')-(Z'\cap Z''))\cap X_0=\emp$.
In other words, it suffices to prove that \emph{a)} implies \emph{b)};
moreover, replacing $X$ and $X_0$ by an open set $U$ of $X$ and $U\cap X_0$ respectively, we reduce to the case where $Z$ is locally closed in $X$, whence the conclusion.

\oldpage[IV-3]{97}
It remains to show that the map $\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{qc}(X)\to\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{qc}(X_0)$ is surjective.
Let $Z_0$ be a locally quasi-constructible part of $X_0$: there is a covering $(V_\alpha)$ of $X_0$ by open sets of $X_0$ such that $Z_0\cap V_\alpha$ is quasi-constructible in $V_\alpha$ (and hence also in $X_0$).
By virtue of \emph{c)}, for each $\alpha$ there is a single open set $U_\alpha$ of $X$ such that $X_0\cap U_\alpha=V_\alpha$, and by virtue of \emph{c'')} a single quasi-constructible set $Z_\alpha$ in $X$ such that $Z_0\cap V_\alpha=X_0\cap Z_\alpha$.
If $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are any two indices, we have $Z_\beta\cap U_\alpha\cap Z_0\cap V_\beta=V_\alpha\cap Z_0\cap V_\beta=Z_\alpha\cap X_0\cap V_\beta\subset Z_\alpha\cap X_0$;
as $Z_\beta\cap U_\alpha$ and $Z_\alpha$ are quasi-constructible in $X$, it follows from \emph{c'')} that $Z_\beta\cap U_\alpha\subset Z_\alpha$.
Setting $Z=\bigcup Z_\alpha$, since the $V_\alpha$ cover $X_0$, it follows from \emph{c)} that the $U_\alpha$ cover $X$, and we see that $Z$ is locally quasi-constructible in $X$ and $Z_0=Z\cap X_0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[10.1.3]
\label{IV.10.1.3}
When a subset $X_0$ of a topological space satisfies the equivalent conditions of \sref{IV.10.1.2}, we say that $X_0$ is \emph{very dense} in $X$.
\end{definition}

We have already seen in the course of the proof of \sref{IV.10.1.2} that $X_0$ is then \emph{dense} in $X$.

\begin{corollary}[10.1.4]
\label{IV.10.1.4}
If $X_0$ is very dense in $X$ and $U$ an open subset of $X$, $U\cap X_0$ is very dense in $U$.
Conversely, if $(U_\alpha)$ is an open covering of $X$ such that $U_\alpha\cap X_0$ is very dense in $U_\alpha$ for every $\alpha$, then $X_0$ is very dense in $X$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
As every locally closed part of $U$ is locally closed in $X$, the first assertion follows from criterion~\emph{a)} of \sref{IV.10.1.2}; similarly for the second, since if $Z\neq\emp$ is locally closed in $X$, $Z\cap U_\alpha$ is locally closed in $U_\alpha$ for every $\alpha$, and $Z\cap U_\alpha\neq\emp$ for at least one $\alpha$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Quasi-homeomorphisms}
\label{subsection:IV.10.2}

\begin{proposition}[10.2.1]
\label{IV.10.2.1}
Let $X_0$, $X$ be two topological spaces, $f:X_0\to X$ a continuous map.
The following conditions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\emph{\alph*)}]
  \item The map $U\mapsto f^{-1}(U)$ from $\mathfrak{D}(X)$ to $\mathfrak{D}(X_0)$ is bijective.
  \item[\emph{a')}] The map $F\mapsto f^{-1}(F)$ from $\mathfrak{F}(X)$ to $\mathfrak{F}(X_0)$ is bijective.
  \item The topology of $X_0$ is the inverse image by $f$ of that of $X$, and $f(X_0)$ is very dense \sref{IV.10.1.3} in $X$.
  \item The functor $\sh{F}\mapsto f^*(\sh{F})$ from the category of sheaves of sets (resp. sheaves of abelian groups) on $X$ to the category of sheaves of sets (resp. sheaves of abelian groups) on $X_0$ is an equivalence of categories.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\oldpage[IV-3]{98}
It is clear that \emph{a)} and \emph{a')} are equivalent, and \emph{a)} implies that the topology of $X_0$ is the inverse image by $f$ of that of $X$.
On the other hand, if $f(X_0)$ is not very dense in $X$, there exist two distinct open sets $U_1$, $U_2$ of $X$ such that $U_1\cap f(X_0)=U_2\cap f(X_0)$, and hence $f^{-1}(U_1)=f^{-1}(U_2)$, which shows that \emph{a)} implies \emph{b)}.
Conversely, condition~\emph{b)} implies that the maps $U\mapsto U\cap f(X_0)$ from $\mathfrak{D}(X)$ to $\mathfrak{D}(f(X_0))$ and $V\mapsto f^{-1}(V)$ from $\mathfrak{D}(f(X_0))$ to $\mathfrak{D}(X_0)$ are bijective, hence so is their composite $U\mapsto f^{-1}(U)$.

To see that \emph{a)} implies \emph{c)}, it suffices to apply the definition of $f^*(\sh{F})$ $(\mathbf{0_I}$, 3.7.1) and the axioms of sheaves: \emph{a)} implies that for every open set $U$ of $X$, the canonical map $\Gamma(U,\sh{F})\to\Gamma(f^{-1}(U),f^*(\sh{F}))$ is a functorial bijection in $\sh{F}$, whence~\emph{c)}.
It remains to show that \emph{c)} implies \emph{b)}.

Suppose first that $f(X_0)$ is not very dense in $X$; there then exist two distinct closed parts $Y\supset Y'$ of $X$ such that $Y\cap f(X_0)=Y'\cap f(X_0)$.
Let $\sh{F}$ (resp. $\sh{F}'$) be the sheaf of abelian groups on $X$ that is the direct image by the canonical injection $Y\to X$ (resp. $Y'\to X$) of the simple sheaf associated to the constant presheaf $\bb{Z}$ on $Y$ (resp. $Y'$); the definition of $f^*$ $(\mathbf{0_I}$, 3.7.1) shows that $f^*(\sh{F})$ is isomorphic to $f^*(\sh{F}')$ but $\sh{F}$ is not isomorphic to $\sh{F}'$, so condition~\emph{c)} is not satisfied.
(One notes that the functor $f^*$ is not even \emph{faithful}, for if $u$ and $v$ are the identity automorphism and the zero endomorphism of $\sh{F}|\sh{F}'$, $f^*(u)$ and $f^*(v)$ are equal.)

Let us now show that \emph{c)} implies that the topology of $X_0$ is the inverse image of that of $X$ by $f$.
Note that if condition~\emph{c)} is satisfied for the category of sheaves of sets, it is also satisfied for the category of sheaves of abelian groups, since it follows immediately from the definitions $(\mathbf{0_{III}}$, 8.2.5) that the latter is none other than the category of \emph{group objects} in the category of sheaves of sets.
It suffices therefore to prove our assertion assuming \emph{c)} verified for the categories of sheaves of abelian groups on $X$ and $X_0$.
Now, if $\bb{Z}_X$ denotes the simple sheaf on $X$ associated to the constant presheaf equal to $\bb{Z}$, we have a canonical isomorphism $\Gamma(X,\sh{F})\xrightarrow{\sim}\Hom(\bb{Z}_X,\sh{F})$ functorial in $\sh{F}$ (by the same reasoning as in $(\mathbf{0_I}$, 5.1.1)); as it is clear by definition $(\mathbf{0_I}$, 3.7.1) that $f^*(\bb{Z}_X)=\bb{Z}_{X_0}$, the hypothesis that $f^*$ is an equivalence implies that the canonical homomorphism $\Gamma(X,\sh{F})\to\Gamma(X_0,f^*(\sh{F}))$ is \emph{bijective} for every sheaf of abelian groups $\sh{F}$ on $X$.
Now, let $Y_0$ be a closed part of $X_0$; let $\sh{F}_0$ be the sheaf that is the direct image by the canonical injection $Y_0\to X_0$ of the simple sheaf associated to the constant presheaf $\bb{Z}$ on $Y_0$.
As $f^*$ is an equivalence, $\sh{F}_0$ is isomorphic to a sheaf of the form $f^*(\sh{F})$, where $\sh{F}$ is a sheaf of abelian groups on $X$.
Consider then the section $s_0$ of $\sh{F}_0$ over $X_0$ such that $(s_0)_{x_0}=1_{x_0}$ if $x_0\in Y_0$, $(s_0)_{x_0}=0_{x_0}$ if $x_0\notin Y_0$.
The preceding remarks show that there exists a section $s$ of $\sh{F}$ over $X$ such that $(s_0)_{x_0}=s_{f(x_0)}$ for every $x_0\in X_0$ (the fibres $(\sh{F}_0)_{x_0}$ and $\sh{F}_{f(x_0)}$ being canonically identified $(\mathbf{0_I}$, 3.7.1)); this implies that $Y_0$ is the inverse image by $f$ of the set of $x\in X$ such that $s_x\neq 0_x$, and $y$ is closed in $X$. Q.E.D.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[10.2.2]
\label{IV.10.2.2}
When a continuous map $f:X_0\to X$ satisfies the equivalent conditions of \sref{IV.10.2.1}, we say that $f$ is a \emph{quasi-homeomorphism} of $X_0$ into $X$.
\end{definition}

By virtue of \sref{IV.10.2.1}[b)], to say that a subset $X_0$ of a topological space $X$ is very dense in $X$ means therefore that the canonical injection $X_0\to X$ is a quasi-homeomorphism.

\begin{corollary}[10.2.3]
\label{IV.10.2.3}
The composite of two quasi-homeomorphisms is a quasi-homeomorphism.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
This follows immediately from \sref{IV.10.2.1}[a)].
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[10.2.4]
\label{IV.10.2.4}
Let $f:X\to Y$ be a quasi-homeomorphism, $Y'$ a locally quasi-constructible part of $Y$, $X'=f^{-1}(Y')$; then the restriction $f'=f|X':X'\to Y'$ is a quasi-homeomorphism.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
\oldpage[IV-3]{99}
It is clear by virtue of \sref{IV.10.2.1}[b)] that the topology induced on $X'$ by that of $X$ is the inverse image of the topology induced on $Y'$ by that of $Y$.
Let $Z\neq\emp$ be a closed part \emph{in} $Y'$, and $z\in Z$; there is an open neighbourhood $U$ of $z$ in $Y$ such that $U\cap Y'$ is the union of a finite number of parts $Y_i'$ closed in $U$; if $j$ is an index such that $z\in Y_j'$, $Z\cap Y_j'$ is closed in $U$.
Since $f(X)\cap U$ is very dense in $U$ \sref{IV.10.1.4}, $Z\cap Y_j'\cap f(X)$ is non-empty \sref{IV.10.1.2}[a)], and \emph{a fortiori} $Z\cap f(X)$; but as $Z\subset Y'$, we have $Z\cap f(X)=Z\cap f'(X')$; the criterion \sref{IV.10.1.2}[a)] shows that $f'(X')$ is very dense in $Y'$, and we conclude with the help of \sref{IV.10.2.1}[b)].
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[10.2.5]
\label{IV.10.2.5}
Let $f:X\to Y$ be a continuous map, $(V_\alpha)$ an open covering of $Y$.
If, for every $\alpha$, the restriction $f^{-1}(V_\alpha)\to V_\alpha$ of $f$ is a quasi-homeomorphism, then $f$ is a quasi-homeomorphism.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
This follows immediately from the criterion \sref{IV.10.2.1}[b)] and \sref{IV.10.1.4}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[10.2.6]
\label{IV.10.2.6}
Let $f:X\to Y$ be a quasi-homeomorphism, $Y'$ a locally quasi-constructible part of $Y$, $X'=f^{-1}(Y')$.
For $Y'$ to be quasi-compact (resp. Noetherian, resp. retrocompact in $Y$), it is necessary and sufficient that $X'$ be quasi-compact (resp. Noetherian, resp. retrocompact in $X$).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Let us prove the first two assertions; by virtue of \sref{IV.10.2.4}, we can restrict to the case where $Y'=Y$.
To say that $X$ is quasi-compact (resp. Noetherian) means that for every increasing filtered family $(U_\alpha)$ in $\mathfrak{D}(X)$ having $X$ as its greatest element (resp. for every increasing filtered family $(U_\alpha)$ in $\mathfrak{D}(X)$), there exists $\gamma$ such that $U_\alpha=U_\gamma$ for $\alpha\geq\gamma$.
As $U\mapsto f^{-1}(U)$ is a bijection from $\mathfrak{D}(Y)$ to $\mathfrak{D}(X)$, our assertion follows immediately from the preceding remark.

The quasi-compact open sets of $X$ are therefore the sets of the form $f^{-1}(U)$ where $U$ is a quasi-compact open set in $Y$, by virtue of \sref{IV.10.2.1}[a)] and of what precedes.
For $X'$ to be retrocompact in $X$, it is necessary and sufficient that for every quasi-compact open set $U$ in $Y$, $f^{-1}(U)\cap X'=f^{-1}(U\cap Y')$ be quasi-compact $(\mathbf{0_{III}}$, 9.1.1);
the first part of the proof shows that this is equivalent to saying that $U\cap Y'$ is quasi-compact for every quasi-compact open set $U$, that is to say that $Y'$ is retrocompact in $Y$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[10.2.7]
\label{IV.10.2.7}
Let $f:X\to Y$ be a quasi-homeomorphism.
Then the map $Z\mapsto f^{-1}(Z)$ from $\mathfrak{P}(Y)$ to $\mathfrak{P}(X)$ defines by restriction the following bijections (cf.~\sref{IV.10.1.1} for the notation):
\begin{align*}
  \mathfrak{D}(Y)&\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathfrak{D}(X)\\
  \mathfrak{F}(Y)&\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathfrak{F}(X)\\
  \mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{f}(Y)&\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{f}(X)\\
  \mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{c}(Y)&\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{c}(X)\\
  \mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{qc}(Y)&\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{qc}(X)\\
  \mathfrak{C}(Y)&\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathfrak{C}(X)\\
  \mathfrak{L}\mathrm{c}(Y)&\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathfrak{L}\mathrm{c}(X).
\end{align*}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\oldpage[IV-3]{100}
For the first two, this is the definition \sref{IV.10.2.2}; as the topology of $X$ is the inverse image by $f$ of that of $f(X)$, the five remaining maps, where one replaces $Y$ by $f(X)$, are bijective.
One can therefore (by \sref{IV.10.2.1}[b)]) restrict to the case where $X$ is a very dense subspace of $Y$, and the fact that the maps $\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{f}(Y)\to\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{f}(X)$, $\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{c}(Y)\to\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{c}(X)$, $\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{qc}(Y)\to\mathfrak{Q}\mathrm{qc}(X)$ are bijective has already been proved \sref{IV.10.1.2}.
Every locally constructible part being locally quasi-constructible, the maps $\mathfrak{C}(Y)\to\mathfrak{C}(X)$ and $\mathfrak{L}\mathrm{c}(Y)\to\mathfrak{L}\mathrm{c}(X)$ are injective; moreover, for every open set $U\subset Y$, the restriction $f^{-1}(U)\to U$ of $f$ is a quasi-homeomorphism \sref{IV.10.2.4}, hence if one shows that $\mathfrak{C}(Y)\to\mathfrak{C}(X)$ is surjective, the same will be true of $\mathfrak{L}\mathrm{c}(Y)\to\mathfrak{L}\mathrm{c}(X)$.
But by virtue of \sref{IV.10.2.6}, every retrocompact open part $Z$ in $X$ is of the form $f^{-1}(T)$, where $T$ is a retrocompact open set in $Y$; this clearly proves the surjectivity of $\mathfrak{C}(Y)\to\mathfrak{C}(X)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remarks}[10.2.8]
\label{IV.10.2.8}
(i)
One has seen in the proof of \sref{IV.10.2.1} that if $f$ is a quasi-homeomorphism, the canonical map
\[
\label{IV.10.2.8.1}\tag{10.2.8.1}
  \Gamma(Y,\sh{F})\to\Gamma(X,f^*(\sh{F}))
\]
is an isomorphism of abelian groups, functorial in $\sh{F}$, in the category of sheaves of abelian groups on $X$.
As $f^*$ is exact in this category $(\mathbf{0_I}$, 3.7.2), this implies that the canonical homomorphisms
\[
  \mathrm{H}^i(Y,\sh{F})\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathrm{H}^i(X,f^*(\sh{F}))
\]
are \emph{bijective} for all $i$.

(ii)
If $f:X\to Y$ is a continuous map and $\sh{F}$, $\sh{G}$ two sheaves of abelian groups on $Y$, we have $f^*(\sh{F}\otimes_{\bb{Z}_Y}\sh{G})=f^*(\sh{F})\otimes_{\bb{Z}_X}f^*(\sh{G})$ up to canonical isomorphism $(\mathbf{0_I}$, 4.3.3).
We conclude that if $f$ is a quasi-homeomorphism, $f^*$ is again an equivalence of the category of sheaves of rings on $Y$ and the category of sheaves of rings on $X$; the datum of a structure of ringed space on $Y$ is therefore equivalent to that of a structure of ringed space on $X$.

Given two ringed spaces $(X,\sh{O}_X)$, $(Y,\sh{O}_Y)$, we say that a morphism of ringed spaces $f=(\psi,\theta):(X,\sh{O}_X)\to(Y,\sh{O}_Y)$ is a \emph{quasi-isomorphism} if $\psi$ is a quasi-homeomorphism of $X$ into $Y$ and $\theta^\#:\psi^*(\sh{O}_Y)\to\sh{O}_X$ is an isomorphism of sheaves of rings; in this case, the ringed space $(X,\sh{O}_X)$ is entirely determined, up to isomorphism, by $(Y,\sh{O}_Y)$, the space $X$, and the quasi-homeomorphism $\psi$ (which one can take arbitrarily).
When $f$ is a quasi-isomorphism of ringed spaces, the functor
\[
  \sh{F}\mapsto f^*(\sh{F})
\]
is an equivalence of the category of $\sh{O}_Y$-Modules and that of $\sh{O}_X$-Modules, since $f^*(\sh{F})$ is canonically identified here with $\psi^*(\sh{F})$.
We conclude for example with isomorphisms of bi-$\delta$-functors
\[
  \operatorname{Ext}^i_{\sh{O}_Y}(\sh{F},\sh{G})\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{Ext}^i_{\sh{O}_X}(f^*(\sh{F}),f^*(\sh{G})).
\]

In general, the usual constructions of the theory of sheaves and homological algebra, carried out on the ringed space $Y$ or the ringed space $X$, are equivalent.
\end{remarks}

\subsection{Jacobson spaces}
\label{subsection:IV.10.3}

\oldpage[IV-3]{101}

\begin{definition}[10.3.1]
\label{IV.10.3.1}
We say that a topological space $X$ is a \emph{Jacobson space} if the set $X_0$ of \emph{closed} points of $X$ is very dense in $X$ (equivalently, if the canonical injection $X_0\to X$ is a \emph{quasi-homeomorphism}).
\end{definition}

This means therefore \sref{IV.10.1.2} that every non-empty locally closed (or even merely locally quasi-constructible) part $Z$ of $X$ contains a closed point of $X$, or equivalently that \emph{every closed part of $X$ is the closure of the set of its closed points}.

\begin{proposition}[10.3.2]
\label{IV.10.3.2}
Let $X$ be a Jacobson space, $Z$ a locally quasi-constructible part of $X$; then the subspace $Z$ of $X$ is a Jacobson space, and for a point of $Z$ to be closed in $Z$, it is necessary and sufficient that it be closed in $X$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $X_0$ is the set of closed points of $X$, $Z\cap X_0$ is very dense in $Z$ by virtue of \sref{IV.10.2.4} applied to the injection $i:X_0\to X$; as the set $Z_0$ of closed points of $Z$ evidently contains $Z\cap X_0$, it is \emph{a fortiori} very dense in $Z$, hence $Z$ is a Jacobson space.
Let us now show that in fact $Z_0=Z\cap X_0$; let $x\in Z$ be a closed point of $Z$; let $\{x\}$ be its closure in $X$;  $\overline{\{x\}}\cap Z=\{x\}$ is a locally quasi-constructible part, and as its intersection with $X_0$ is non-empty \sref{IV.10.1.2}, we have $x\in X_0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[10.3.3]
\label{IV.10.3.3}
Let $X$ be a topological space, $(U_\alpha)$ an open covering of $X$.
For $X$ to be a Jacobson space, it is necessary and sufficient that each of the subspaces $U_\alpha$ be a Jacobson space.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The condition is necessary by virtue of \sref{IV.10.3.2}.
Conversely, let us show that the hypothesis that the $U_\alpha$ are Jacobson spaces implies that for a closed point $x\in X$ to be closed in $X$, it suffices that it be closed in $U_\alpha$.
It suffices indeed to see that $x$ is also closed in each of the $U_\beta$ that contain it; but $U_\alpha\cap U_\beta$ is open in $U_\alpha$, hence $x$ is closed in $U_\alpha\cap U_\beta$, and by virtue of \sref{IV.10.3.2}, $x$ is also closed in $U_\beta$, which completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Jacobson preschemes and Jacobson rings}
\label{subsection:IV.10.4}

\begin{definition}[10.4.1]
\label{IV.10.4.1}
We say that a prescheme $X$ is a \emph{Jacobson prescheme} if the underlying topological space of $X$ is a Jacobson space.
We say that a ring $A$ is a \emph{Jacobson ring} if $\Spec(A)$ is a Jacobson prescheme.
\end{definition}

Every closed part of $X=\Spec(A)$ is of the form $Z=\mathrm{V}(\mathfrak{a})$, where $\mathfrak{a}$ is an ideal equal to its radical, and the set $X_0$ of closed points of $X$ is the set of maximal ideals of $A$; to say that $Z\cap X_0$ is dense in $Z$ therefore means that $\mathfrak{a}$ is an \emph{intersection of maximal ideals} \sref[I]{I.1.1.4}; as $\mathfrak{a}$ is an intersection of prime ideals, this is equivalent to saying that every prime ideal of $A$ is an intersection of maximal ideals; by virtue of \sref{IV.10.3.1} and \sref{IV.10.1.2}, the usual definition of Jacobson rings (Bourbaki, \emph{Alg.~comm.}, chap.~V, \textsection\,3, n\textsuperscript{o}\,4, d\'{e}f.~1) therefore coincides with the definition \sref{IV.10.4.1}.

\begin{proposition}[10.4.2]
\label{IV.10.4.2}
\oldpage[IV-3]{102}
Let $X$ be a prescheme, $(U_\alpha)$ a covering of $X$ by affine open sets.
For $X$ to be a Jacobson prescheme, it is necessary and sufficient that the rings $\Gamma(U_\alpha,\sh{O}_X)$ be Jacobson rings.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
This follows from \sref{IV.10.3.3} and the definition \sref{IV.10.4.1}.
\end{proof}

\begin{env}[10.4.3]
\label{IV.10.4.3}
A discrete prescheme is a Jacobson prescheme; an Artinian ring is therefore a Jacobson ring.
Every principal ideal domain having an infinity of maximal ideals (for example $\bb{Z}$) is a Jacobson ring; a local Noetherian ring $A$ is a Jacobson ring if and only if its maximal ideal is its only prime ideal, that is to say if $A$ is Artinian.
Every sub-prescheme of a Jacobson prescheme is a Jacobson prescheme by virtue of \sref{IV.10.3.2}.
\end{env}

\begin{proposition}[10.4.4]
\label{IV.10.4.4}
Let $B$ be an integral ring.
The following conditions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\emph{\alph*)}]
  \item There exists $f\neq 0$ in $B$ such that $B_f$ is a field.
  \item The field of fractions of $B$ is a $B$-algebra of finite type.
  \item There exists a field $K$ containing $B$ which is a $B$-algebra of finite type.
  \item The generic point of $\Spec(B)$ is isolated in $\Spec(B)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
It is clear that \emph{d)} is equivalent to \emph{a)}, since \emph{d)} means that there exists $f\neq 0$ in $B$ such that $\mathrm{D}(f)$ is reduced to the generic point of $\Spec(B)$.
It is trivial that \emph{a)} implies \emph{b)} and \emph{b)} implies \emph{c)}.
Finally, \emph{c)} implies \emph{a)}, by virtue of Bourbaki, \emph{Alg.~comm.}, chap.~V, \textsection\,3, n\textsuperscript{o}\,1, cor.~2 of th.~1.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[10.4.5]
\label{IV.10.4.5}
Let $A$ be a ring.
The following conditions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\emph{\alph*)}]
  \item $A$ is a Jacobson ring.
  \item For every non-maximal prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$ of $A$ and every $f\neq 0$ in $B=A/\mathfrak{p}$, $B_f$ is not a field.
  \item[\emph{b')}] Every $A$-algebra of finite type $K$ that is a field is a finite $A$-algebra.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We know that \emph{a)} implies \emph{b')} (Bourbaki, \emph{Alg.~comm.}, chap.~V, \textsection\,3, n\textsuperscript{o}\,4, cor.~3 of th.~3).
Moreover, the kernel of the homomorphism $A\to K$ is then a \emph{maximal} ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ of $A$, and $K$ is a \emph{finite} extension of $A/\mathfrak{m}$ (\emph{loc.~cit.}).
It is trivial that \emph{b')} implies \emph{b)}, since $A/\mathfrak{p}$ is not a field, every $B$-algebra of finite type that is a field is an $A$-algebra of finite type, and $B_f$ is a $B$-algebra of finite type.
It remains to see that \emph{b)} implies \emph{a)}.
We will use the following lemma:
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[10.4.5.1]
\label{IV.10.4.5.1}
Let $X$ be a topological space having the following property: for every non-empty locally closed part $Z$ of $X$, there exists a part $Z'$ of $Z$, locally closed in $X$ (or in $Z$, which amounts to the same), and a point $x\in Z'$, closed in $Z'$.
Then, for $X$ to be a Jacobson space, it is necessary and sufficient that no non-closed point $x$ of $X$ be isolated in $\overline{\{x\}}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $X$ is a Jacobson space, a non-closed point $x$ of $X$ cannot be isolated in $\overline{\{x\}}$, for this would mean that there exists an open set $U$ of $X$ such that $U\cap\overline{\{x\}}=\{x\}$; but $U\cap\overline{\{x\}}$ is locally closed in $X$ and would contain no closed point of $X$, which is contrary to the hypothesis.
Conversely, suppose the condition of the statement is satisfied; then the set of closed points $X_0$ of $X$ is identical to the set of $x\in X$ that are isolated in $\overline{\{x\}}$; but it follows from \sref{IV.5.1.10.1} that
\oldpage[IV-3]{103}
this set is \emph{very dense} in $X$, hence $X$ is a Jacobson space by definition.

Recall \sref{IV.5.1.10} that the hypothesis made on $X$ in \sref{IV.10.4.5.1} is always satisfied when the underlying space of $X$ is a \emph{prescheme}.

Returning to the proof of \sref{IV.10.4.5}, condition~\emph{b)} implies that for every non-closed point $x$ of $\Spec(A)$, the generic point $x$ of $\Spec(A/\mathfrak{j}_x)=\mathrm{V}(\mathfrak{j}_x)=\overline{\{x\}}$ is not isolated in $\overline{\{x\}}$, hence \emph{b)} implies \emph{a)} by virtue of the lemma \sref{IV.10.4.5.1} and \sref{IV.10.4.4}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[10.4.6]
\label{IV.10.4.6}
Every algebra of finite type $B$ over a Jacobson ring $A$ is a Jacobson ring, and the inverse image in $A$ of every maximal ideal of $B$ is a maximal ideal of $A$.
In particular, every algebra of finite type over a field or over $\bb{Z}$ is a Jacobson ring.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
A $B$-algebra of finite type $K$ which is a field is also an $A$-algebra of finite type, hence an $A$-algebra of finite type and \emph{a fortiori} a $B$-module of finite type, whence the first assertion; the second was demonstrated in the course of the proof of \sref{IV.10.4.5}, applied to $K=B/\mathfrak{m}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[10.4.7]
\label{IV.10.4.7}
If $X$ is a Jacobson prescheme, $f:Y\to X$ a morphism locally of finite type, then $Y$ is a Jacobson prescheme and the image by $f$ of every closed point in $Y$ is a closed point in $X$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The question being local on $X$ and on $Y$, we reduce to the case where $X$ and $Y$ are affine, and the corollary then follows from \sref{IV.10.4.6}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[10.4.8]
\label{IV.10.4.8}
If $k$ is an algebraically closed field, $X$ a $k$-prescheme locally of finite type, the set of rational points of $X$ over $k$ is very dense in $X$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Indeed, $X$ is a Jacobson prescheme \sref{IV.10.4.7} and the closed points of $X$ are exactly the rational points over $k$ \sref[I]{I.6.4.2}.
\end{proof}

\begin{env}[10.4.9]
\label{IV.10.4.9}
The fact that preschemes locally of finite type over a field or over $\bb{Z}$ are Jacobson preschemes is particularly important, because of the possibility of reducing to this case in many questions of Algebraic Geometry \sref{IV.8.1.2}[c)].
We will give two examples:
\end{env}

\begin{env}[10.4.10]
\label{IV.10.4.10}
\emph{Applications~I: Proof of \sref{IV.6.15.9}.}
Let $k$ be a separably closed field, $X$ a $k$-prescheme locally of finite type over $k$ and \emph{unibranch}.
We know that the integral closure of a $k$-algebra of finite type $A$ in a finite extension of its field of fractions is a finite $A$-algebra (Bourbaki, \emph{Alg.~comm.}, chap.~V, \textsection\,3, n\textsuperscript{o}\,2, th.~2), hence every $k$-algebra of finite type is a universally Japanese ring; we conclude that the set of points $x\in X$ where $X$ is geometrically unibranch is locally constructible \sref{IV.9.7.10}.
But the hypothesis and the lemma \sref{IV.6.15.8} imply that this set contains all the \emph{closed} points of $X$.
The conclusion follows therefore from \sref{IV.10.4.6}, \sref{IV.10.3.1}, and the bijectivity of the map $\mathfrak{L}\mathrm{c}(X)\to\mathfrak{L}\mathrm{c}(X_0)$ (where $X_0$ is the set of closed points of $X$) \sref{IV.10.2.7}.
\end{env}

\begin{env}[10.4.11]
\label{IV.10.4.11}
\emph{Applications~II:}
\end{env}

\begin{proposition}[10.4.11]
\label{IV.10.4.11.prop}
Let $S$ be a prescheme, $X$ an $S$-prescheme of finite type.
Every $S$-endomorphism of $X$ which is radicial is surjective (hence bijective).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\oldpage[IV-3]{104}
Let $f:X\to S$ be the structural morphism, $g:X\to X$ the $S$-endomorphism considered, and, for every $s\in S$, let $g_s$ be the morphism deduced from $g$ by the base change $\Spec(k(s))\to S$, which is a $k(s)$-endomorphism of the fibre $f^{-1}(s)=X\times_S\Spec(k(s))$.

To prove that $g$ is surjective, it suffices to prove that $g_s$ is surjective for every $s\in S$, hence one can (by virtue of \sref[I]{I.3.5.7}) restrict to the case of a field $k$, in which case $f$ is a morphism of finite presentation, since $S$ is Noetherian.
Applying \sref{IV.8.9.1} and \sref{IV.8.10.5}[vii)], we reduce to the case where $S=\Spec(A)$, where $A$ is a sub-$\bb{Z}$-algebra of finite type of $k$.
But $X$ is then a Jacobson prescheme \sref{IV.10.4.7}, and $g(X)$ is constructible in $X$ \sref{IV.1.8.5}, hence, to prove that $g(X)=X$, it suffices to show that $g(X)$ contains all the closed points of $X$ \sref{IV.10.3.1}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[10.4.11.1]
\label{IV.10.4.11.1}
Let $Y$ be a $\bb{Z}$-prescheme of finite type.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item For a point $y\in Y$ to be closed in $Y$, it is necessary and sufficient that $k(y)$ be a finite field.
  \item For every prime number $p$ and every integer $d\geq 1$, the set of points $y\in Y$ such that $k(y)$ is an extension of $\bb{F}_p$ whose degree divides $d$ is finite.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Assertion (i) follows from the fact that the image of a closed point $y\in Y$ in $\Spec(\bb{Z})$ is a closed point \sref{IV.10.4.7}, hence a prime number, and from \sref[I]{I.6.4.2}.
On the other hand, as $Y$ is a finite union of affine open sets of finite type over $\bb{Z}$, we can restrict to proving (ii) in the case where $Y=\Spec(C)$, where $C$ is a $\bb{Z}$-algebra of finite type.
Now, the points $y\in Y$ such that the degree of $k(y)$ over $\bb{F}_p$ divides $d$ correspond bijectively to the homomorphisms $C\to\bb{F}_{p^d}$; but if $(t_i)$ is a system of generators of the $\bb{Z}$-algebra $C$, every homomorphism of $C$ is determined by its values on the elements $t_i$, and hence there are at most a finite number of homomorphisms of $C$ into a finite field.
\end{proof}

Since $X$ is a $\bb{Z}$-prescheme of finite type, let $T_{p,d}$ be the set of closed points $z\in X$ such that $k(z)$ is an extension of $\bb{F}_p$ of degree dividing $d$; it follows from \sref{IV.10.4.11.1} that the set $T_{p,d}$ is finite and that the set of closed points of $X$ is the union of the $T_{p,d}$.
Moreover, if $z\in T_{p,d}$ and if $h$ is any endomorphism of $X$, $h(z)\in T_{p,d}$, that is to say $T_{p,d}$ is \emph{stable} under every endomorphism of $X$.
As $g$ is by hypothesis injective, its restriction to $T_{p,d}$ is \emph{bijective} since $T_{p,d}$ is finite, which completes the proof of the proposition.

We will see later \sref{IV.17.9.6} that when one furthermore supposes that $X$ is an $S$-prescheme of finite presentation, and moreover that $g$ is a monomorphism, then one can affirm that $g$ is an \emph{automorphism} of $X$.

\subsection{Noetherian Jacobson preschemes}
\label{subsection:IV.10.5}

\begin{proposition}[10.5.1]
\label{IV.10.5.1}
\oldpage[IV-3]{105}
Let $B$ be an integral Noetherian ring.
The conditions equivalent \emph{a)} to \emph{d)} of \sref{IV.10.4.4} are then also equivalent to the following:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\emph{\alph*)}]\setcounter{enumi}{4}
  \item $\Spec(B)$ is finite.
  \item $B$ is a semi-local ring of dimension $\leq 1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
It follows from the Artin--Tate theorem $(\mathbf{0}$, 16.3.3) that conditions \emph{a)} and \emph{f)} are equivalent.
Condition~\emph{f)} implies that $\Spec(B)$ is the union of the finite set of its closed points and its generic point, hence \emph{f)} implies \emph{e)} without supposing $A$ Noetherian.
Finally \emph{e)} implies \emph{d)} without supposing $A$ Noetherian, for the generic point $x$ of $X$ is the complement of the union of the closures $\overline{\{y\}}$, where $y$ runs through the set of points $y\neq x$, and as these points are finite in number, $\{x\}$ is the complement of a closed set in $X$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[10.5.2]
\label{IV.10.5.2}
Let $A$ be a Noetherian ring.
For $A$ to be a Jacobson ring, it is necessary and sufficient that there exist no prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$ of $A$ such that $A/\mathfrak{p}$ is a semi-local ring of dimension~$1$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
This follows immediately from \sref{IV.10.5.1} and condition~\emph{b)} of \sref{IV.10.4.5}, the prime ideals $\mathfrak{p}$ such that $A/\mathfrak{p}$ is semi-local of dimension~$0$ being the maximal ideals of $A$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[10.5.3]
\label{IV.10.5.3}
Let $X$ be a Noetherian irreducible prescheme.
The following conditions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\emph{\alph*)}]
  \item The generic point of $X$ is isolated.
  \item $X$ is finite.
  \item $X$ is of dimension $\leq 1$ and the set of its closed points is finite.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
There exists a finite number of irreducible affine open sets $U_i$ ($1\leq i\leq n$) covering $X$, each of which contains the generic point of $X$; it suffices to prove the equivalence of \emph{a)}, \emph{b)}, and \emph{c)} for each of the $(U_i)_{\mathrm{red}}$ (taking into account $(\mathbf{0}$, 14.1.7)).
But this equivalence follows from \sref{IV.10.5.1}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[10.5.4]
\label{IV.10.5.4}
A Noetherian prescheme $X$ satisfying the equivalent conditions of \sref{IV.10.5.3} is not necessarily an affine scheme; in fact it can even be \emph{non-separated}.
One obtains an example by replacing, in the example \sref[I]{I.5.5.11} of the ``affine line with doubled point'', $X_1$ and $X_2$ by the spectra of the valuation ring of a discrete valuation, $U_{12}$ and $U_{21}$ by the open sets reduced to the generic point in $X_1$ and $X_2$ respectively; the non-separated prescheme $X$ thus obtained has exactly $3$ points.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[10.5.5]
\label{IV.10.5.5}
Let $X$ be a Noetherian prescheme.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item The set $X_0$ of points $x\in X$ such that $\overline{\{x\}}$ is finite is very dense in $X$.
  \item For $X$ to be a Jacobson prescheme, it is necessary and sufficient that there exist no sub-prescheme of $X$ isomorphic to the spectrum of an integral semi-local ring of dimension~$1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The condition that $\overline{\{x\}}$ is finite is equivalent to the fact that $x$ is isolated in $\overline{\{x\}}$ \sref{IV.10.5.3}, hence $\overline{\{x\}}$, being the underlying space of a sub-prescheme (Noetherian) of $X$, and assertion~(i) follows from \sref{IV.5.1.10.1}; taking into account \sref{IV.10.4.5.1}, for a non-closed point $x$ of $X$ to belong to $X_0$, it is necessary and sufficient that the sub-prescheme closed and integral $Y$ of $X$ having $\overline{\{x\}}$ as underlying space be of dimension~$1$ and finite, hence a finite union of sub-preschemes that are spectra of integral semi-local rings of dimension~$1$.
Conversely, if there exists a sub-prescheme $Z$ of $X$ which is the spectrum of an integral semi-local ring of dimension~$1$, $Z$ is not a Jacobson prescheme \sref{IV.10.5.2}, hence neither is $X$ \sref{IV.10.3.2}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[10.5.6]
\label{IV.10.5.6}
\oldpage[IV-3]{106}
Assertion~(ii) of \sref{IV.10.5.5} is still valid when $X$ is \emph{locally Noetherian}: indeed, if $(V_\alpha)$ is a covering of $X$ by affine Noetherian open sets, every sub-prescheme of one of the $V_\alpha$ is a sub-prescheme of $X$; conversely, if a sub-prescheme $Z$ of $X$ is isomorphic to the spectrum of an integral semi-local ring of dimension~$1$, there is an $\alpha$ such that $V_\alpha\cap Z$ contains an affine open set $U$ of $Z$ not reduced to the generic point of $Z$, which is therefore also the spectrum of an integral semi-local ring of dimension~$1$.
We conclude with the help of \sref{IV.10.3.3}.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[10.5.7]
\label{IV.10.5.7}
Let $X$ be a locally Noetherian prescheme, $Y$ a closed part of $X$ such that every non-empty closed part of $X$ meets $Y$.
Then the prescheme induced on the open set $X-Y$ is a Jacobson prescheme.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let us apply the criterion \sref{IV.10.5.5}[ii)], and suppose that there is a sub-prescheme $Z$ of $X-Y$ which is the spectrum of an integral semi-local ring of dimension~$1$, with generic $z$ of $Z$ being therefore isolated in $Z$ (or in the closure $\overline{Z}$ of $Z$ in $X$), and $Z$ being distinct from $\{z\}$.
Let $y\neq z$ be a point of $Z$; as it does not belong to $Y$, it is not a closed point of $X$, and its closure $\overline{\{y\}}$ in $X$ meets $Y$ at a point $x\neq y$, which is therefore not in $Z$.
The existence of the chain $\{x\}\subset\overline{\{y\}}\subset\overline{\{z\}}$ shows that the dimension of $\overline{\{z\}}$ would be $\geq 2$, and it would be the same for the dimension of $U\cap\overline{\{z\}}$, where $U$ is an affine open neighbourhood (hence Noetherian) of $x$ in $X$.
But this contradicts the fact that the generic point $z$ of $U\cap\overline{\{z\}}$ is isolated in $U\cap\overline{\{z\}}$ \sref{IV.10.5.3}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[10.5.8]
\label{IV.10.5.8}
Let $A$ be a Noetherian ring; for every element $f$ of the radical $\mathfrak{R}$ of $A$, the ring $A_f$ is a Jacobson ring, and the open set $\Spec(A)-\mathrm{V}(\mathfrak{R})$ is a Jacobson scheme.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Setting $X=\Spec(A)$, $Y=\mathrm{V}(\mathfrak{J})$, where $\mathfrak{J}$ is an ideal of $A$, to say that every non-empty closed part of $X$ meets $Y$ is equivalent $(\mathbf{0_I}$, 2.1.3) to saying that $Y$ contains all the \emph{closed} points of $X$, or equivalently that $\mathfrak{J}$ is \emph{contained in the radical} $\mathfrak{R}$ of $A$.
If $f\in\mathfrak{R}$, the open set $\mathrm{D}(f)$ does not meet $\mathrm{V}(\mathfrak{R})$, and is a Jacobson space by virtue of \sref{IV.10.5.7}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[10.5.9]
\label{IV.10.5.9}
Let $A$ be a local Noetherian ring, $\mathfrak{m}$ its maximal ideal, $X=\Spec(A)$, $Y=X-\{\mathfrak{m}\}$; then $Y$ is a Jacobson scheme, whose closed points are the prime ideals $\mathfrak{p}\in\Spec(A)$ such that $\dim(A/\mathfrak{p})=1$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The first assertion is a particular case of \sref{IV.10.5.8}; on the other hand, the closed points of $Y$ are the prime ideals $\mathfrak{p}$ of $A$ which are maximal elements of the set of prime ideals $\neq\mathfrak{m}$, which, by definition of the dimension, means that $\dim(A/\mathfrak{p})=1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[10.5.10]
\label{IV.10.5.10}
Let $A$ be a local Noetherian ring, reduced and complete, and which is not a field.
Then the finite intersections of the kernels of the local homomorphisms of $A$ into discrete valuation rings $V$, which make $V$ a finite $A$-algebra, form a filter base tending towards $0$ for the adic topology of $A$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\oldpage[IV-3]{107}
It suffices (Bourbaki, \emph{Alg.~comm.}, chap.~III, \textsection\,2, n\textsuperscript{o}\,7, prop.~8) to prove that the intersection of the kernels in question is reduced to~$0$.
Suppose first that $A$ is integral and of dimension~$1$; by virtue of the theorem of Nagata $(\mathbf{0}$, 23.1.5 and 23.1.6), the integral closure $A'$ of $A$ is a complete local integral ring and a finite $A$-algebra; it is therefore a discrete valuation ring, and the proposition follows immediately in this case.

Let us pass to the general case; let $x$ be the closed point of $X=\Spec(A)$, and set $Y=X-\{x\}$; we know \sref{IV.10.5.9} that $Y$ is a Jacobson prescheme.
Let us show that the intersection of the prime ideals $\mathfrak{p}$ of $A$ such that $\dim(A/\mathfrak{p})=1$ is reduced to~$0$; the proposition will follow since, for each such prime $\mathfrak{p}$, the intersection of the kernels of the local homomorphisms of $A/\mathfrak{p}$ into discrete valuation rings $V$, making $V$ an $(A/\mathfrak{p})$-algebra of finite type, is reduced to~$0$.
But to say that the intersection of these prime ideals is reduced to~$0$ means that the set of these ideals is dense in $X$, or equivalently that $Y$ is dense in $X$, and this also follows from \sref{IV.10.5.9}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Dimension in Jacobson preschemes}
\label{subsection:IV.10.6}
\label{IV.10.6}

The results of this subsection refine in certain cases and generalize results of \textsection\,5.

\begin{proposition}[10.6.1]
\label{IV.10.6.1}
Let $S$ be a locally Noetherian prescheme, satisfying moreover the following conditions: $1\textsuperscript{o}$~$S$ is a Jacobson prescheme; $2\textsuperscript{o}$~for every $s\in S$, $\sh{O}_{S,s}$ is universally catenary \sref{IV.5.6.2}; $3\textsuperscript{o}$~every irreducible component $S'$ of $S$ is equicodimensional (equivalently, for every closed point $s$ of $S'$ and every sub-prescheme of $S$ having $S'$ as underlying space, we have $\dim(S')=\dim(\sh{O}_{S',s})$).
We then have the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item For every morphism locally of finite type $g:X\to S$, $X$ satisfies the conditions $1\textsuperscript{o}$, $2\textsuperscript{o}$, and $3\textsuperscript{o}$ above.
    In particular, if $X$ is equidimensional (for example if $X$ is irreducible), $X$ is catenary and for every closed point $x$ of $X$, we have $\dim(\sh{O}_{X,x})=\dim(X)$ $(\mathbf{0}$, 14.3.3).
  \item Let $X$, $Y$ be two $S$-preschemes locally of finite type over $S$, $f:X\to Y$ an $S$-morphism; suppose $X$ irreducible and $f$ dominant.
    If $\xi$ (resp. $\eta$) is the generic point of $X$ (resp. $Y$) and $e=\dim(f^{-1}(\eta))=\deg.\mathrm{tr}_{k(\eta)}k(\xi)$, we have
    \[
    \label{IV.10.6.1.1}\tag{10.6.1.1}
      \dim(X)=\dim(Y)+e.
    \]
  \item Let $X$, $Y$ be two $S$-preschemes locally of finite type over $S$, $f:X\to Y$ an $S$-morphism, $n$ an integer $\geq 0$ such that $\dim(f^{-1}(y))\geq n$ (resp. $\dim(f^{-1}(y))\leq n$) for every $y\in Y$.
    Then we have
    \[
    \label{IV.10.6.1.2}\tag{10.6.1.2}
      \dim(X)\geq\dim(Y)+n
    \]
    (resp.
    \[
    \label{IV.10.6.1.3}\tag{10.6.1.3}
      \dim(X)\leq\dim(Y)+n).
    \]
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\oldpage[IV-3]{108}
(i)
Property $1\textsuperscript{o}$ for $X$ follows from \sref{IV.10.4.7}.
For every $x\in X$, $\sh{O}_{X,x}$ is a local ring of an $\sh{O}_{S,g(x)}$-algebra of finite type and a prime ideal, and the homomorphism $\sh{O}_{S,g(x)}\to\sh{O}_{X,x}$ is local; hence \sref{IV.5.6.3}[iv)] $\sh{O}_{X,x}$ is universally catenary.
To show that $X$ satisfies condition $3\textsuperscript{o}$, we consider several cases:

\emph{a)} $X$ is a closed irreducible sub-prescheme of $S$; let $S'$ be an irreducible component of $S$ containing $X$, $\xi$ the generic point of $X$, $x$ a closed point of $X$; for every $s\in S'$, $\sh{O}_{S',s}$, quotient of $\sh{O}_{S,s}$, is catenary \sref{IV.5.6.1}, hence the conditions $2\textsuperscript{o}$ and $3\textsuperscript{o}$ imply that $S'$ is biequidimensional $(\mathbf{0}$, 14.3.3).
By virtue of \sref{IV.5.1.2} and $(\mathbf{0}$, 14.3.3.2), we have
\[
  \dim(\sh{O}_{X,x})=\dim(\sh{O}_{S',x})-\dim(\sh{O}_{S',\xi})=\dim(S')-\dim(\sh{O}_{S',\xi})
\]
which proves that $\dim(\sh{O}_{X,x})$ does not depend on the closed point $x$ considered, whence the assertion in this case \sref{IV.5.1.4}.

\emph{b)} $X$ is irreducible and $g$ dominant.
Then, for every closed point $x$ of $X$, $g(x)$ is a closed point of $S$ by \sref{IV.10.4.7}; as $\sh{O}_{S,g(x)}$ is universally catenary, it follows from \sref{IV.5.6.5.3} that
\[
  \dim(\sh{O}_{X,x})=\dim(\sh{O}_{S,g(x)})+e
\]
where $e=\dim(g^{-1}(\zeta))$, $\zeta$ being the generic point of $S$.
As $\dim(S)=\dim(\sh{O}_{S,g(x)})$ by virtue of condition $3\textsuperscript{o}$ for $S$, we have $\dim(\sh{O}_{X,x})=\dim(S)+e$ for every closed point $x\in X$; this proves condition $3\textsuperscript{o}$ for $X$ \sref{IV.5.1.4}, and at the same time the formula
\[
\label{IV.10.6.1.4}\tag{10.6.1.4}
  \dim(X)=\dim(S)+e.
\]

\emph{c)} General case.
Considering a reduced sub-prescheme $X'$ of $X$ having as underlying space an irreducible component of $X$, we reduce to the case where $X$ is integral; using \sref[I]{I.5.2.2} and the case \emph{a)} proved above, one can then replace $S$ by the sub-prescheme reduced having $g(X)$ as underlying space; we are then reduced to case~\emph{b)}, and this completes the proof of (i).

(ii) The morphism $f$ being locally of finite type \sref[I]{I.1.3.4}, we can apply the results of (i) by replacing $S$ by $Y$; moreover, as $X$ is irreducible and $f$ dominant, \sref{IV.10.6.1.4} gives \sref{IV.10.6.1.1}.

(iii) The assertion relative to the case $\dim(f^{-1}(y))\leq n$ for every $y\in Y$ has already been proved under more general hypotheses in \sref{IV.5.6.7}.
Suppose that $\dim(f^{-1}(y))\geq n$ for every $y\in Y$, and consider a generic point $\eta$ of an irreducible component $Y'$ of $Y$; there exists at least one irreducible component $Z$ of $f^{-1}(\eta)$ of dimension $\geq n$; if $\xi$ is the generic point of $Z$, $\xi$ is also the generic point of an irreducible component $X'$ of $X$ such that $f(X')$ is dense in $Y'$ $(\mathbf{0_I}$, 2.1.8).
Considering the reduced sub-preschemes of $X$, $Y$ having as underlying spaces $X'$, $Y'$ respectively, and the restriction $X'\to Y'$ of $f$ \sref[I]{I.5.2.2}; it follows from (ii) that $\dim(X')\geq\dim(Y')+n$; this being true for every irreducible component $Y'$ of $Y$, we conclude the inequality \sref{IV.10.6.1.2}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[10.6.2]
\label{IV.10.6.2}
\oldpage[IV-3]{109}
Suppose that $X$ satisfies conditions $1\textsuperscript{o}$, $2\textsuperscript{o}$, and $3\textsuperscript{o}$ of \sref{IV.10.6.1}.
Then, for every dense open set $U$ in $X$, we have $\dim(U)=\dim(X)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
One knows that $\dim(U)\leq\dim(X)$ $(\mathbf{0}$, 14.1.4); moreover, as $X$ is a Jacobson prescheme, $U$ contains a closed point of every irreducible component of $X$, hence $\dim(U)=\dim(X)$ by virtue of \sref{IV.10.6.1} and $(\mathbf{0}$, 14.1.2.1).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[10.6.3]
\label{IV.10.6.3}
Suppose that $X$ satisfies conditions $1\textsuperscript{o}$, $2\textsuperscript{o}$, and $3\textsuperscript{o}$ of \sref{IV.10.6.1}, and let $Y$ be a closed part of $X$.
Then, for every $x\in Y$, and every open neighbourhood $U$ of $x$ in $X$ not meeting the irreducible components of $Y$ that do not contain $x$, we have
\[
\label{IV.10.6.3.1}\tag{10.6.3.1}
  \dim_x(Y)=\dim(U\cap Y)=\dim\overline{\{x\}}+\codim(\overline{\{x\}},Y)=\sup_i\dim(Y_i)
\]
where $Y_i$ ($1\leq i\leq m$) are the irreducible components of $Y$ containing $x$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Considering a closed sub-prescheme of $X$ having $Y$ as underlying space, one can restrict, by virtue of \sref{IV.10.6.1}[i)], to the case where $Y=X$.
By virtue of the choice of $U$, $U$ is the union of the $U\cap X_i$, hence $\dim(U)=\sup\dim(U\cap X_i)$; but by \sref{IV.10.6.2} applied to a closed sub-prescheme of $X$ having $X_i$ as underlying space, one has (taking into account \sref{IV.10.6.1}[i)]), $\dim(U\cap X_i)=\dim(X_i)$; this proves that the second and fourth terms of \sref{IV.10.6.3.1} are equal.
As $U\cap X_i$ is biequidimensional \sref{IV.10.6.1}[i)], we have
\[
\label{IV.10.6.3.2}\tag{10.6.3.2}
  \dim(X_i)=\dim(U\cap X_i)=\dim(U\cap\overline{\{x\}})+\codim(U\cap\overline{\{x\}},U\cap X_i)
\]
applied to a closed sub-prescheme of $X$ having $\overline{\{x\}}$ as underlying space, one has $\dim(U\cap\overline{\{x\}})=\dim(\overline{\{x\}})$; as one also has, for the same reasons,
\[
\label{IV.10.6.3.3}\tag{10.6.3.3}
  \dim(X_i)=\dim(\overline{\{x\}})+\codim(\overline{\{x\}},X_i)
\]
we obtain
\[
  \dim(U)=\dim(\overline{\{x\}})+\sup_i\codim(\overline{\{x\}},X_i)=\dim(\overline{\{x\}})+\codim(\overline{\{x\}},X)
\]
by definition of the codimension $(\mathbf{0}$, 14.2.1).
This shows that $\dim(U)$ of the open neighbourhood $U$ of $x$ satisfying the conditions of the statement is \emph{independent} of the choice of $U$, hence is equal to $\dim_x(X)$, by $(\mathbf{0}$, 14.1.4.1).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[10.6.4]
\label{IV.10.6.4}
Under the hypotheses of \sref{IV.10.6.3}, let $\sh{F}$ be a coherent $\sh{O}_X$-Module, $Y$ its support.
For every $x\in Y$, we have
\[
\label{IV.10.6.4.1}\tag{10.6.4.1}
  \dim(\overline{\{x\}})+\dim(\sh{F}_x)=\dim_x Y.
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
This follows from \sref{IV.10.6.3.1} and the formula $\dim(\sh{F}_x)=\codim(\overline{\{x\}},Y)$ \sref{IV.5.1.12.2}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Examples and counter-examples}
\label{subsection:IV.10.7}

\begin{env}[10.7.1]
\label{IV.10.7.1}
Let $S$ be a locally Noetherian prescheme of \emph{dimension $\leq 1$} and suppose that $S$ is a Jacobson prescheme; when $S$ is Noetherian, this amounts to saying that the irreducible components of $S$ of dimension~$1$ are infinite, since every $x\in S$ which is not a closed point is the generic point of such a component \sref{IV.10.4.5} and \sref{IV.10.5.4}.
Then $S$ also satisfies conditions $2\textsuperscript{o}$ and $3\textsuperscript{o}$ of \sref{IV.10.6.1}: indeed, every local ring $\sh{O}_{S,s}$ is of dimension~$0$ or $1$, and is hence necessarily catenary, and by \sref{IV.5.6.4}, universally catenary \sref{IV.7.2.9}; on the other hand, an irreducible component $S'$ of $S$, either is reduced to a point, or is of dimension~$1$, and for every closed point $s\in S'$, $\sh{O}_{S',s}$ is necessarily of dimension~$1$.
We deduce from these remarks and from \sref{IV.10.6.1} that every prescheme locally of finite type over $S$ satisfies also the properties $1\textsuperscript{o}$, $2\textsuperscript{o}$, and $3\textsuperscript{o}$ of \sref{IV.10.6.1}: this includes in particular preschemes \emph{locally of finite type over a field or over $\bb{Z}$}.
\end{env}

\begin{env}[10.7.2]
\label{IV.10.7.2}
\oldpage[IV-3]{110}
Let $A$ be a local Noetherian ring \emph{universally catenary} and let $S$ be the complement of the closed point in $X=\Spec(A)$.
Then $S$ satisfies conditions $1\textsuperscript{o}$, $2\textsuperscript{o}$, and $3\textsuperscript{o}$ of \sref{IV.10.6.1}: indeed, we have already seen that $S$ is a Jacobson prescheme \sref{IV.10.5.9}; similarly, for the same reason as for the local rings $A_\mathfrak{p}$, it is a Jacobson prescheme, and moreover the local rings $\sh{O}_{S,s}=\sh{O}_{X,s}$ are universally catenary.
On the other hand, an irreducible component $X'$ of $X$ is the complement of $\mathfrak{a}$ in $X$; for every closed point $x$ of $S$, the adherence of $x$ is $\{x,\mathfrak{a}\}$, and $X'$ is by hypothesis biequidimensional $(\mathbf{0}$, 14.3.3), hence $\dim(\sh{O}_{X',x})=\dim(X')-1$ $(\mathbf{0}$, 14.3.2), which proves that $S'$ is equicodimensional \sref{IV.5.1.4}.
\end{env}

\begin{env}[10.7.3]
\label{IV.10.7.3}
Let $A$ be a discrete valuation ring; let us show that in the ring $B=A[T_1,\ldots,T_n]$ of polynomials in $n$ indeterminates, there exist two maximal ideals $\mathfrak{m}$, $\mathfrak{n}$ of heights $n$ and $n+1$ respectively.
We have already seen in \sref{IV.5.2.5}[i)] (for $n=1$; the proof is by induction on $n$). Let $A[T_1,\ldots,T_{n+1}]$ be a faithfully flat free $A[T_1,\ldots,T_n]$-module; hence there are in $A[T_1,\ldots,T_{n+1}]$ two maximal ideals $\mathfrak{m}'$, $\mathfrak{n}'$ of heights $n+1$ and $n+2$ respectively \sref{IV.5.5.1}; moreover, by \sref{IV.5.5.3}, these ideals are necessarily of heights $n+1$ and $n+2$.
Suppose $n\geq 2$; let $R=1+3$ which is a multiplicative part of $B$; setting $B'=R^{-1}B$, the ideal $\mathfrak{J}'=R^{-1}\mathfrak{J}$ is contained in the \emph{radical} of $B'$ (Bourbaki, \emph{Alg.~comm.}, chap.~III, \textsection\,3, n\textsuperscript{o}\,5, prop.~12); so the topological subspace $X'=\Spec(B')$ is identified with a subspace of $X$, and the local rings $\sh{O}_{S,x}$ and $\sh{O}_{X',x}$ are the same by \sref[I]{I.1.6.2}.
Considering then in $X'$ the closed set $Y'=\mathrm{V}(\mathfrak{J}')$, and setting $S=X'-Y'$; we know \sref{IV.10.5.7} that $S$ is a Jacobson prescheme, evidently irreducible and Noetherian; moreover the local rings $\sh{O}_{S,s}=\sh{O}_{X',s}$ are universally catenary, being the same as the local rings of $A$.

However, there exist two closed points $a$, $b$ of $S$ such that $\sh{O}_{S,a}$ and $\sh{O}_{S,b}$ \emph{do not have the same dimension}, that is to say $S$ does not satisfy condition $3\textsuperscript{o}$ of \sref{IV.10.6.1}.
To see this, consider the two maximal ideals $\mathfrak{m}'=R^{-1}\mathfrak{m}$ and $\mathfrak{n}'=R^{-1}\mathfrak{n}$, of heights $n$ and $n+1$ respectively; the corresponding points $a'$, $b'$ are the only closed points of $X'$, corresponding to $\mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{b}$.
Since $B_{\mathfrak{m}'}$ is biequidimensional, $\mathfrak{p}'$ is then a prime ideal of height $n-1$.
Similarly $\mathfrak{n}'$ corresponds to a point $b$ of $X'$ such that the adherence of $b$ in $X'$ is $\{b,b'\}$.
Since $a$ and $b$ are closed points of $S$, and are in $S$, this proves our claim.
\end{env}

\subsection{Rectified depth}
\label{subsection:IV.10.8}
\label{IV.10.8}

\begin{definition}[10.8.1]
\label{IV.10.8.1}
Let $X$ be a locally Noetherian prescheme, $\sh{F}$ a coherent $\sh{O}_X$-Module.
For every $x\in X$, we call the \emph{rectified depth} of $\sh{F}$ at the point $x$ and denote by $\operatorname{prof}^*_x(\sh{F})$ the number (an integer $\geq 0$ or $+\infty$) equal to
\[
\label{IV.10.8.1.1}\tag{10.8.1.1}
  \operatorname{prof}^*_x(\sh{F})=\operatorname{prof}(\sh{F}_x)+\dim(\overline{\{x\}})
\]
where $\overline{\{x\}}$ is the closure of the point $x$ in $X$.
For any part $Z$ of $X$, we call the \emph{rectified depth} of $\sh{F}$ along $Z$ and denote by $\operatorname{prof}^*_Z(\sh{F})$ the number
\[
\label{IV.10.8.1.2}\tag{10.8.1.2}
  \operatorname{prof}^*_Z(\sh{F})=\inf_{x\in Z}\operatorname{prof}^*_x(\sh{F}).
\]
\end{definition}

In other terms, for every integer $n$, the relation $\operatorname{prof}^*_Z(\sh{F})\geq n$ is equivalent to $\operatorname{prof}^*_x(\sh{F})\geq n$ for every $x\in Z$.
If $Z=X$, one writes $\operatorname{prof}^*(\sh{F})$ instead of $\operatorname{prof}^*_X(\sh{F})$.

\begin{remarks}[10.8.2]
\label{IV.10.8.2}
\oldpage[IV-3]{111}
(i)
At every closed point $x\in X$, the rectified depth is equal to the depth.

(ii)
Let $Y$ be a closed sub-prescheme of $X$, $j:Y\to X$ the canonical injection, $\sh{G}$ a coherent $\sh{O}_Y$-Module.
We know \sref{IV.5.7.3}[vi)] that $\operatorname{prof}(\sh{G}_x)=\operatorname{prof}(j_*(\sh{G})_x)$ for every $x\in Y$; we deduce that $\operatorname{prof}^*_x(\sh{G})=\operatorname{prof}^*_{j_*(x)}(j_*(\sh{G}))$ for every $x\in Y$.

(iii)
The notion of rectified depth is only of interest when it is of \emph{local} character, that is to say when it does not change upon replacing $X$ by an arbitrary open neighbourhood $U$ of $x$.
This requires evidently that $x$ not be isolated in $\overline{\{x\}}$, hence $x$ is not a closed point and hence $X$ must be a \emph{Jacobson prescheme} \sref{IV.10.4.5.1}; most often, it will also be necessary to know that $\dim(U)=\dim(X)$ for every dense open $U$ in $X$, and one will therefore need to suppose that $X$ also satisfies conditions $2\textsuperscript{o}$ and $3\textsuperscript{o}$ of \sref{IV.10.6.1}.
\end{remarks}

\begin{lemma}[10.8.3]
\label{IV.10.8.3}
Let $X$ be a regular and biequidimensional prescheme, $\sh{F}$ a coherent $\sh{O}_X$-Module.
Then, for every $x\in X$,
\[
\label{IV.10.8.3.1}\tag{10.8.3.1}
  \operatorname{prof}^*_x(\sh{F})=\dim(X)-\operatorname{dim.proj}(\sh{F}_x).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Indeed, as $X$ is biequidimensional, we have $(\mathbf{0}$, 14.3.5.1)
\[
  \dim(\overline{\{x\}})=\dim(X)-\codim(\overline{\{x\}},X)=\dim(X)-\dim(\sh{O}_{X,x})
\]
by virtue of \sref{IV.5.1.2}.
On the other hand, as $X$ is regular, we have by $(\mathbf{0}$, 17.3.4)
\[
  \operatorname{prof}(\sh{F}_x)=\dim(\sh{O}_{X,x})-\operatorname{dim.proj}(\sh{F}_x)
\]
whence the lemma.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[10.8.4]
\label{IV.10.8.4}
Under the hypotheses of \sref{IV.10.8.3}, the function $x\mapsto\operatorname{prof}^*_x(\sh{F})$ is lower semi-continuous.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
This follows from \sref{IV.10.8.3.1}, since $x\mapsto\operatorname{dim.proj}(\sh{F}_x)$ is upper semi-continuous \sref{IV.6.11.1}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[10.8.5]
\label{IV.10.8.5}
Let $S$ be a locally Noetherian prescheme, $X$ a prescheme locally of finite type over $S$, $\sh{F}$ a coherent $\sh{O}_X$-Module.
Suppose that $S$ satisfies the following conditions: $1\textsuperscript{o}$~$S$ is a Jacobson prescheme; $2\textsuperscript{o}$~$S$ is regular; $3\textsuperscript{o}$~the irreducible components of $S$ are equicodimensional.
Then the function $x\mapsto\operatorname{prof}^*_x(\sh{F})$ is lower semi-continuous in $X$; in other words, for every integer $n$, the set $U_n$ of $x\in X$ such that $\operatorname{prof}^*_x(\sh{F})\geq n$ is open.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\oldpage[IV-3]{112}
As the local rings $\sh{O}_{S,s}$ of $S$ are regular, they are universally catenary $(\mathbf{0}$, 16.5.12), hence $S$ satisfies conditions $1\textsuperscript{o}$, $2\textsuperscript{o}$, and $3\textsuperscript{o}$ of \sref{IV.10.6.1}, and $X$ does too \sref{IV.10.6.1}[i)].
The notion of rectified depth being of local character \sref{IV.10.8.2}[iii)], we can restrict to the case where $S=\Spec(A)$ and $X=\Spec(B)$ are affine, $A$ being a regular ring and $B$ an $A$-algebra of finite type, hence a quotient of a polynomial ring $C=A[T_1,\ldots,T_n]$, and the latter is regular $(\mathbf{0}$, 17.3.7).
One can therefore suppose that $X$ is a closed sub-prescheme of a regular prescheme $Y$ also satisfying conditions $1\textsuperscript{o}$, $2\textsuperscript{o}$, and $3\textsuperscript{o}$ of \sref{IV.10.6.1}; taking into account the remark \sref{IV.10.8.2}[ii)], we are thus reduced to the case where $X$ is moreover regular and Noetherian.
But then, as the local rings of $X$ are integral, the irreducible components of $X$ are also irreducible.
Then, as the local rings of $X$ are catenary $(\mathbf{0}$, 16.5.12), l'hypothesis $3\textsuperscript{o}$ of \sref{IV.10.6.1} implies that $X$ is biequidimensional (\sref{IV.5.1.5} and $(\mathbf{0}$, 14.3.3)); it therefore suffices to apply \sref{IV.10.8.4}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[10.8.6]
\label{IV.10.8.6}
Under the hypotheses of \sref{IV.10.8.5}, for every $x\in X$, the number $\operatorname{prof}^*_x(\sh{F})$ is the unique integer $n$ having the following property: there exists an open neighbourhood $U$ of $x$ in $X$ such that for every point $x'\in U\cap\overline{\{x\}}$, \emph{closed} in $U$, we have $\operatorname{prof}(\sh{F}_{x'})=n$.
In particular, for $\operatorname{prof}^*_x(\sh{F})\geq m$ (resp. $\operatorname{prof}^*_x(\sh{F})\leq m$), it is necessary and sufficient that there exist an open neighbourhood $V$ of $x$ such that, for every point $x'\in V\cap\overline{\{x\}}$, \emph{closed} in $V$, we have $\operatorname{prof}(\sh{F}_{x'})\geq m$ (resp. $\operatorname{prof}(\sh{F}_{x'})\leq m$).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Indeed, one can restrict to the case where $x$ is not a closed point; if $\operatorname{prof}^*_y(\sh{F})\leq n$, the set of $y\in X$ such that $\operatorname{prof}^*_y(\sh{F})\leq n$ is closed by virtue of \sref{IV.10.8.5}, hence contains $\overline{\{x\}}$, and by virtue of the lower semi-continuity of $y\mapsto\operatorname{prof}^*_y(\sh{F})$, there exists an open neighbourhood $U$ of $x$ such that $\operatorname{prof}(\sh{F}_{x'})=n$ for every $y\in U\cap\overline{\{x\}}$, hence $\operatorname{prof}(\sh{F}_{x'})=n$ if $x'\in U\cap\overline{\{x\}}$ is \emph{closed} in $U$ (since the rectified depth is local).

For preschemes satisfying the hypotheses of \sref{IV.10.8.5}, the notion of rectified depth can therefore be defined with the aid of the depth at the \emph{closed points} only (these forming a set that is very dense in every closed part of $X$).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[10.8.7]
\label{IV.10.8.7}
Let $S$ be a locally Noetherian prescheme satisfying conditions $1\textsuperscript{o}$, $2\textsuperscript{o}$, and $3\textsuperscript{o}$ of \sref{IV.10.6.1}.
Let $X$ be a prescheme locally of finite type over $S$, $\sh{F}$ a coherent $\sh{O}_X$-Module, $Y=\Supp(\sh{F})$.
Then, for every $x\in Y$, we have
\[
\label{IV.10.8.7.1}\tag{10.8.7.1}
  \operatorname{prof}^*_x(\sh{F})=\dim_x(Y)-\operatorname{coprof}(\sh{F}_x).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Indeed, by definition, $\operatorname{coprof}(\sh{F}_x)=\dim(\sh{F}_x)-\operatorname{prof}(\sh{F}_x)$, and it follows from \sref{IV.10.6.4} that $\dim_x(Y)=\dim(\overline{\{x\}})+\dim(\sh{F}_x)$; whence \sref{IV.10.8.7.1} by the definition of $\operatorname{prof}^*_x(\sh{F})$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[10.8.8]
\label{IV.10.8.8}
Under the hypotheses on $f$ and $\sh{F}$ of \sref{IV.9.9.1}, the function $x\mapsto\operatorname{prof}^*_x(\sh{F}_{f(x)})$ is constructible.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Note that for every $s\in S$, the fibre $X_s$, being a prescheme locally of finite type over $k(s)$, is a Jacobson prescheme \sref{IV.10.4.7}; as moreover $\Spec(k(s))$ satisfies the conditions of \sref{IV.10.6.1}, we have $\operatorname{prof}^*_x(\sh{F}_{f(x)})=\dim_x(\Supp(\sh{F}_{f(x)}))-\operatorname{coprof}((\sh{F}_{f(x)})_x)$ \sref{IV.10.8.7}.
Now, if $Z=\Supp(\sh{F})$, we have $Z_{f(x)}=\Supp(\sh{F}_{f(x)})$ \sref[I]{I.9.1.13} and $Z$ is locally constructible \sref{IV.8.9.1}; hence the functions $x\mapsto\dim_x(\Supp(\sh{F}_{f(x)}))$ and $x\mapsto\operatorname{coprof}((\sh{F}_{f(x)})_x)$ are locally constructible (\sref{IV.9.9.1} and \sref{IV.9.9.3}), which proves the proposition.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Maximal spectra and ultrapreschemes}
\label{subsection:IV.10.9}
\label{IV.10.9}

\begin{env}[10.9.1]
\label{IV.10.9.1}
Let $X$ be a Jacobson prescheme, and let $S(X)$ be the \emph{ringed space} whose underlying space is the subspace of closed points of $X$, and whose sheaf of rings is the sheaf induced on this subspace by $\sh{O}_X$, in other words the sheaf $\psi^*(\sh{O}_X)$, with $\psi:S(X)\to X$ denoting the canonical injection.
As $\psi$ is a quasi-homeomorphism, one has seen in \sref{IV.10.2.8}[ii)] that if $\theta:\sh{O}_X\to\psi_*(\sh{O}_{S(X)})$ is the homomorphism of sheaves of rings such that $\theta^\#$ is the identity, then $j_X=(\psi,\theta)$ is a \emph{quasi-isomorphism} of ringed spaces, and $\sh{F}\mapsto j_X^*(\sh{F})$ is an equivalence of the category of $\sh{O}_X$-Modules locally free (resp. coherent) and that of $\sh{O}_{S(X)}$-Modules locally free (resp. coherent); moreover, if $\sh{L}$ is an $\sh{O}_X$-Module locally free and if $U$ is an open set of $X$, such that $\sh{L}|U$ is isomorphic to $(\sh{O}_X|U)^n$, $j_X^*(\sh{L})|(U\cap S(X))$ is isomorphic to $(\sh{O}_{S(X)}|(U\cap S(X)))^n$.
\end{env}

\begin{env}[10.9.2]
\label{IV.10.9.2}
Let $X$, $Y$ be two Jacobson preschemes and $f=(\rho,\lambda):X\to Y$ a morphism \emph{locally of finite type}.
We have seen in \sref{IV.10.4.7} that $\rho(S(X))\subset S(Y)$ and that $\rho$ restricted to $S(X)$ gives a continuous map $S(X)\to S(Y)$.
On the other hand, for every open set $V$ of $Y$, one defines by composition a homomorphism of rings
\[
  \Gamma(V\cap S(Y),\sh{O}_{S(Y)})\to\Gamma(f^{-1}(V),\sh{O}_X)\to\Gamma(f^{-1}(V)\cap S(X),\sh{O}_{S(X)})
\]
where the two extreme isomorphisms have been defined in \sref{IV.10.9.1}; it is clear that this defines a homomorphism of sheaves of rings $S(\lambda):\sh{O}_{S(Y)}\to\rho_*(\sh{O}_{S(X)})$ (recalling that the open sets of $S(Y)$ correspond bijectively to those of $Y$ (resp. $S(X)$) \sref{IV.10.2.1}); we thus obtain a morphism of ringed spaces $S(f)=(S(\rho),S(\lambda)):(S(X),\sh{O}_{S(X)})\to(S(Y),\sh{O}_{S(Y)})$ such that the diagram
\[
\xymatrix{
  S(X)\ar[r]^{S(f)}\ar[d]_{j_X} & S(Y)\ar[d]^{j_Y}\\
  X\ar[r]^{f} & Y
}
\]
is commutative; moreover, if $Z$ is a third Jacobson prescheme and $g:Y\to Z$ a morphism locally of finite type, it is clear that $S(g\circ f)=S(g)\circ S(f)$.
We have thus defined a \emph{covariant functor} $S:C\to C'$, where $C'$ is the category of \emph{ringed spaces with local rings} and $C$ the category whose objects are the \emph{Jacobson preschemes} and whose morphisms are the morphisms \emph{locally of finite type} between Jacobson preschemes.
\end{env}

\begin{env}[10.9.3]
\label{IV.10.9.3}
\oldpage[IV-3]{113}
Let us determine the sub-category $C''$ of $C'$ consisting of the ringed spaces isomorphic to $S(X)$ and whose morphisms come from $S(f)$.
Suppose first that $X=\Spec(A)$, where $A$ is a Jacobson ring; then $S(X)$ is the set of \emph{maximal ideals} of $A$, equipped with: $1\textsuperscript{o}$~the topology induced by that of $X$, so that a base of this topology is formed by the $\mathrm{D}^m(h)=\mathrm{D}(h)\cap S(X)$, the set of maximal ideals $\mathfrak{m}$ such that $h\notin\mathfrak{m}$, where $h$ runs through $A$; $2\textsuperscript{o}$~the sheaf of rings $\sh{O}_{S(X)}$ such that $\Gamma(\mathrm{D}^m(h),\sh{O}_{S(X)})=A_f$.
We call this ringed space the \emph{maximal spectrum} of $A$ and denote it $\Spm(A)$.

One notes that if $j:\mathrm{D}(h)\to X$ is the canonical injection, the ringed space induced by $S(X)$ on $S(\mathrm{D}(h))$ is $S(\mathrm{D}(h))$.

Let $B$ be a second Jacobson ring, $Y=\Spec(B)$, $\varphi:B\to A$ a homomorphism of rings making $A$ a $B$-algebra of finite type, $f=({}^a\!\varphi,\widetilde{\varphi}):X\to Y$ the corresponding morphism of preschemes, and $S(f):\Spm(A)\to\Spm(B)$ the morphism of ringed spaces corresponding to $f$.
It is clear that $S(f)=(\psi,\theta)$ is a morphism of \emph{ringed spaces with local rings}, that is $(\mathrm{Err_{II}}$, 1.8.2) for every $x\in\Spm(A)$, $\theta_x^\#$ is a local homomorphism.
Reciprocally:
\end{env}

\begin{proposition}[10.9.4]
\label{IV.10.9.4}
Let $A$, $B$ be two Jacobson rings.
If $u=(\psi,\theta):\Spm(A)\to\Spm(B)$ is a morphism of ringed spaces with local rings such that $\Gamma(\theta):B\to A$ makes $A$ a $B$-algebra of finite type, there exists one and only one morphism of preschemes $f:\Spec(A)\to\Spec(B)$ such that $u=S(f)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The uniqueness of $f$ is evident, since if $f=({}^a\!\varphi,\widetilde{\varphi})$, one must have $\varphi=\Gamma(\theta)$; it remains to see that $S(f)$ is defined and equal to $u=S(f)$.
Now, the first assertion follows from the fact that $\varphi$ is supposed to make $A$ a $B$-algebra of finite type, and the fact that $\theta_x^\#$ is a local homomorphism for every $x\in\Spm(A)$ allows us to repeat the reasoning of \sref[I]{I.1.7.3} restricting to the maximal points of $\Spm(A)$: one shows thus successively that $\psi(x)={}^a\!\varphi(x)$ for every $x\in\Spm(A)$, then that $\theta_x^\#=\varphi_x:B_{\psi(x)}\to A_x$, which completes the proof that $u=S(f)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{env}[10.9.5]
\label{IV.10.9.5}
\oldpage[IV-3]{114}
Let us now consider a ringed space $(X,\sh{O}_X)$; we will say that an open part $U$ of $X$ is \emph{ultra-affine} if the ringed space $(U,\sh{O}_X|U)$ is isomorphic to a maximal spectrum $\Spm(A)$, where $A$ is a Jacobson ring.
We will say that $X$ is an \emph{ultra-prescheme} if every point of $X$ admits an ultra-affine open neighbourhood.
One shows, as in \sref[I]{I.2.1.3} and \sref[I]{I.2.1.4}, that the ultra-affine open sets form a \emph{base} of the topology of $X$ and that $X$ is a Kolmogoroff space.
If $Y$ is a second ultra-prescheme, we will say that a morphism of ringed spaces $f:X\to Y$ is a \emph{morphism of ultra-preschemes} if it satisfies the following conditions: $1\textsuperscript{o}$~$f$ is a morphism of \emph{ringed spaces with local rings}; $2\textsuperscript{o}$~for every $x\in X$, there is an ultra-affine open neighbourhood $V$ of $f(x)$ in $Y$ and an ultra-affine open neighbourhood $U$ of $x$ in $X$ such that $f(U)\subset V$ and the homomorphism of rings $\Gamma(V,\sh{O}_Y)\to\Gamma(U,\sh{O}_X)$ corresponding to $f$ makes $\Gamma(U,\sh{O}_X)$ a $\Gamma(V,\sh{O}_Y)$-algebra of finite type.

It is immediate that one thus composes morphisms well, the composite of two morphisms of ultra-preschemes being an ultra-prescheme morphism thanks to the final remark of \sref{IV.10.9.3}.
It is clear that the category $C_0''$ thus defined is a sub-category of $C'$ that contains $C''$; we now propose to show that $C''=C_0''$, in other words:
\end{env}

\begin{proposition}[10.9.6]
\label{IV.10.9.6}
The functor $X\mapsto S(X)$ from $C$ to $C_0''$ is \emph{fully faithful}, that is for $X$, $Y$ in $C$, the canonical map
\[
  \Hom_C(X,Y)\to\Hom_{C_0''}(S(X),S(Y))
\]
is bijective.
It is an equivalence of categories.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let us first show that it is \emph{injective}: let $f$, $g$ be two morphisms locally of finite type from $X$ to $Y$ and suppose that $S(f)=S(g)$.
This implies first that for every open set $V$ of $Y$, $f^{-1}(V)\cap S(X)=g^{-1}(V)\cap S(X)$, hence \sref{IV.10.3.1} and \sref{IV.10.2.7} $f^{-1}(V)=g^{-1}(V)$; it therefore suffices to prove that for every affine open $V$ of $Y$, $f$ and $g$ coincide on $f^{-1}(V)=g^{-1}(V)$, which reduces to the case where $X$ and $Y$ are affine.
It suffices \sref[I]{I.2.2.4} to prove that the two homomorphisms of rings $B\to\Gamma(X,\sh{O}_X)$ corresponding to $f$ and $g$ are the same; now, for every $s\in B$, the images of $s$ by these homomorphisms are two sections of $\sh{O}_X$ over $X$ which, by hypothesis, induce the \emph{same} section over $S(X)$; we say therefore \sref{IV.10.2.8} that these sections are identical, whence our assertion.

Let us show in the second place that every morphism $h:S(X)\to S(Y)$ (in the category $C_0''$) is of the form $S(f)$, where $f:X\to Y$ is a morphism locally of finite type.
By hypothesis, there is an ultra-affine open covering $(U')$ of $S(X)$ (resp. $(V'_\mu)$) and for each $\lambda$, it corresponds under $h$ to an ultra-affine open of $S(Y)$.
By \sref{IV.10.9.4} and the remark following, we can determine the unique morphism $f:X\to Y$ and conclude that $h=S(f)$.

$2\textsuperscript{o}$~It remains to prove that every ultra-prescheme $X'$ is of the form $S(X)$ for some Jacobson prescheme $X$ (which will necessarily be unique up to isomorphism, by $1\textsuperscript{o}$).
There are ultra-affine open sets covering $X'$, each isomorphic to $S(U_\alpha)$ by open sets of $X'$, $U_\alpha$ being the spectrum of a Jacobson ring; for every pair of indices $\alpha$, $\beta$, consider the unique open $U_{\alpha\beta}$ of $U_\alpha$ whose trace on $S(U_\alpha)$ is $S(U_\alpha)\cap S(U_\beta)$; by virtue of $1\textsuperscript{o}$, the identity of $U_\alpha^\beta\cap U_\beta^\alpha$ is of the form $S(\theta_{\alpha\beta})$, where $\theta_{\alpha\beta}:U_{\alpha\beta}\to U_{\beta\alpha}$ is an isomorphism of preschemes.
One checks immediately (by virtue of $1\textsuperscript{o}$) that the family $(\theta_{\alpha\beta})$ satisfies the recollement condition $(\mathbf{0_I}$, 4.1.7), and that this family defines a prescheme $X$, such that the $U_\alpha$ are identified with affine open sets of $X$; it is clear that $X'=S(X)$, which completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Serre's algebraic spaces}
\label{subsection:IV.10.10}
\label{IV.10.10}

\begin{env}[10.10.1]
\label{IV.10.10.1}
\oldpage[IV-3]{115}
The language introduced by Serre in (FAC) is sometimes convenient, in particular in the questions where the principal interest is attached to the rational points over a base field $k$ (algebraically closed by hypothesis) of the ``algebraic varieties'' over $k$ that one considers.
We will briefly outline here this language, to allow the reader to translate the results of Serre into the language of schemes by connecting them to the preceding considerations.
It is moreover possible to develop the language of Serre equally for preschemes over a non-algebraically-closed field (and even over an Artinian ring); but this introduces considerable technical complications, and moreover, over an arbitrary base field, the advantages (mainly psychological) of the Serre viewpoint disappear; thus we will confine ourselves to the framework fixed by Serre.
The present subsection, like the preceding one, will not be used in the rest of this Treatise, and we will therefore limit ourselves to brief indications.
\end{env}

\begin{env}[10.10.2]
\label{IV.10.10.2}
Given a fixed ring $R$, one can naturally (as in every category) define the notion of $R$-ultra-prescheme.
Consider in particular an \emph{algebraically closed} field $k$; $\Spm(k)$ is then identical to $\Spec(k)$; we say that an $\Spm(k)$-ultra-prescheme $X'$ is a \emph{prealgebraic space} over $k$: it is therefore a $k$-ringed space with local rings whose every point has an open neighbourhood isomorphic to the maximal spectrum of a $k$-algebra of finite type; it amounts to the same to say (by \sref{IV.10.9.6}) that $X'=S(X)$, where $X$ is a $k$-prescheme locally of finite type.
If $X$, $Y$, $Z$ are three $k$-preschemes locally of finite type over $k$, $X\times_k Y$ is also a $k$-prescheme locally of finite type \sref[I]{I.1.3.4}, hence by virtue of \sref{IV.10.9.6}, the notion of product exists in the category of prealgebraic spaces over $k$ (the ``fibre product'' $X'\times_{k'}Y'$, where $X'$, $Y'$, $Z'$ are three prealgebraic spaces over $k$).
One can also define the diagonal morphism $\Delta_{X'}:X'\to X'\times_{k'}X'$ (which is none other than $S(\Delta_X)$); we say that $X'$ is an \emph{algebraic space} over $k$ if $X$ is a scheme, and this amounts to the same as saying that the image of $\Delta_{X'}$ is a \emph{closed part} of $X'\times_{k'}X'$.
\end{env}

\begin{env}[10.10.3]
\label{IV.10.10.3}
The simplifications which follow from the hypothesis that $k$ is algebraically closed stem from the fact that, for a $k$-prescheme $X$ locally of finite type, there is a bijective correspondence between \emph{closed points} of $X$, \emph{points of $X$ with values in $k$} \sref[I]{I.3.4.4}, and \emph{rational points of $X$} \sref[I]{I.3.4.5}, in virtue of \sref[I]{I.6.4.2}.
This shows in particular (in virtue of \sref[I]{I.4.3.1}) that for two $k$-prealgebraic spaces $X'$, $Y'$, the underlying set of the product $X'\times_{k'}Y'$ is \emph{identical to the set-theoretic product} $X'\times Y'$ (although of course the topology of the underlying space of $X'\times_{k'}Y'$ is \emph{not} the product topology of the topologies of the underlying spaces of $X'$ and $Y'$; it is in general strictly finer than the latter).

On the other hand, the local rings $\sh{O}_{x'}$ at the points of a prealgebraic space $X'$ over $k$ are the $k$-algebras whose residue field is \emph{isomorphic} to $k$; if $A$ and $B$ are two such local $k$-algebras, every $k$-homomorphism $\varphi:A\to B$ is necessarily \emph{local}: indeed, if an element $x$ of the maximal ideal of $A$ were such that $\varphi(x)$ is invertible, there would exist $\lambda\in k$ non-zero such that $\varphi(x-\lambda\cdot 1)$ belongs to the maximal ideal of $B$, which is absurd since $x-\lambda\cdot 1$ is invertible in $A$.
We conclude immediately that if $X'=S(X)$, $Y'=S(Y)$ are two $k$-prealgebraic spaces, every morphism $X'\to Y'$ of \emph{$k$-ringed spaces} is also a morphism of \emph{ringed spaces with local rings} \sref[I]{I.1.8.2}; moreover, if $A$ and $B$ are two $k$-algebras of finite type, $\varphi$ makes $A$ a $B$-algebra of finite type; hence \emph{every} morphism of $k$-prealgebraic spaces over $k$ is, by virtue of \sref{IV.10.9.6}, of the form $S(f)$, where $f:X\to Y$ is a \emph{morphism of $k$-preschemes}.
\end{env}

\begin{env}[10.10.4]
\label{IV.10.10.4}
We say that a $k$-prealgebraic space $X'=S(X)$ is \emph{reduced} if $X$ is reduced; as the set of $x\in X$ where $X$ is reduced is open $(\mathbf{0_I}$, 5.2.2), its complement contains at least one closed point if it is non-empty \sref{IV.5.1.11}, and it amounts to the same to say that $X'$ is reduced or that each of its local rings $\sh{O}_{X'}$ is reduced.
We have just seen in \sref{IV.10.10.3} that one can define $\sh{O}_{X'}$ as a \emph{subsheaf} of $\sh{A}(X')$ (in (FAC), Serre limits himself in fact to \emph{reduced} prealgebraic spaces).
One notes that if $X'$ and $Y'$ are two reduced $k$-prealgebraic spaces, the same is true of $X'\times_{k'}Y'$: indeed, it suffices to see that if $A$ and $B$ are two reduced $k$-algebras of finite type, the same is true of $A\otimes_k B$; but we have already seen that the radicals of $A$ and $B$ are reduced to~$0$, and as $k$ is algebraically closed, $A$ and $B$ are ``separable'' algebras in the sense of Bourbaki (\emph{Alg.}, chap.~VIII, \textsection\,7, n\textsuperscript{o}\,5, prop.~5); hence $A\otimes_k B$ is without radical (\emph{loc.~cit.}, n\textsuperscript{o}\,6, cor.~3 of th.~3).
However, if $Z'$ is a third prealgebraic space over $k$, the ``fibre product'' $X'\times_{k'}Y'$ of two reduced prealgebraic spaces over $Z'$ is not in general reduced, which implies that the category of these spaces is insufficient for many questions (notably in the theory of algebraic groups).
But, as one has just seen above, one can keep the language of Serre without limiting oneself, as in (FAC), to considering only \emph{quasi-compact} prealgebraic spaces, to the case of reduced prealgebraic spaces.
\end{env}

\begin{env}[10.10.5]
\label{IV.10.10.5}
\oldpage[IV-3]{116}
Finally, one can also consider ultra-preschemes over an \emph{arbitrary} field $k$ while preserving a language close to that of Serre, and by introducing, as Weil did, an algebraically closed extension $K$ of $k$ (chosen large enough, for example of transcendence degree infinite over $k$, to have sufficiently many ``generic points'' in the sense of Weil).
For every prescheme $X$ locally of finite type over $k$, one associates the set $S_K(X)=S(X\otimes_k K)$ of points of $X$ with values in $K$; one has a canonical map $j:S_K(X)\to X$ which one shows to be a quasi-homeomorphism when one equips $S_K(X)$ with the topology that is the inverse image of that of $X$ by $j$; one equips $S_K(X)$ with the sheaf of $k$-algebras $j^*(\sh{O}_X)$, and one thus obtains a sub-category of the category of $(k,K)$-prealgebraic spaces.
One can show that one can again define products and generalize the results of \sref{IV.10.10.3} and \sref{IV.10.10.4} ($\sh{A}(X')$ being replaced here by the sheaf $\sh{A}_K(X')$ of germs of maps from $X'$ \emph{into} $K$).
However, this point of view makes an algebraically closed sub-field of $k$ play an artificial role, and we note it only to set it aside.
\end{env}
