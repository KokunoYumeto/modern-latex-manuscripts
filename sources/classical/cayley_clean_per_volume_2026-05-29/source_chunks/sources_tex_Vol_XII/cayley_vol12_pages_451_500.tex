\section*{860. On Briot and Bouquet's Theory of the Differential Equation $F(u, \frac{du}{dz}) = 0$}

\begin{center}
[From the \textit{Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society}, vol.~\textsc{xviii}. (1887), pp.~314--324.]
\end{center}

I consider the theory of a differential equation of the first order as developed in Briot and Bouquet's \textit{Th\'eorie des fonctions doublement p\'eriodiques et en particulier des fonctions elliptiques} (\S, 1\textsuperscript{st}~ed., Paris, 1859), but I make some substantial variations in the mode of treatment.

I remark that, writing $u = x$, $\dfrac{du}{dz} = y$, I make the theory to depend altogether upon that of the curve $F(x, y) = 0$; viz. the form of my result is, in order that a differential equation of the first order $F\!\left(u, \dfrac{du}{dz}\right) = 0$ may have a monotropic integral, the curve $F(x, y) = 0$ must satisfy certain conditions.

Briot and Bouquet give their result (Theorem IV., p.~296) under a somewhat different form, as follows; viz. changing only the word ``monodrome'' into ``monotrope,'' their theorem is:

``Pour qu'une \'equation diff\'erentielle du premier ordre de la forme
\[
\left(\frac{du}{dz}\right)^m + f_1(u)\left(\frac{du}{dz}\right)^{m-1} + \cdots + f_m(u) = 0
\]
admette une int\'egrale monotrope:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item les coefficients $f_1(u), f_2(u), \ldots, f_m(u)$ doivent \^etre des polyn\^omes entiers en $u$ et, au plus, le premier du second degr\'e, le second du quatri\`eme degr\'e, \ldots, le dernier du degr\'e $2m$;
\item quand pour une certaine valeur de $u$ l'\'equation a une racine multiple diff\'erente de z\'ero, $\dfrac{du}{dz}$ doit rester monotrope par rapport \`a $u$;
\item quand pour une certaine valeur $u_1$ de $u$, l'\'equation a une racine multiple \'egale \`a z\'ero, le premier terme du d\'eveloppement de $\dfrac{du}{dz}$ suivant les puissances croissantes de $(u - u_1)^{1/n}$ doit avoir l'exposant $1$, si cet exposant est plus petit que l'unit\'e;
\item enfin l'\'equation diff\'erentielle, que l'on d\'eduit de la premi\`ere en posant $w = \dfrac{1}{v}$, doit offrir pour $v = 0$ les m\^emes caract\`eres.
\end{enumerate}
Ces conditions sont suffisantes.''

I notice that this may be regarded as a statement referring to the curve
\[
F(x, y) = y^m + f_1(x)y^{m-1} + \cdots + f_m(x) = 0,
\]
but that in 2 a notion of monotropy is assumed, for $y$ must be a monotropic function of $x$; and that 4 in effect introduces the new curve $F\!\left(\dfrac{1}{x}, \dfrac{y}{x^2}\right) = 0$.

\subsection*{1.}

We have between the variables $u$ and $z$ a differential equation of the first order $F\!\left(u, \dfrac{du}{dz}\right) = 0$, $F$ a rational and integral function of $u$ and $\dfrac{du}{dz}$. The equation determines $u$ as a function of $z$, and we wish to know whether $u$ is a monotropic function of $z$. It will not be so if we have a \textit{tropical point}, viz. a point $z = c$, such that, in the neighbourhood thereof, the value of $u$ is given by a series $u - a = B(z - c)^\rho + \ldots$, in ascending powers of $z - c$, involving fractional powers of $z - c$. Conversely, if there is no tropical point, then $u$ will be a monotropic function of $z$; but we must consider and exclude not only tropical points at a finite distance, but also the point at infinity, viz. there must not be any development $u = Bz^\rho + \ldots$, in descending powers, involving fractional powers of $z$.

\subsection*{2.}

A one-valued function (\textit{eindeutige Function}, or \textit{fonction uniforme} or \textit{bien d\'etermin\'ee}) is monotropic; since there is only one value, the function must, after a circuit described by the point $z$, recover its original value. But, conversely, a monotropic function is one-valued, that is, no two or more valued function can be monotropic. For, suppose a two-valued function of $z$, having the values $Z_1$ and $Z_2$, is monotropic; there is by hypothesis no tropical point, that is, no point $z = c$, such that the function $Z_1$, after a circuit described by the point $z$, instead of recovering its original value, assumes the value $Z_2$; and the like as to $Z_2$. But, this being so, it is meaningless to regard $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ as two values of the same function; they must be considered as two distinct and separate functions, each of them a one-valued monotropic function. And it thus appears that the two notions, monotropic and one-valued, are in fact equivalent.

\subsection*{3.}

In what precedes, $u$ and $z$ are regarded as complex variables geometrically represented by means of two real points in their own planes respectively. But we now write $\dfrac{du}{dz} = y$, $u = x$, whence $F(x, y) = 0$, regarding $x$, $y$ as the coordinates, real or imaginary, of a point of the curve represented by this equation. And this curve has, moreover, to be considered from a non-projective point of view; we have to distinguish between, and separately consider, finite points ($x$ and $y$ each of them finite), and the points at infinity ($x$ and $y$ either or each of them infinite). And it is moreover necessary to distinguish between, and separately consider, points on the axis of $x$ (or say axial points), and points off the axis of $x$ (say non-axial points). Taking, for convenience, $a$ to denote a finite value which may be $= 0$, $b$ a finite value which is not $= 0$ (or say, $a$ not $= \infty$, $b$ not $= \infty$ or $0$), we have thus the six cases,
\[
1\quad (a, b); \qquad 2\quad (a, 0); \qquad 3\quad (a, \infty); \qquad 4\quad (\infty, b); \qquad 5\quad (\infty, 0); \qquad 6\quad (\infty, \infty).
\]

I regard also the axes of $x$ and $y$ as horizontal and vertical respectively, and speak of the tangent or element of the curve as horizontal, vertical, or inclined, accordingly.

\subsection*{4.}

In the neighbourhood of any given point of the curve, we have $y =$ a series of powers of $x$, the form of the series being different for different classes of points. I write $\Pf(x)$ to denote a series (finite or infinite) $A + Bx + Cx^2 + \ldots$, in ascending powers of $x$; it is to be throughout understood that the leading coefficient $A$ is not $= 0$. Of course, $\Pf(x-a)$, $\Pf\{(x-a)^{1/n}\}$, $\Pf(x^{-1})$, \&c., will denote the like series $A + B(x - a) + \ldots$, $A + B(x - a)^{1/n} + \ldots$, $A + Bx^{-1} + \ldots$, \&c. The coefficients after the leading coefficient $A$ may any or all of them vanish; thus $\Pf\{(x - a)^{1/n}\}$ extends to denote the series $\Pf(x - a)$ of integer powers, but naturally the former notation is not used unless the series contains fractional powers.

We can, by means of the symbol $\Pf$, express for any given point of the curve the form of the expansion in the neighbourhood thereof; and, attaching to the point the expansion which belongs to it, we may, for instance, speak of a point $y = \Pf(x - a)$; viz. if $b$ is the constant term of the series, then this is a non-axial point $(a, b)$, which is an ordinary (non-singular) point of the curve, and for which the element is not vertical; a like point with the element vertical would be a point $y = b + (x - a)^2 \Pf(x - a)$.

Observe that, in the case of a multiple point, there is a separate expansion for each branch through the point, and in thus attaching an expansion to the point we regard the point as belonging to one of these branches; in dealing with a multiple point, it is necessary to consider separately all the different expansions, that is, all the branches through the point.

It is clear that for a point $(a, b)$, $(a, 0)$, or $(a, \infty)$ the expression for $y$ depends on $\Pf(x - a)$, or $\Pf\{(x - a)^{1/n}\}$; while, for a point $(\infty, b)$, $(\infty, 0)$, or $(\infty, \infty)$, it depends on $\Pf(x^{-1})$, or $\Pf(x^{1/n})$; viz. in the two cases respectively, we have a series in ascending powers of $x - a$, or in descending powers of $x$.

\subsection*{5.}

In regard to any given point of the curve, substituting for $y$, $x$ their values $\dfrac{du}{dz}$, $u$, and thence forming the reciprocal of $\dfrac{du}{dz}$, we obtain $\dfrac{dz}{du} =$ a series in $u$; viz. this is a series in ascending powers of $(u - a)$, or in descending powers of $u$. Integrating, we have $z - c =$ a series in $u$, which series may or may not contain a logarithmic term $\log u$ or $\log(u - a)$; and, reverting the series, we obtain $u =$ a series in $(z - c)$. This result, applicable to the neighbourhood of the point $u = a$, may contain only integer powers of $z-c$, and be accordingly of the form $u =$ a one-valued series in $z$; and we then say that the point on the curve is a ``permissive'' point. Or it may contain fractional powers of $z-c$, and be accordingly of the form $u =$ a more-than-one-valued series in $z$; and we then say that the point on the curve is a ``prohibitive'' point.

The necessary and sufficient condition in order that $u$ may be a monotropic function of $z$ is: the points of the curve must be all of them permissive points; or, what is the same thing, there must not be any prohibitive point.

\subsection*{6.}

I form a complete table of permissive points, as follows:

$n$ a positive integer $= 2$ at least. $\epsilon = 0$, $1$, or $-1$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|l|}
\hline
 & \textbf{Point} & \textbf{Form} \\
\hline
$(a, b)$ & $y = \Pf(x - a)$ & general \\
$(a, b)$ & $y = b + (x - a)^2 \Pf(x - a)$ & vertical element \\
$(a, 0)$ & $y = (x - a) \Pf(x - a)$ & general \\
$(a, 0)$ & $y = (x - a)^{1 + \frac{1}{n}} \Pf\{(x - a)^{1/n}\}$ & tangent horizontal \\
$(a, \infty)$ & $y = (x - a)^{-1} \Pf(x - a)$ & general \\
$(a, \infty)$ & $y = (x - a)^{-1 + \frac{1}{n}} \Pf\{(x - a)^{1/n}\}$ & element vertical \\
$(\infty, b)$ & $y = \Pf(x^{-1})$ & general \\
$(\infty, b)$ & $y = b + x^{-2} \Pf(x^{-1})$ & element vertical \\
$(\infty, 0)$ & $y = x^{-1} \Pf(x^{-1})$ & general \\
$(\infty, 0)$ & $y = x^{-1 - \frac{1}{n}} \Pf(x^{-1/n})$ & element horizontal \\
$(\infty, \infty)$ & $y = x \Pf(x^{-1})$ & general \\
$(\infty, \infty)$ & $y = x^{1 + \frac{1}{n}} \Pf(x^{-1/n})$ & element vertical \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

viz. it is only a point $(a, b)$, $(a, 0)$, $(\infty, b)$ or $(\infty, \infty)$ which may be permissive; a point $(a, \infty)$ or $(\infty, 0)$ is prohibitive.

\subsection*{7.}

A point $y = \Pf(x - a)$ is permissive. We have $\dfrac{du}{dz} = \Pf(u)$, $\dfrac{dz}{du} = \dfrac{1}{\Pf(u)}$, whence $z - c = (u - a)\Pf(u - a)$, $u - a = (z - c)\Pf(z - c)$, the required result. The several steps will be readily understood; the reciprocal of a series $\Pf(u - a)$ is a series of the like form $\Pf(u - a)$; integrating this in regard to $u$ as a series in $u - a$ (no constant being added on the right-hand side), we obtain a series $(u - a)\Pf(u - a)$; and finally, $z - c$ being equal to this expression, we obtain, by reversion, a like expression $u - a = (z - c)\Pf(z - c)$. We might, for convenience, have written $u$, $z$ in place of $x - a$, $u - a$, $z - c$, respectively. The proof would have run: $y = \Pf(x)$, $\dfrac{du}{dz} = \Pf(u)$, $\dfrac{dz}{du} = \Pf(u)$, $z = u\Pf(u)$, $u = z\Pf(z)$, meaning $u - a = (z - c)\Pf(z - c)$, as above; and, in what follows, this is done accordingly.

It is worth while to make an instance in which the integration can be performed; say we consider the point $(0, 1)$ of the curve $y = \dfrac{1}{1 + x + x^2}$, which point is $y = \dfrac{1}{1 + x + x^2} = \Pf(x)$. We have $\dfrac{du}{dz} = \dfrac{1}{1 + u + u^2}$, $\dfrac{dz}{du} = 1 + 2u$, $z = \log(1 + u + u^2)$, or $u + u^2 = e^z - 1$; giving a series $u = z\Pf(z)$, or the point is a permissive point, as in the general case. We have here $u + u^2 = e^z - 1$, viz. $u$ is not a monotropic function of $z$; but this is by reason of a prohibitive point $(\infty, \infty)$.

\subsection*{8.}

A point $y = (x - a)\Pf(x - a)$ is permissive. Writing as above $u$, $z$ in place of $u - a$, $z - c$, we have $\dfrac{du}{dz} = u\Pf(u)$, $\dfrac{dz}{du} = \dfrac{1}{u}\Pf(u)$, or say $u\dfrac{dz}{du} = \dfrac{1}{u} + \Pf(u)$, whence $mz = \log u + u\Pf(u)$, that is, $ue^{u\Pf(u)} = e^{mz}$, or say $u\Pf(u) = e^{mz}$, whence $u = e^{mz}\Pf(e^{mz})$, a one-valued series.

I take a particular instance, the point $(0, 0)$ for the curve $y = \dfrac{x}{1 - x}$; here $\dfrac{du}{dz} = \dfrac{u}{1 - u}$, $\dfrac{dz}{du} = \dfrac{1}{u} - 1$, $z = \log(u) - u$, or $ue^{-u} = e^z$; the point is permissive. The equation just obtained shows that $u$ is not a monotropic function of $z$; but this is by reason of the prohibitive points $(1, \infty)$.

\subsection*{9.}

A point $y = (x - a)^2 \Pf(x - a)$ is permissive. Here $\dfrac{du}{dz} = u^2 \Pf(u)$, $\dfrac{dz}{du} = \dfrac{1}{u^2}\Pf(u) = \dfrac{A}{u^2} + \dfrac{B}{u} + \Pf(u)$, whence $z = -\dfrac{A}{u} + B\log u + u\Pf(u)$, where the logarithmic term occurs or does not occur, according as $B$ is not or is $= 0$. In the former case, we have $e^{z/B} = u e^{u\Pf(u)}$; in the latter case, $z = \dfrac{A}{u} + u\Pf(u)$; and in each case we can, by reversion, express $u$ as a one-valued series in $z$.

We may take as examples the two curves $y = \dfrac{x^2}{1 - x}$ and $y = \dfrac{x^2}{1 + x}$; in each case the point $(0, 0)$ is a permissive point, but, by reason of the prohibitive points $(1, \infty)$ and $(-1, \infty)$, $u$ is not a monotropic function of $z$.

\subsection*{10.}

As to the other forms of permissive points, the proofs depend upon those for the forms already considered. Thus for $y = (x - a)^{1 + \frac{\epsilon}{n}} \Pf\{(x - a)^{1/n}\}$, or $\dfrac{du}{dz} = u^{1 + \frac{\epsilon}{n}} \Pf(u^{1/n})$, writing $v = u^{1/n}$, we find $\dfrac{dv}{dz} = v^{1 + \epsilon} \Pf(v)$; that is, $z = \Pf(v)$, $v\Pf(v)$, or $v^2\Pf(v)$; in each case $v =$ one-valued series in $z$, and thence $u = v^n =$ one-valued series in $z$.

Similarly for $y = \Pf(x^{-1})$, $y = x\Pf(x^{-1})$, $y = x^2\Pf(x^{-1})$, or say $y = x^{1 + \epsilon}\Pf(x^{-1})$; here $\dfrac{du}{dz} = u^{1 + \epsilon}\Pf(u^{-1})$, or, writing $v = u^{-1}$, we have $\dfrac{dv}{dz} = v^{1 - \epsilon}\Pf(v)$; viz. this is $\dfrac{dv}{dz} = \Pf(v)$, $v\Pf(v)$, and $v^2\Pf(v)$; in each case $v =$ one-valued series in $z$, and thence $u = v^{-1} =$ one-valued series in $z$.

And finally, for $y = x^{1 + \frac{\epsilon}{n}} \Pf(x^{-1/n})$, here $\dfrac{du}{dz} = u^{1 + \frac{\epsilon}{n}} \Pf(u^{-1/n})$, or, writing $v = u^{-\epsilon/n}$, we have $\dfrac{dv}{dz} = v^{1 - \epsilon}\Pf(v)$, that is, $\dfrac{dv}{dz} = \Pf(v)$, $v\Pf(v)$, or $v^2\Pf(v)$, or as before; $v =$ one-valued series in $z$, and thence $u = v^{-n/\epsilon} =$ one-valued series in $z$.

\subsection*{11.}

There is no difficulty in showing that every point of the curve, not being a permissive point as above, is a prohibitive point. Thus for a point $(a, b)$, if the series for $y - b$ contain any fractional power, say, if we have $y = b + (x - a)^{\frac{1}{n}}$, then $\dfrac{du}{dz} = b + u^{\frac{1}{n}}$, $\dfrac{dz}{du} = \dfrac{1}{b + u^{\frac{1}{n}}}$, whence, reverting, $u = bz +$ series containing the fractional power $z^{\frac{1}{n} + 1}$.

Again, a point $y = (x - a)^m \Pf(x - a)$, $m = 3$ or any higher integer value, is prohibitive. Attending only to the leading term, we have $\dfrac{du}{dz} = u^m$, whence $\dfrac{dz}{du} = u^{-m}$, $z = u^{1-m}$, whence, reverting, $u = z^{\frac{1}{1-m}}$, which is a fractional power; and this is also the case if $m$ be a negative integer. And similarly, in other cases, the series for $u$ will always contain fractional powers of $z$.

\subsection*{12.}

In order that $u$ may be a monotropic function of $z$, the points of the curve must be all of them permissive, or, what is the same thing, there must be no prohibitive point; we have to consider the geometrical signification of this condition.

If we attend first to the ordinary (or non-singular) points of the curve, a non-axial point $(a, b)$ must be a point $y = \Pf(x - a)$, viz. there must be no such point with a vertical element. An axial point $(a, 0)$ may be $y = (x - a)\Pf(x - a)$, viz. the element may be inclined; or the point may be $y = (x - a)^2 \Pf(x - a)$, viz. the element may be horizontal (but in this case there must be an ordinary or two-pointic contact only); or the point may be $y = (x - a)^{\frac{1}{n}} \Pf\{(x - a)^{\frac{1}{n}}\}$, viz. the element may be vertical. The point must not be $(a, \infty)$, viz. there must be no asymptote parallel to (or coincident with) the axis of $y$.

The point may be $(\infty, b)$, $y = \Pf(x^{-1})$, that is, there may be an ordinary, or osculating, asymptote parallel to the axis of $x$. But there is no point $(\infty, 0)$, that is, there must be no asymptote coincident with the axis of $x$. There may be points $(\infty, \infty)$, $y = x\Pf(x^{-1})$, that is, ordinary or osculating asymptotes inclined to the axes; and there may also be points $(\infty, \infty)$, $y = x^2\Pf(x^{-1})$, that is, asymptotic parabolas of vertical axis.

\subsection*{13.}

We have, moreover, conditions as to the singular points; thus, every non-axial multiple point $(a, b)$ must be a point with each branch of the form $y = \Pf(x - a)$, that is, each branch must be an ordinary (non-cuspidal) branch, and must be non-vertical. The axial multiple or singular points $(a, 0)$ may have ordinary (non-cuspidal) branches $y = (x - a)\Pf(x - a)$ and $y = (x - a)^2\Pf(x - a)$, inclined or horizontal, or else $y = (x - a)^{\frac{1}{n}} \Pf\{(x - a)^{\frac{1}{n}}\}$, vertical; and there may also be cuspidal branches $y = (x - a)^{\frac{1}{n}} \Pf\{(x - a)^{\frac{1}{n}}\}$, $y = (x - a)^{1 + \frac{1}{n}} \Pf\{(x - a)^{\frac{1}{n}}\}$, and $y = (x - a)^{1 - \frac{1}{n}} \Pf\{(x - a)^{\frac{1}{n}}\}$, inclined, horizontal, or vertical.

There must be no singular points $(a, \infty)$; any such point is, in fact, excluded by the condition, no asymptote parallel to or coincident with the axis of $y$. There may be multiple points $(\infty, b)$, but not $(\infty, 0)$; viz. as above, there may be asymptotes parallel to, but not coincident with, the axis of $x$; but in this case, the several branches must be each of them an ordinary branch $y = \Pf(x^{-1})$. Finally, there may be multiple or singular points $(\infty, \infty)$, but each branch must be either an ordinary branch $y = x\Pf(x^{-1})$ or $y = x^2\Pf(x^{-1})$, or a cuspidal branch $y = x^{1 + \frac{1}{n}} \Pf(x^{-1/n})$, $y = x^{1 - \frac{1}{n}} \Pf(x^{-1/n})$, or $y = x^{1 + \frac{2}{n}} \Pf(x^{-1/n})$, inclined, horizontal, or vertical.

\subsection*{14.}

The enumeration seems long and difficult. But for a given curve we have, in fact, only to see that there are no ordinary non-axial points $(a, b)$ of vertical element, and to further examine the finite singular points $(a, b)$ and $(a, 0)$, and also the several infinite branches as giving ordinary or else singular points $(a, \infty)$, $(\infty, b)$, $(\infty, 0)$, or $(\infty, \infty)$, viz. it has to be seen for each infinite branch that the expansion is of the proper form.

\subsection*{15.}

Writing the equation of the curve in the form
\[
f_0(x)y^m + f_1(x)y^{m-1} + \cdots + f_m(x) = 0,
\]
where $f_0$, $f_1$, \ldots, $f_m$ denote rational and integral functions of $x$, then, if $a$ be a root of the equation $f_0(x) = 0$, there will be on the curve a point $(a, \infty)$, which is prohibitive; $f_0(x)$ is therefore a constant, or, taking it to be $= 1$, the form must be
\[
y^m + f_1(x)y^{m-1} + \cdots + f_m(x) = 0,
\]
where $f_1$, $f_2$, \ldots, $f_m$ denote rational and integral functions of $x$.

It is to be shown that the degrees of these functions are equal respectively to $2$, $4$, \ldots, $2m$ at most. Supposing that the degrees are $2$, $4$, \ldots, $2m$; then for the points at infinity we find an expansion $y = Ax^2 + \ldots$, in descending powers of $x$, by substitution in an equation $y^m + a_1 x^2 y^{m-1} + a_2 x^4 y^{m-2} + \ldots + a_m x^{2m} = 0$; whence $m\rho = 2$, and $A$ is determined by an equation of the order $m$; there are thus $m$ branches $y = x^2 \Pf(x^{-1})$, corresponding to permissive points. If, however, any one of the functions $f$ be of a higher degree, then it may be shown (but the formal proof is not easily given) that there will be at any rate a branch $y = Ax^\rho +$ \&c., where $\rho$ has a value $> 2$, and we have thus a prohibitive point; hence the degrees must be as stated.

\subsection*{16.}

As an example of a differential equation with a monotropic solution, take
\[
\left(\frac{du}{dz}\right)^2 = 1 - 27u^2 + 4u^3;
\]
we have here the curve $y^2 = 1 - 27x^2 + 4x^3$, which is a nodal cubic, as shown in the annexed figure. To put the node in evidence, the equation may be written in the form $(y + 2)^2(y - 1) + 27x^2 = 0$; viz. the node is the point $(0, -2)$, and the equation of the tangents is $(y + 2)^2 - 27x^2 = 0$. The curve besides meets the line $x = 0$ in the point $y = 1$, where the tangent is horizontal, and it meets the axis $y = 0$ in the points $x = \pm\sqrt{\frac{27}{4}}$, at each of which the tangent is vertical; these two points, as lying on the axis $y = 0$, are thus each of them permissive; and they are the only points where the tangent is vertical (in fact, the tangents from the point at infinity on the line $x = 0$ are the two lines $x = \pm\sqrt{\frac{27}{4}}$, and the line infinity counting twice, as a tangent at an inflexion). For the points at infinity, we have $y = -3x^3 - 1 - \tfrac{1}{3}x^{-3} +$ \&c., which is of the form $y = x^3 \Pf\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right)$, included in $y = x^{1 + \frac{1}{n}} \Pf(x^{-1/n})$, and the point is thus permissive; there is no prohibitive point, and the differential equation has a monotropic solution. This is, in fact, the rational solution $u = (z - c) - (z - c)^3$, or say $u = z - z^3$; the curve is thus given by the two equations $x = z - z^3$, $y = 1 - 3z^2$.

\subsection*{17.}

As a second example, take the differential equation
\[
\left(\frac{du}{dz}\right)^2 = (1 - u^2)(1 - k^2 u^2).
\]
We have here the curve $y^2 = (1 - x^2)(1 - k^2 x^2) = 0$, which is a trinodal quartic curve, as shown in the figure. There is a node at the origin with the tangents $y^2 - x^2 = 0$, and, writing the equation in the form $(3y + 1)(3y - 2)^2 - (27x^2 - 8)^2 = 0$, we have for the other two nodes $x = \pm\dfrac{2\sqrt{2}}{3\sqrt{3}}$, $y = \dfrac{2}{3}$. The lowest points are given by $x = 0$, $y = \pm\dfrac{1}{k}$; viz. the line $y = \dfrac{1}{k}$ is a horizontal tangent. Writing $x = 0$, we have $y^2 - \dfrac{1}{k^2} = 0$, that is, $y = \pm\dfrac{1}{k}$, the node at the origin, and $y = \dfrac{1}{k}$, the height of the loop; and, writing $y = 0$, we have $1 - k^2 x^2 = 0$, that is, $x = \pm\dfrac{1}{k}$, the other two intersections with the axis of $x$. The tangents at these two points are vertical; as being on the axis, they are thus permissive points. And they are the only points with a vertical tangent; in fact, the point $(x = 0, y = \infty)$ is a point of the curve, with the line at infinity as an osculating tangent (4-pointic intersection); hence the tangents from the point $x = 0$, $y = \infty$ are the line at infinity counting four times, and the lines $x = \pm\dfrac{1}{k}$ touching at the points $\left(\pm\dfrac{1}{k}, 0\right)$ as above.

For the infinite branches, we have $y = kx^2 + \ldots$, which is of the right form $y = x^{1 + \frac{1}{n}} \Pf\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right)$. We thus see that there is no prohibitive point; and the differential equation has a monotropic solution accordingly. Writing $z$ in place of $z - c$, the solution in fact is $u = \dfrac{e^z + e^{-z}}{e^z - e^{-z}}$, the curve is given by the equations $x = \dfrac{e^z - e^{-z}}{e^z + e^{-z}}$, $y = \dfrac{4}{(e^z + e^{-z})^2}$; hence, putting $\theta = \dfrac{e^z - e^{-z}}{e^z + e^{-z}}$, the curve is given by the two equations $x = \theta - \theta^3$, $y = (1 - 3\theta^2)$\ldots\ the monotropic function $u$ satisfies the differential equation.

\subsection*{18.}

$F\!\left(u, \dfrac{du}{dz}\right) = 0$ must be either a rational function, a singly periodic function, or a doubly periodic function of $z$; say the forms are $u = \Pf(z)$, $u = \Pf(e^z)$, and $u = \Pf\{\sn(gz, k)\} + \cn(gz, k)\dn(gz, k)\, \mathrm{Q}\{\sn(gz, k)\}$, where $\Pf$, $\mathrm{Q}$ denote rational functions. I do not at present consider the criteria (such as are given in Briot and Bouquet's Theorem V., p.~301) for determining by means of the curve which is the form of the integral. I remark, however, that in the first and second cases the curve is unicursal, while in the third case it is bicursal; or say that, according as the deficiency is $= 0$ or $1$, the integral is rational or simply periodic, or else it is doubly periodic. Moreover, the curve being unicursal, we can express the coordinates as equal to rational functions $\Pf(\theta)$, $\mathrm{Q}(\theta)$ of a parameter; and, being bicursal, we can express them as equal to rational functions of $\sn(gz, k)$, $\cn(gz, k)$, $\dn(gz, k)$.

\section*{861. Note on a Formula relating to the Zero-Value of a Theta-Function}

\begin{center}
[From \textit{Crelle's Journal der Mathem.}, t.~c. (1887), pp.~87, 88.]
\end{center}

I had some difficulty in verifying for the case of a single theta-function, a formula given in Herr Thomae's paper ``Beitrag zur Theorie der $\vartheta$-Functionen,'' \textit{Crelle's Journal}, vol.~\textsc{lxxi}. (1870), pp.~201--222. The formula in question (see p.~216) is given as follows:
\[
\vartheta(0, 0, \ldots, 0) = \frac{\sqrt[4]{A}}{(\pi i)^{\frac{1}{2}\rho}} \sqrt{\text{Discr.}(k_1, k_2, \ldots, k_{\rho+1}) \cdot \text{Discr.}(k_1, k_2, \ldots, k_{\rho+1})},
\]
but the denominator factor should I think be $(\pi i)^{\frac{1}{2}\rho}$ instead of $(2\pi i)^{\frac{1}{2}\rho}$. Making this alteration, then in the case of a single theta-function, $\rho = 1$, and the function belongs to the radical
\[
\sqrt{x - k_1 \cdot x - k_2 \cdot x - k_3 \cdot x - k_4},
\]
where $(k_1, k_2, k_3, k_4) = (-\rho, -1, +1, +\frac{1}{\rho})$. The determinant $|A_{\alpha\beta}|$ is a single term $= A$, and the formula becomes
\[
\vartheta(0) = \frac{\sqrt[4]{A}}{\sqrt{\pi i}} \sqrt{(k_3 - k_1)(k_4 - k_2)},
\]
where $k_3 - k_1$, $k_4 - k_2$ are each $= 1 + \rho$, and we have therefore
\[
\vartheta(0) = \frac{\sqrt[4]{A}}{\sqrt{\pi i}} \sqrt{1 + \rho}.
\]
Also $A$ denotes the integral
\[
A = \int_{k_2}^{k_3} \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1 - x^2 \cdot 1 - k^2 x^2}} = ikK',
\]
and the formula thus is
\[
\vartheta(0) = \frac{\sqrt[4]{ikK'}}{\sqrt{\pi}} (1 + \rho)^{\frac{1}{2}}.
\]

But observe that, in the theta-function as defined by the equation $\mathcal{S}(x) = \Sigma e^{ax^2 + 2nx}$, $a$ is used to denote the value $a = \dfrac{iK'}{K} = \dfrac{1}{\pi i}B$, where $A$ is the above-mentioned integral, and $B$ is the integral
\[
B = \int_{k_2}^{k_3} \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x - k_1 \cdot x - k_2 \cdot x - k_3 \cdot x - k_4}},
\]
which value must however be taken negatively, viz. we must write $B = -2kK'$, and we then have
\[
a = \frac{iK'}{K} = \frac{-2kK'}{2\pi i} = \frac{B}{2\pi i},
\]
viz. writing as usual $q = e^{-\frac{\pi K'}{K}} = e^{\frac{i\pi B}{2\pi i}} = e^{\frac{1}{2}B}$, the $e^a$ of the theta-function is not $= q$, but it is $= q^2$; and the zero-value $\mathcal{S}(0)$ is $= 1 + 2q^2 + 2q^8 + 2q^{18} + \ldots$.

The equation thus is
\[
2q^2 = \frac{\sqrt[4]{ikK'}}{\sqrt{\pi}} (1 + \rho)^{\frac{1}{2}},
\]
which is right; in fact, writing $k'$ in place of $k$, and consequently $K'$, $q$ in place of $K$, $r$ respectively, the equation becomes
\[
q^2 = \frac{\sqrt[4]{iKk'}}{\sqrt{\pi}} (1 + \rho)^{\frac{1}{2}},
\]
and changing $q$ into $q^4$, then (\textit{Fund.~Nova}, p.~92) $K$ is changed into $K\left(1 + \dfrac{k'}{1}\right)$ and $z$ we have the formula in question.

As a verification for small values of $q$, observe that we have
\[
\frac{2K}{\pi} = 1 + 4q + 4q^2 + \ldots, \qquad \frac{2kK}{\pi} = 2\sqrt[4]{q}(1 + q + \ldots),
\]
and thence $\vartheta(0) = 1 + 2q^2 + 2q^8 + \ldots = 1 - 4q^2 + \ldots$, or $\sqrt{\dfrac{2kK}{\pi}} = 1 - 4q^2 + \ldots$.

\section*{862. Note on the Theory of Linear Differential Equations}

\begin{center}
[From \textit{Crelle's Journal der Mathem.}, t.~c. (1887), pp.~286--295.]
\end{center}

\subsection*{1.}

I consider a linear differential equation
\[
p_0 \frac{d^m y}{dx^m} + p_1 \frac{d^{m-1} y}{dx^{m-1}} + \cdots + p_m y = 0,
\]
where $p_0$, $p_1$, \ldots, $p_m$ are rational and integral functions of $x$, having no common factor: as usual, $x$ is a complex magnitude represented by a point, and we consider the integrals belonging to a singular point $x = a$ of the differential equation. An integral may be a \textit{regular integral}, or it may be what Thom\'e calls a \textit{normal elementary integral}: the theory of these integrals (which I would rather call \textit{subregular integrals}) requires, I think, further examination.

\subsection*{2.}

I retain $x$ as the independent variable, but for shortness use $t$ to denote $\dfrac{1}{x - a}$: and I take as the dependent variable $z = \dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dx}$: we have then $z$ determined by a non-linear differential equation of the order $m - 1$, and from any value of $z$ we derive a corresponding value $e^{\int z\,dx}$ of $y$.

\subsection*{3.}

To obtain the $z$-equation, using for shortness accents to denote differentiation in regard to $x$, we have $z = \dfrac{y'}{y}$, and thence $z' = \dfrac{y''}{y} - \left(\dfrac{y'}{y}\right)^2$, that is $z' + z^2 = \dfrac{y''}{y}$; hence $\dfrac{y''}{y} = z^2 + z'$, also $2zz' + z'' = \dfrac{y'''}{y} - \dfrac{y'y''}{y^2}$, that is $\dfrac{y'''}{y} = z^3 + 3zz' + z''$, or finally $z^3 + 3zz' + z'' = \dfrac{y'''}{y}$; and so on; viz. the values of $\dfrac{y'}{y}$, $\dfrac{y''}{y}$, $\dfrac{y'''}{y}$, \ldots\ are $z$, $z^2 + z'$, $z^3 + 3zz' + z''$, \ldots; generally for $\dfrac{y^{(n)}}{y}$ the first term is $z^n$ and the last term is $z^{(n-1)}$.

If, to fix the ideas, the $y$-equation is
\[
p_0 y''' + p_1 y'' + p_2 y' + p_3 y = 0,
\]
then, dividing by $y$, the $z$-equation is
\[
p_0(z^3 + 3zz' + z'') + p_1(z^2 + z') + p_2 z + p_3 = 0;
\]
and similarly, from the $y$-equation of the order $m$, we derive a $z$-equation of the order $m - 1$,
\[
p_0[z^m + \ldots + z^{(m-1)}] + \cdots + p_{m-2}(z^2 + z') + p_{m-1}z + p_m = 0.
\]

\subsection*{4.}

In this equation, each coefficient is in the first instance a rational and integral function of $x$, or, what is the same thing, of $x - a$; writing $t$ to denote $\dfrac{1}{x - a}$, each coefficient is thus the sum of a finite number of negative integer powers of $t$; hence multiplying the whole equation by a proper positive power of $t$, the several coefficients become each of them the sum of a finite number of positive powers of $t$, or arranging in descending powers of $t$, say the general form is $p_r = \alpha_r t^{\lambda_r} + \beta_r t^{\lambda_r - 1} + \cdots + \kappa_r$, where $\lambda_r$ is a positive integer which may be $= 0$, and which has for each coefficient its proper value.

We wish to satisfy the $z$-equation by a value $z =$ descending series in $t$, viz. the form is $z = At^\alpha + A' t^{\alpha - \alpha'} + \ldots$, with powers which are integral or fractional, but where the number of positive powers is finite.

The theory is almost identical with that of the solution in like form of the algebraical equation $p_0 z^m + p_1 z^{m-1} + \cdots + p_{m-1}z + p_m = 0$, or say the equation $U = 0$, where $U$ is a rational and integral function of $z$ and $t$, of the degree $m$ as regards $z$.

\subsection*{5.}

Considering $z$ and $t$ as Cartesian coordinates, the equation in question, $U = 0$, represents a geometrical curve such for any given value of the abscissa $t$, the ordinate $z$ has $m$ values: the curve is of the order $m$ at least, but it may be of a higher order $m + \kappa$; when this is so, there is at infinity on the axis $t = 0$, a $\kappa$-tuple point $K$, and thus any line parallel to the axis $t = 0$, intersects the curve in the point $K$ counting as $\kappa$ intersections, and in $m$ other points, which are the points belonging to the $m$ values of the ordinate $z$. Taking $s = 1$, and for $z$, $t$ writing $\dfrac{z}{s}$, $\dfrac{t}{s}$ respectively, we have between the trilinear coordinates $z$, $t$, $s$ an equation $U = 0$ of the order $m + \kappa$, where $s = 0$ is the equation of the line infinity: and the curve has a $\kappa$-tuple point at the point $K$ for which $t = 0$, $s = 0$.

\subsection*{6.}

Starting from the equation $U = 0$, for an arbitrary value of $t$ we have $m$ values of $z$ all of them different. These may be developed, each of them as a descending series in $t$, and the development is effected in the usual manner; viz. considering for the moment $t$ as very large, then $z$ is in general very large and it has a leading term $At^\alpha$, which is determined by writing in the equation $z = At^\alpha$, and then finding $\alpha$ in such wise that there are two or more exponents equal to each other and greater (that is, nearer $+\infty$) than any other exponent: we have thus a highest power of $t$, the whole coefficient for which must vanish, viz. we obtain an equation of two or more terms giving for $A$ a value or values not $= 0$; in the term $At^\alpha$ in question, $\alpha$ is in general positive, but it may be $= 0$, or be negative.

The leading term $At^\alpha$ being found in this manner, the law for the exponents of the subsequent terms is frequently at once apparent; but, if necessary, we write $z = At^\alpha + A' t^{\alpha - \alpha'}$, and determine in like manner the exponent $\alpha - \alpha'$ and the coefficient $A'$. Proceeding in this manner until the law of the exponent becomes apparent, and then by the method of indeterminate coefficients, we finally arrive at a series $z = At^\alpha + A' t^{\alpha - \alpha'} + A'' t^{\alpha - \alpha' - \alpha''} + \ldots$, in descending powers of $t$: the exponents are integral or fractional, but the number of positive exponents is always finite.

There is either a single series or it may be two or more series; but the number of series is at most $= m$, and when it is less than $m$, then the coefficients in the several series or some of them will contain radicals, and by giving to each radical its different values, the system of series will determine $m$ different values of $z$.

\subsection*{7.}

The curve meets the line infinity ($s = 0$) in the point $K$ counting as $\kappa$ intersections, and in $m$ other points, some or all of which may coincide with $K$; the forms of the series depend on the configuration of the $m$ points, or say on the relation of the curve to the line infinity. Thus suppose $\kappa = 0$, and further that the $m$ points are all of them distinct, that is, let the curve be a curve of the order $m$ meeting the line infinity in $m$ distinct points, or, what is the same thing, having $m$ asymptotes, no two of them parallel; we have in this case $m$ series, each of the form $z = At + B + \dfrac{C}{t} + \ldots$, where the several coefficients $A$ have distinct values.

\subsection*{8.}

In the foregoing case $A$ is determined by an equation of the order $m$, having unequal roots; and taking for $A$ any root at pleasure, the remaining coefficients $B$, $C$, \ldots\ are each of them linearly determined. If the equation has two equal roots, this may correspond to the case of two parallel asymptotes (the curve has here a node at infinity); the coefficients $B$, $C$, \ldots\ will in this case depend on a quadric radical, and by giving to this radical its two values, we obtain the two series $z = At + B + \dfrac{C}{t} + \ldots$, corresponding to the two equal roots of the equation. But if instead of a node at infinity we have the line infinity a tangent to the curve, then instead of the two parallel asymptotes, we have an asymptotic parabola; the series assumes a new form, viz. it contains terms in $t^{\frac{1}{2}}$, $t^{-\frac{1}{2}}$, \ldots; the coefficients of the integer powers have determinate values, but those of the powers $t^{\frac{1}{2}}$, $t^{-\frac{1}{2}}$, \ldots\ contain as factor a quadric radical, and giving to this radical its two values, we have two series.

\subsection*{9.}

The like considerations apply in the case where the line infinity passes through higher multiple points of the curve, or has with it a contact or contacts other than a single ordinary contact: in every case, reckoning as distinct series those obtained by attaching to each radical its different values, the number of distinct series will be $= m$ precisely.

\subsection*{10.}

Reverting now to the differential equation $p_0[z^m + \ldots + z^{(m-1)}] + \cdots + p_{m-2}(z^2 + z') + p_{m-1}z + p_m = 0$, it is to be observed that the very same process is applicable to the determination of $z$ in the form of a descending series $z = At^\alpha + A' t^{\alpha - \alpha'} + \ldots$; moreover, it frequently happens that the determination of the leading term $At^\alpha$ is made by means of the algebraical equation $p_0 z^m + \ldots + p_{m-2}z^2 + p_{m-1}z + p_m = 0$, viz. this is so whenever the value of $\alpha$ is greater than $1$: in fact, writing $z = At^\alpha(> 1)$, then in the sets of terms $\{z^m + \ldots + z^{(m-1)}\}$, \ldots, $(z^3 + 3zz' + z'')$, $(z^2 + z')$, the first terms $z^m$, \ldots, $z^3$, $z^2$ are each of them of a higher degree than any other term in the same brackets, so that attending only to the terms of highest degree the sets may be reduced to $z^m$, \ldots, $z^3$, $z^2$. But in the determination of the subsequent exponents and coefficients, the omitted terms or some of them would of course come into play: and it is at least not obvious that we can, for the forms of the series which satisfy the differential equation, employ geometrical considerations such as those which were used in regard to the series satisfying the algebraical equation.

\subsection*{11.}

Considering as above the coefficients $p_0$, $p_1$, \ldots, $p_m$ as rational and integral functions of $t$, it is easy to determine the degrees of these coefficients in such wise that the exponent $\alpha$ of the leading term $At^\alpha$ shall have a given value. Suppose first, this given value is $\alpha = a$, a positive integer greater than $1$: the degrees may be $\theta$, $\theta + a$, $\theta + 2a$, \ldots, $\theta + ma$, ($\theta = 0$ or any positive integer at pleasure); and not only so, but if we write
\[
p_0 = L_0 t^\theta + \ldots, \quad p_1 = L_1 t^{\theta + a} + \ldots, \quad \ldots, \quad p_m = L_m t^{\theta + ma} + \ldots,
\]
then writing $z = At^a$, clearly the highest power in the equation is $t^{\theta + ma}$, and equating the coefficient hereof to zero, we have
\[
L_0 A^m + L_1 A^{m-1} + \cdots + L_{m-1}A + L_m = 0,
\]
an equation of the degree $m$ for the determination of the coefficient of the leading term $At^a$ ($a > 1$). Assuming that the roots are all unequal, we have $m$ series each of the form $z = At^a + Bt^{a-1} + \ldots + K + \dfrac{L}{t} + \ldots$.

\subsection*{12.}

Suppose $\alpha = 1$, that is, let the leading term be $At$; here the degrees may be $\theta$, $\theta + 1$, $\theta + 2$, \ldots, $\theta + m$, ($\theta = 0$ or any positive integer as before), but we have a different form for the $A$-equation. In fact, here ($z = At$) in the sets of terms $\{z^m + \ldots + z^{(m-1)}\}$, \ldots, $(z^3 + 3zz' + z'')$, $(z^2 + z')$, the terms in each brackets are of the same degree, viz. the degrees are $m$, \ldots, $3$, $2$; and not only so, but we have $z^2 + z' = (A^2 + A)t^2$, that is, $= [A]^2 t^2$; $z^3 + 3zz' + z'' = [A]^3 t^3$, \ldots. Hence if $p_0 = L_0 t^\theta + \ldots$, $p_1 = L_1 t^{\theta + 1} + \ldots$, \ldots, $p_m = L_m t^{\theta + m} + \ldots$, the $A$-equation is $L_0 [A]^m + L_1 [A]^{m-1} + \cdots + L_{m-1}[A] + L_m = 0$, an equation of the degree $m$ as before; assuming that the roots are unequal, we have here $m$ series each of the form $z = At + B + \dfrac{C}{t} + \dfrac{D}{t^2} + \ldots$, being, as will presently appear, the case of $m$ regular integrals for the $y$-equation.

\subsection*{13.}

In either of the last-mentioned cases, the formula for the $A$-equation holds good if any two or more of the coefficients $p_0$, $p_1$, \ldots, $p_m$ are of the degrees aforesaid, the others of them being of inferior degrees; we have only to put $= 0$ the $L$'s which belong to the coefficients of inferior degrees. If neither $L_0$ nor $L_m$ be $= 0$, the equation is still an equation of the order $m$, with $m$ effective roots; but if any of the coefficients $L$ at the beginning of the equation vanish we thus introduce roots $= \infty$: and if any of the coefficients $L$ at the end of the equation vanish, we thus introduce roots $= 0$: the number of effective roots is $= m$ less the number of roots $\infty$ or $0$: the case requires further consideration. If the equation has equal roots, then from what precedes, it is easy to infer that we may have distinct series containing as in the general case only integer powers of $t$, or we may have series containing fractional powers.

\subsection*{14.}

To illustrate the case of a series with fractional powers, and also in order to work out a simple example with some completeness, I consider the differential equation of the second order $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} + p_2 y = 0$, giving the $z$-equation $z^2 + z' + p_2 = 0$; $p_2$ is a rational and integral function of $t$ ($= \frac{1}{x}$), and we have two cases according as the order of the function is even or odd.

\subsection*{15.}

Suppose first that the order is even, and for convenience taking it to be $= 4$, and assuming the value of $p_2$ accordingly, I consider the equation $z^2 + z' = \alpha t^4 + \beta t^3 + \gamma t^2 + \delta t + \epsilon$. Here the form is at once seen to be $z = At^2 + Bt + C + \dfrac{D}{t} + \dfrac{E}{t^2} + \ldots$; and observing that we have $\dfrac{dz}{dt} = 2At + B - \dfrac{D}{t^2} - \dfrac{2E}{t^3} + \ldots$, we find $A^2 = \alpha$, $2AB - 2A = \beta$, $2AC + B^2 - B = \gamma$, $2AD + 2BC + C = \delta$, $2AE + 2BD + C^2 + D = \epsilon$, $2AF + 2BE + 2CD + 2E = 0$, \ldots\ equations which give
\[
A = \sqrt{\alpha}, \quad B = \frac{\beta + 2\sqrt{\alpha}}{2\sqrt{\alpha}}, \quad C = \frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\alpha}} - \frac{\beta^2}{8\alpha\sqrt{\alpha}} + \frac{\beta}{4\alpha} + \frac{3}{4\alpha\sqrt{\alpha}}, \quad \ldots
\]

\subsection*{16.}

Similarly, if the series be $z = \dfrac{F}{t^a} + \ldots$ ($a$ being a positive integer $= 2$ at least), then writing as above $t = \dfrac{1}{x}$, this is $\dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{F}{x^a} + \ldots + \dfrac{A}{x}$, whence $\log y = A \log x + Bx + \tfrac{1}{2}Cx^2 + \ldots$, or passing to the value of $y$, but expanding the exponential of $Bx + Cx^2 + \ldots$, this is $y = x^A(1 + Bx + Cx^2 + \ldots)$, viz. we have here a \textit{regular integral}. By what precedes, it appears that, for the values $p_0 = L_0 x^{-\theta} + \ldots$, $p_1 = L_1 x^{-\theta - 1} + \ldots$, \ldots, $p_m = L_m x^{-\theta - m} + \ldots$, the exponent $A$ is determined by the equation $L_0 [A]^m + L_1 [A]^{m-1} + \cdots + L_{m-1}[A] + L_m = 0$, which is the equation for that purpose considered by Fuchs, and is what I would call the \textit{indicial equation}. The logarithmic forms which belong to the case of equal roots may be deduced from the case of unequal roots, by supposing two or more of the roots to approach each other continuously and become ultimately equal.

\subsection*{17.}

We then have $\dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{A}{x^2} + \dfrac{B}{x} + C + Dx + Ex^2 + \ldots$, and consequently
\[
\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{A}{x^2} + \frac{B}{x} + C + Dx + Ex^2 + \ldots,
\]
where the second exponential is to be expanded in the form $1 + Cx + Dx^2 + \ldots$; there are of course two values, say $y_1$ and $y_2$, corresponding to the values $+\sqrt{\alpha}$ and $-\sqrt{\alpha}$ of the radical respectively.

\subsection*{18.}

Secondly, if the order be odd, and for convenience taking it to be $= 3$, and assuming the value of $p_2$ accordingly, I consider the equation $z^2 + z = \alpha t^3 + \beta t^2 + \gamma t + \delta + \epsilon t^{-1} + \ldots$. Here the series is at once seen to be $z = At^{\frac{3}{2}} + Bt + Ct^{\frac{1}{2}} + D + Et^{-\frac{1}{2}} + \ldots$: we have $z^2 = A^2 t^3 + 2AB t^{\frac{5}{2}} + (2AC + B^2)t^2 + \ldots$, and we thence derive the equations $A^2 = \alpha$, $2AB - \beta = 0$, $2AC + B^2 - B = \gamma$, $2AD + 2BC - C = \delta$, $2AE + 2BD + C^2 - D = \epsilon$, \ldots\ (where in the next equation we have on the right-hand side $\zeta$, and in each of the subsequent equations we have on the right-hand side $0$). We find $E = \ldots$, where observe that the coefficients $A$, $C$, $E$, \ldots\ contain as a factor $\sqrt{\beta}$, but the coefficients $B$, $D$, \ldots\ are rational.

\subsection*{19.}

We have $z = \dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{A}{x^{\frac{3}{2}}} + \dfrac{B}{x} + \dfrac{C}{x^{\frac{1}{2}}} + D + Ex^{\frac{1}{2}} + Fx + \ldots$, giving $\log y = \dfrac{2A}{x^{\frac{1}{2}}} + B\log x + 2Cx^{\frac{1}{2}} + Dx + \tfrac{2}{3}Ex^{\frac{3}{2}} + \tfrac{1}{2}Fx^2 + \ldots$; and thence passing to the value of $y$, but expanding the exponential of $2Cx^{\frac{1}{2}} + Dx + \ldots$, we have say the integral
\[
y_1 = e^{\frac{2A}{x^{\frac{1}{2}}}} x^B (M + Nx^{\frac{1}{2}}),
\]
and then also, changing the sign of $\sqrt{\beta}$, the integral
\[
y_2 = e^{-\frac{2A}{x^{\frac{1}{2}}}} x^B (M - Nx^{\frac{1}{2}}).
\]
Writing for a moment $Q = e^{\frac{2A}{x^{\frac{1}{2}}}}$, $M = 1 + Dx + \ldots$, $N = C + Ex + \ldots$, the two integrals are $x^B Q(M + Nx^{\frac{1}{2}})$, and $x^B Q^{-1}(M - Nx^{\frac{1}{2}})$: these may be replaced by their sum and difference, viz. these are
\begin{align*}
y_1 + y_2 &= x^B M(Q + Q^{-1}) + x^{B + \frac{1}{2}} N(Q - Q^{-1}), \\
y_1 - y_2 &= x^B M(Q - Q^{-1}) + x^{B + \frac{1}{2}} N(Q + Q^{-1}),
\end{align*}
where $Q + Q^{-1}$ is a rational function of $x$, and $Q - Q^{-1}$ is $= \alpha$ multiplied by a rational function of $x$; hence $y_1 + y_2$ is $= x^B$ multiplied by a rational function of $x$, but $y_1 - y_2$ is $= x^{B + \frac{1}{2}}$ multiplied by a rational function of $x$.

\section*{863. Note on the Theory of Linear Differential Equations}

\begin{center}
[From \textit{Crelle's Journal der Mathem.}, t.~ci. (1887), pp.~209--213.]
\end{center}

The theorem V. given by Fuchs in the memoir ``Zur Theorie der linearen Differentialgleichungen mit ver\"anderlichen Coefficienten,'' \textit{Crelle's Journal}, t.~\textsc{lxviii}. (1868), pp.~354--385 (see p.~374) for the purpose of deciding whether the integrals belonging to a group of roots of the ``determinirende Fundamentalgleichung'' (or as I call it, the Indicial equation) do or do not involve logarithms, may I think be exhibited in a clearer form.

Starting from the differential equation $P(y) = 0$ of the order $m$, then if $X$ be any function of $x$ not satisfying the differential equation, we can at once form a differential equation of the order $m + 1$, satisfied by all the solutions of the differential equation, and having also the solution $y = X$; the required equation is in fact
\[
\frac{d}{dx}P(y) \cdot P(X) - P(y) \cdot \frac{d}{dx}P(X) = 0.
\]
This I call the \textit{augmented equation}.

I recall that the equation $P(y) = 0$, considered by Fuchs, is an equation having for each singular point $x = a$, $m$ regular integrals, viz. the coefficients $p_0$, $p_1$, \ldots, $p_m$ have the forms $q_0(x - a)^m$, $q_1(x - a)^{m-1}$, \ldots, $q_m$, where $q_0$, $q_1$, \ldots, $q_m$ are rational and integral functions of $x - a$, $q_0$ not vanishing for $x = a$, and the other functions $q_1$, $q_2$, \ldots, $q_m$ not in general vanishing for $x = a$.

Writing $y = (x - a)^\theta$, we obtain $P(x - a)^\theta = I(\theta)(x - a)^\theta +$ higher powers of $(x - a)$, where $I(\theta)$, the coefficient of the lowest power of $(x - a)$, is a function of $\theta$ of the order $m$, which I call the \textit{indicial coefficient}; and equating it to zero, we have $I(\theta) = 0$, the determinirende Fundamentalgleichung, or Indicial equation, being an equation of the order $m$.

If the roots of this equation are such that no two of them are equal or differ only by an integer number, then we have $m$ particular integrals each of them of the form $y = (x - a)^r +$ higher powers of $(x - a)$, where $r$ is any root of the indicial equation: but if we have in the indicial equation a group of $\lambda$ roots $r_1$, $r_2$, \ldots, $r_\lambda$, such that the difference of each two of them is either zero or an integer, then the integrals which correspond to these roots involve or may involve logarithms; in particular, if any two of the roots are equal, the integrals for the group will involve logarithms.

Consider now the differential equation $P(y) = 0$ in reference to the singular point $x = a$ as above, and writing $X = (x - a)^\epsilon f$, where $\epsilon$ is in the first instance arbitrary, and $f$ is a rational and integral function of $x - a$ not vanishing for $x = a$, we form the augmented equation which, observing that we have in general $P(X) = (x - a)^\epsilon Q$, $Q$ a rational and integral function of $x - a$ not vanishing for $x = a$, and dividing the whole equation by $(x - a)^{\epsilon - 1}$, may be written
\[
\frac{d}{dx}P(y) \cdot (x - a)Q - P(y) \cdot [\epsilon Q + (x - a)\tfrac{d}{dx}Q] = 0,
\]
an equation of the same form as the original equation (but of the order $m + 1$ instead of $m$), and having an indicial equation.

In fact, writing as before $y = (x - a)^\theta$, we have in $\tfrac{d}{dx}P(y) \cdot (x - a)Q$ the term of lowest order $\theta I(\theta) \cdot Q \cdot (x - a)^\theta$ and in $P(y) \cdot \epsilon Q$ the term of lowest order $\epsilon I(\theta) \cdot Q \cdot (x - a)^\theta$, whereas in $P(y)(x - a)\tfrac{d}{dx}Q$ the term of lowest order is of a higher degree. We have thus for the indicial equation of the augmented equation
\[
(\theta - \epsilon)I(\theta) = 0,
\]
viz. this is the equation $I(\theta) = 0$ with the additional factor $\theta - \epsilon$, corresponding to the additional root $\epsilon$.

\subsection*{Application to Legendre's Equation.}

This is Legendre's equation $(1 - x^2)\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} - 2x\dfrac{dy}{dx} + (n^2 + n)y = 0$, with $\dfrac{1}{x}$ substituted for $x$, so that, instead of a singular point $x = \infty$, there may be a singular point $x = 0$.

Attending to the singular point $x = 0$, we have $P(x^\theta) = (\theta^2 - \theta - n^2 - n)x^\theta +$ higher powers, so that the indicial equation $I(\theta) = 0$ is $\theta^2 - \theta - n^2 - n = 0$, that is, $(\theta + n)(\theta - n - 1) = 0$, or we have the roots $n$, $n + 1$, which differ by an integer, and thus form a group, if $n$ be $=$ an integer, or be $=$ an integer $+ \tfrac{1}{2}$; to fix the ideas, say that the roots are $-p$, $p + 1$ or else $p + \tfrac{1}{2}$, $p + \tfrac{1}{2}$ where $p$ is a positive integer.

Writing for greater convenience $x^\epsilon f = x^\epsilon + F$, where $F$ is a sum of powers of $x$ higher than $\epsilon$, we find without difficulty
\[
P(x^\epsilon f) = x^\epsilon\{(\epsilon + n)(\epsilon - n - 1) - (\epsilon^2 + \epsilon)\tfrac{1}{x} + (x - x^2)x^{-\epsilon}F'' - (n^2 + n)x^{-\epsilon}F\},
\]
which, so long as $\epsilon$ remains arbitrary, is of the form $x^\epsilon Q$, $Q = (\epsilon + n)(\epsilon - n - 1) +$ powers of $x$; if however $\epsilon$ be a root of the indicial equation, for instance, if $\epsilon = n$, then the expression in brackets $\{\}$ contains at any rate the factor $x$, so that the form is $P(x^{-n}f) = x^\mu Q$, where $\mu$ is $= n + 1$ at least; we can however, by a proper determination of the function $f$, make $\mu$ acquire a larger value.

For instance, suppose $n$, $n + 1 = 2$, $3$; $\epsilon = n = 2$, and assume
\[
x^\epsilon f = x^{-2} + Bx^{-1} + C + Dx + Ex^2 + Fx^3 + Gx^4 + \ldots
\]
To calculate $P(x^\epsilon f)$, we have
\[
P(x^\epsilon f) = \begin{vmatrix}
-2 & -B & 0 & D & 2E & 3F & 4G \\
\cdot & -2B & -3C & -4D & -5E & -6F & -7G \\
\cdot & \cdot & 6 & 2 & 0 & 0 & 0
\end{vmatrix}
\]
giving $P(x^\epsilon f) = x^{-2}\{6 + 2Bx + (-6C + 6)x^2 + (-4D - 6B + 2D)x^3 + \ldots\}$.

We have here if $B$ not $= 0$, $\mu = 1$; but if $B = 0$, then we cannot in any way make the coefficient of $x^2$ to vanish, and consequently $\mu = 3$.

With this last value of $\mu$, the group $(\mu, n, n + 1)$, that is, $(\mu, 2, 3)$, becomes $(3, 2, 3)$ which contains two equal roots, and the conclusion from the theorem thus is that the integrals $u_1$, $u_2$, corresponding to the roots $2$, $3$, involve logarithmic values.

And similarly in general the integrals $u_1$, $u_2$, corresponding to the roots $p + \tfrac{1}{2}$, $p + \tfrac{1}{2}$ ($p$ any positive integer), involve logarithmic values: this also is right.

The examples exhibit the true character of the theorem, and show I think that it is a less remarkable one than would at first sight appear: in fact, in working them out, we really ascertain by an actual substitution whether the differential equation can be satisfied by series of powers only, without logarithms. Thus for $n = 2$ as above, it appears that the equation is satisfied by the series
\[
y = x^{-2}\left[1 - \frac{3}{2}x^2 + \frac{15}{8}x^4 + \ldots\right],
\]
and the like series with the terms in $x^2$, $x^4$, \ldots\ involving $\log x$; and the theorem gives the same result.

y' &= y + \rho\eta, \\

\end{align*}
viz. the equations give $x$, $y$, $z$, $\xi$, $\eta$, $\zeta$, each of them as a function of two independent parameters $u$, $v$, $\rho$ is a function of $u$, $v$ and of the arbitrary constant $C$; hence, giving to $C$ any assumed value, we have $x'$, $y'$, $z'$ each of them a function of the two arbitrary parameters $u$, $v$; that is, $x'$, $y'$, $z'$ are the coordinates of a point on a surface, one of a system of parallel surfaces (corresponding to the different values of $C$) which are the surfaces in question.

\section*{865. On Multiple Algebra}

\begin{center}
[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.~\textsc{xxii}. (1887), pp.~270--308.]
\end{center}

\subsection*{1.}

I reproduce a passage from my Presidential Address, British Association, Southport, 1883.

``Outside of ordinary mathematics we have some theories which must be referred to: algebraical, geometrical, logical. It is, as in many other cases, difficult to draw the line: we do in ordinary mathematics use symbols not denoting quantities, which we nevertheless combine in the way of addition and multiplication, $a + b$ and $ab$, and which may be such as not to obey the commutative law $ab = ba$; in particular, this is or may be so in regard to symbols of operation; and it could hardly be said that any development whatever of the theory of such symbols of operation did not belong to ordinary algebra.

But I do separate from ordinary algebra the system of multiple algebra or linear associative algebra developed in the valuable memoir by the late Benjamin Peirce, ``Linear Associative Algebra'' (1870, reprinted 1881 in the \textit{American Journal of Mathematics}, vol.~\textsc{iv}., with notes and addenda by his son, C.~S.~Peirce): we here consider symbols A, B, \&c., which are linear functions of a determinate number of letters or units $i$, $j$, $k$, $l$, \&c., with coefficients which are ordinary analytical magnitudes real or imaginary, viz. the coefficients are in general of the form $\alpha + i\beta$, where $i$ is the before-mentioned imaginary, or $\sqrt{-1}$ of ordinary analysis. The letters $i$, $j$, $k$, \&c., are such that every binary combination $i^2$, $ij$, $ji$, \&c., ($ij$ not in general $= ji$), is equal to a linear function of the letters, but under the restriction of satisfying the associative law; viz. for each combination of three letters $ij \cdot k = i \cdot jk$, so that there is a determinate and unique product of three or more letters; or, what is the same thing; the laws of combination of the units $i$, $j$, $k$, \ldots\ are defined by a multiplication table giving the values of $i^2$, $ij$, $ji$, \&c.; the original units may be replaced by linear functions of these units, so as to give rise for the units finally adopted to a multiplication table of the most simple form; and it is very remarkable how frequently in these simplified forms we have nilpotent or idempotent symbols ($i^2 = 0$ or $i^2 = i$ as the case may be), and symbols $i$, $j$, such that $ij = ji = 0$; and consequently how simple are the forms of the multiplication tables which define the several systems respectively.

``I have spoken of this multiple algebra before referring to various geometrical theories of earlier date, because I consider it as the general analytical basis, and the true basis, of these theories. I do not realise to myself directly the notions of the addition or multiplication of two lines, areas, rotations, or other geometrical, kinematical, or mechanical entities; and I would formulate a general theory as follows: consider any such entity as determined by the proper number of parameters $a$, $b$, $c$, \ldots\ (for instance, in the case of a finite line given in magnitude and position, these might be the length, the coordinates of one end, and the direction-cosines of the line considered as drawn from this end); and represent it by or connect it with the linear function $ai + bj + ck +$ \&c., formed with these parameters as coefficients and with a given set of units $i$, $j$, $k$, \&c. Conversely, any such linear function represents an entity of the kind in question. Two given entities are represented by two linear functions; the sum of these is a like function representing an entity of the same kind, which may be regarded as the sum of the two entities; and the product of them (taken in a determinate order) is in like manner a like function representing an entity of the same kind, which may be regarded as the product of the two entities. The laws of addition and multiplication of the units are given by a multiplication table; and thus the whole theory is made to depend upon that of the multiple algebra or linear associative algebra; and, in particular, assuming (as is or may be the case in the theories referred to) that the product is not in general commutative, this is simply the consequence of the assumed multiplication table.''

\subsection*{2.}

It is proper to refer at once to the original memoir by Benjamin Peirce, to a paper by me ``On the Theory of Matrices'' (Phil.\ Trans., 1858), pp.~17--37, [52], and to the ``Memoir on the Theory of Matrices'' (ibid., 1858, pp.~475--496), [75]. A set of $n^2$ quantities arranged in a square is a \textit{matrix}; two matrices are added by adding corresponding terms; they are multiplied according to a rule first given by Cayley, viz. if the matrices are $(a, b, c)$ and $(a', b', c')$, then the product is $(aa' + bb' + cc', ab' + bd' + cf', \ldots)$, \&c. The theory includes as a particular case the theory of quaternions, where $n = 2$.

\subsection*{3.}

I consider a double symbol $(x, y)$, and assume for the laws of combination:
\[
(x, y) + (x', y') = (x + x', y + y'),
\]
and
\[
(x, y)(x', y') = (X, Y),
\]
where $P$, $Q$, $X$, $Y$ are given functions of $x$, $y$, $x'$, $y'$.

For greater simplicity the law of addition is taken to be
\[
(x, y) + (x', y') = (x + x', y + y'),
\]
so that as regards addition the multiple symbols are commutative and associative. But this is or is not the case for multiplication, according to the form of the given functions $X$, $Y$; for instance, if $(x, y)(x', y') = (xx' - yy', xy' + yx')$, then in regard to multiplication the symbols will be commutative and associative. But if $(x, y, z)(x', y', z') = (yz' - y'z, zx' - z'x, xy' - x'y)$, then the symbols will be associative, but not commutative.

\subsection*{4.}

I remark here that we are in general concerned with symbols of a given multiplicity, double symbols $(x, y)$, triple symbols $(x, y, z)$, $n$-tuple symbols $(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)$, as the case may be, and that as well the product as the sum is a symbol \textit{ejusdem generis}, and consequently of the same multiplicity, with the component symbols; this is to be assumed throughout in the absence of an express statement to the contrary.

It is, moreover, proper to narrow the notion of multiplication by restricting it to the case where the terms $(X, Y, \ldots)$ of the product are linear functions of the terms $(x, y, \ldots)$ and $(x', y', \ldots)$ of the component symbols respectively; any other form $(X, Y, \ldots)$ is better designated not as a product, but as a combination (or by some other name) of the component symbols $(x, y, \ldots)$ and $(x', y', \ldots)$.

\subsection*{5.}

I assume, moreover, that, if $m$ be any ordinary analytical magnitude, then the product of the multiple symbol $(x, y, \ldots)$ and the ordinary magnitude $m$ is the multiple symbol $(mx, my, \ldots)$; this is in effect to assume that each of the extraordinaries is commutable with every ordinary magnitude.

\subsection*{6.}

I do not attempt any enumeration or classification of the different systems of multiple algebra, but I consider in particular the system of double algebra. In this case, writing $(x, y) = ix + jy$, where $i$, $j$ are the extraordinaries, we have for the product:
\[
(ix + jy)(ix' + jy') = i(xx' - yy') + j(xy' + yx'),
\]
which expressions will agree together if, and only if, $i^2 = i$, $ij = j$, $ji = j$, $j^2 = i$, or, as these equations may be written, $i^2 = i$, $ij = ji = j$, $j^2 = i$.

\subsection*{7.}

And, conversely, from this multiplication table of the extraordinaries $i$, $j$, we obtain $(ix + jy)(ix' + jy') = i(xx' - yy') + j(xy' + yx')$, that is, $(x, y)(x', y') = (xx' - yy', xy' + yx')$, the originally assumed formula of multiplication.

In the example just given, we have $i^2 = i$, $ij = ji = j$, viz. the symbol $i$ comports itself like unity, and may be put $= 1$; we have $(x, y) = x + jy$, with the multiplication table $1 \cdot j = j \cdot 1 = j$, $j^2 = -1$; or simply the equation $j^2 = -1$; and then $(x + jy)(x' + jy') = xx' - yy' + j(xy' + yx')$; and it is convenient to regard $1$ as an extraordinary, and speak of the system of extraordinaries $1$, $j$.

\subsection*{8.}

The separate terms $x$, $y$, \ldots, whatever be their number, may always without loss of generality be arranged in a line; but it may be convenient to arrange them in a different form, for instance, in that of a square; we may have for instance symbols $x$, $y$, $z$, $w$ with the laws of combination $(x, y, z, w) + (x', y', z', w') = (x + x', y + y', z + z', w + w')$, and $(x, y, z, w)(x', y', z', w') = (X, Y, Z, W)$.

\subsection*{9.}

A multiple symbol may be connected with a geometrical or physical entity of any kind: viz. any such entity, depending on a number of parameters susceptible of analytical magnitude, to fix the ideas say on two parameters $(x, y)$, may be connected with the multiple symbol $(x, y)$. We cannot in general directly conceive the addition or multiplication of such entities, but the assumed laws of combination of the multiple symbols in effect serve as definitions of the operations. Thus $(x, y) + (x', y') = (x + x', y + y')$; $(x, y) \cdot (x', y') = (xx' - yy', xy' + yx')$: or the sum of the entities whose parameters are $x$, $y$ and $x'$, $y'$ is by definition the entity whose parameters are $x + x'$, $y + y'$; and the product of the same entities is by definition the entity whose parameters are $xx' - yy'$, $xy' + yx'$.

The entities are thus, as regards addition, commutative and associative; but, as regards multiplication they are or are not commutative, or associative, according to the assumed law of multiplication of the multiple symbols.

\subsection*{10.}

If, as above, the multiple symbol be represented by means of extraordinaries, $(x, y) = ix + jy$, then the extraordinaries $i$, $j$, qua multiple symbols $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$, are themselves special entities of the kind in question, their laws of combination (as such special entities) being included in the general laws for the combination of such entities; or, if we please, these general laws being derived from the assumed laws for the special entities.

\subsection*{11.}

The theory of imaginaries, or imaginary quantities, of ordinary analysis, belongs to the foregoing theory. The ordinary imaginary $x + iy$ is the double symbol $(x, y)$, with the law of multiplication $(x, y)(x', y') = (xx' - yy', xy' + yx')$, that is, $i^2 = -1$. And the theory extends to the ``plurality of imaginaries,'' considered by Hamilton and others.

\subsection*{12.}

Another peculiarity may be noticed: suppose that, in general, $(x, y, z, w) + (x', y', z', w') = (x + x', y + y', z + z', w + w')$; $(x, y, z, w)(x', y', z', w') = (X, Y, Z, W)$; then, as a particular case hereof, we have the addition-equation $(x, y, 0, 0) + (x', y', 0, 0) = (x + x', y + y', 0, 0)$ and it may very well be that for the same two symbols the multiplication-equation may take the form $(x, y, 0, 0)(x', y', 0, 0) = (0, 0, Z, W)$, ($Z$, $W$ each of them a function of $x$, $y$, $x'$, $y'$); viz. here, in a different point of view, the component symbols $(x, y, 0, 0)$ and $(x', y', 0, 0)$, are double systems of a certain kind $\{x, y\}$, $\{x', y'\}$, and the product is a double system of a different kind $[Z, W]$, or it might have been $(0, Y, Z, W)$, a triple system $[Y, Z, W]$.

Of course, all peculiarity disappears when we revert from the particular symbols $(x, y, 0, 0)$ to the general symbols $(x, y, z, w)$, from which we may regard them as derived.

\subsection*{13.}

The peculiarity just referred to presents itself naturally when we regard the symbols as representing geometrical or physical entities; it may very well be that the product of two entities of a certain kind is taken to be an entity of a different kind (for instance, the product of two points to be a line), and that the analytical theory of the multiple symbol is constructed in order to such a relation; and it may further be that only the symbol $(x, y, 0, 0)$ or $\{x, y\}$ is interpreted with reference to an entity of the one kind, the general symbol $(x, y, z, w)$ being interpreted as representing an entity of the other kind.

\subsection*{14.}

In the theory of imaginaries, we have the product of two extraordinaries $i$, $j$, each of them an imaginary of the form $x + iy$, is equal to a linear function of $1$ and $i$; that is, the product of two entities of the one kind is an entity of the same kind. But we may very well have a system in which this is not the case: for instance, taking extraordinaries $1$, $i$, $j$, $k$, we may have the product of any two of them a linear function of all four. An interesting case is when the product of two imaginaries is a quaternion; here the product of two entities of the one kind is an entity of a different kind.

\subsection*{15. Geometrical Representation of Imaginaries.}

Art. Nos.~16--42.

\textbf{16. Argand, 1806.} The theory is that established by Argand in the paper ``Essai sur une mani\`ere de repr\'esenter les quantit\'es imaginaires dans les constructions g\'eom\'etriques'' (Paris, 1806, republished in \textit{Gergonne's Annales}, t.~\textsc{iv}. (1813), pp.~133--147). Argand denotes a directed line by an algebraic quantity; and in particular he has the theorem that the sum of two such lines is the diagonal of the parallelogram formed by them. As to the product, if two lines have the lengths $r$, $r'$ and inclinations $\theta$, $\theta'$ to the axis, then the product-line has the length $rr'$ and the inclination $\theta + \theta'$; in particular, if the lines are at right angles, the product is the line having length $rr'$ and inclination $\theta + \theta' + 90^{\circ}$. He thus arrives at the representation of $\sqrt{-1}$ by a line at right angles to the axis.

\textbf{17. Francais, 1813.} The theory was independently discovered by Francais; see his paper ``Nouveaux principes de g\'eom\'etrie de position, et interpr\'etation g\'eom\'etrique des symboles imaginaires'' (\textit{Gergonne's Annales}, t.~\textsc{iv}. (1813), pp.~61--71). He gives the following rule for multiplication: to multiply two directed lines, multiply the lengths and add the inclinations.

\textbf{18. Warren, 1828.} John Warren, in his ``Treatise on the Geometrical Representation of the Square Roots of Negative Quantities'' (Cambridge, 1828), develops the theory at length. Peacock, Preface to Algebra, 1830, speaks of Warren's work as ``distinguished for great originality and for extreme boldness in the use of definitions.''

But we have, in fact, passed out of this view, and have come to regard $a + bi$ as an ordinary analytical magnitude; viz. in every case an ordinary symbol represents or may represent such a magnitude, and the magnitude (and as a particular case thereof, the symbol $i$) is commutable with the extraordinaries of any system of multiple algebra.

\subsection*{15. Geometrical Representation of Imaginaries.}

\subsection*{The Barycentric Calculus: M\"obius.}

Art. Nos.~30--34.

\textbf{30. M\"obius, 1827.} M\"obius, \textit{Der barycentrische Calcul}, 8vo. Leipzig, 1827, reprinted in Vol.~I. of the \textit{Gesammelte Werke}, Leipzig, 1885. The theory applies to points in a line, in a plane, or in space; but it will be sufficient to consider the case of points in a plane.

The idea is that, taking in the plane any three points $A$, $B$, $C$ as fundamental points, then regarding these as loaded with the proper positive or negative weights $p$, $q$, $r$, any other point $P$ of the plane may be regarded as the centre of gravity of the three weights; we have the equation $P = pA + qB + rC$, where $p + q + r = 1$; this is the \textit{barycentric} representation of the point $P$. And then, taking another point $P' = p'A + q'B + r'C$, we have $P + P' = (p + p')A + (q + q')B + (r + r')C$, which is the barycentric representation of the sum of the two points. And so for the product.

\textbf{31.} The system of M\"obius is, in fact, a system of multiple algebra; the points $A$, $B$, $C$ are extraordinaries, and the point $P = pA + qB + rC$ is a multiple symbol of which the multiplicities are $(p, q, r)$. The multiplication table is a table giving the products $A^2$, $AB$, $BA$, $AC$, \&c.; and from this the product of any two points $P = pA + qB + rC$ and $P' = p'A + q'B + r'C$ can be at once obtained.

\textbf{32.} In particular, if $P = P'$, we obtain $P^2$, the barycentric square of the point $P$; and so on. M\"obius does not use any general multiplication table, but he has a special theory of the product of two points, which is, in fact, the theory of the products $AB$, $AC$, $BC$, each of them being a determinate point of the line joining the two points; and from these he obtains the product of any two points $P$, $P'$ as a point on the line $PP'$.

\textbf{33.} The method is applied very elegantly to the solution of problems, for instance, t.~V., p.~248; given two lines $AB$ and $CD$, to find a point $H$, such that the triangles $ABH$, $CDH$ may be directly similar, that is, such that they can be, by a rotation of one of them, brought to be similarly situate. The condition for this is $\dfrac{AH}{AB} = \dfrac{CH}{CD}$, that is, $AH \cdot CD = AB \cdot CH$, $= AB(AH - AC)$, whence $AB \cdot AC = AH(AB - CD)$; or constructing a point $E$ such that $BE = DC$, viz. the line $BE$ is drawn from $B$ equal and parallel to and in the same sense with $DC$, then $AB - CD = AE$, and the foregoing equation becomes $AH \cdot AE = AB \cdot AC$, or say $\dfrac{AH}{AB} = \dfrac{AC}{AE}$; viz. the required point $H$ is such that the triangle $ACH$ is directly similar to $AEB$.

\textbf{34.} I notice an expression t.~VIII., p.~19, for the area of a triangle $ABC$, $= \tfrac{1}{4}(BC \cdot \text{cj.}\, AB - AB \cdot \text{cj.}\, BC)$, where cj.\ $AB$, conjugate of $AB$, means the line through the mid-point of $AB$ at right angles to $AB$.

\subsection*{Quaternions.}

Art. Nos.~35--42.

\textbf{35.} A quaternion is the sum of a scalar or mere magnitude $w$, plus a vector $ix + jy + kz$, which represents a line drawn from an origin to the point whose rectangular coordinates are $x$, $y$, $z$; we have thus a quaternion $Q = w + ix + jy + kz$, representing a line, and in connexion therewith a scalar $w$. Hamilton refers to the theory of Warren and Peacock as having in part suggested his investigations, but the contrast is very striking; neither the form $x + iy$, where the directed magnitude $x$ is unaccompanied by an extraordinary, nor the form $kx + iy$ with any multiplication table for $k$, $i$, is in any wise analogous to the quaternion form.

The system is a system of multiple algebra; $1$, $i$, $j$, $k$ are extraordinaries, and the multiplication table is $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$, whence $ij = k$, $jk = i$, $ki = j$, $ji = -k$, $kj = -i$, $ik = -j$.

\textbf{36.} But for the multiplication of two vectors, the form is simplified: viz. we have
\[
W = (ix + jy + kz)(ix' + jy' + kz') = -(xx' + yy' + zz') + i(yz' - y'z) + j(zx' - z'x) + k(xy' - x'y).
\]

We may consider unit-vectors, $U = ix + jy + kz$, where $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1$, and unit-quaternions $= w + ix + jy + kz$, where $w^2 + x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1$; obviously the general form of a vector is $V = rU$, or, say the length is $r$, and the cosine-inclinations are $x$, $y$, $z$; similarly, the general form of a unit-quaternion is $= \cos\theta + \sin\theta \cdot U$, and that of a quaternion is $Q = T(\cos\theta + \sin\theta \cdot U)$, $T$ is the tensor, $\theta$ the amplitude, and $x$, $y$, $z$ the cosine-inclinations of the unit-vector.

For the product of two unit-vectors, we have $UU' = -(xx' + yy' + zz') + i(yz' - y'z) + j(zx' - z'x) + k(xy' - x'y)$, which is a unit-quaternion.

\textbf{37.} The product of a quaternion by a vector is a vector; viz. $QV =$ a vector, say it is $V'' = iX'' + jY'' + kZ''$. Let $\delta$, considered as a positive angle less than $\pi$, so that $\cos\delta = xx' + yy' + zz'$, $\sin\delta = +\sqrt{1 - \cos^2\delta}$, be the inclination of the two vectors; we have $\cos\delta = xx' + yy' + zz'$.

\textbf{38.} The figure serves also to represent the unit-quaternion $= \cos\delta + \sin\delta \cdot U''$, which is the product of the two vectors.

Observe that, in thus expressing a quaternion as a product of two vectors, only the plane of the vectors and the angle between them are determinate; we may rotate the angle $UOU'$ in its own plane at pleasure.

In particular, if the two vectors are at right angles, then $\delta = 90^{\circ}$, and the product is $UU'' = U''$, viz. the product of the two unit-vectors at right angles to each other is a unit-vector at right angles to the plane of the two unit-vectors.

If $\delta = 0$, then the two unit-vectors coincide, and we have $U^2 = -1$, viz. the square of a unit-vector is the scalar $-1$.

The product of any two vectors is given by the like formulae; if $V = rU$, $V' = r'U'$ respectively, then $VV' = rr' \cdot UU'$.

\textbf{39.} We hence find $QQ' = QU \cdot (-UQ') = VV' = (iX + jY + kZ)(iX' + jY' + kZ')$, $= -XX' - YY' - ZZ' + i(YZ' - Y'Z) + j(ZX' - Z'X) + k(XY' - X'Y)$. This should, of course, be identically equal to $ww' - xx' - yy' - zz' + i(wx' + xw' + yz' - y'z) + j(wy' + yw' + zx' - z'x) + k(wz' + zw' + xy' - x'y)$, on substituting for $X$, $Y$, $Z$, $X'$, $Y'$, $Z'$ their values as above.

\textbf{40.} The steps are, taking $U$ the unit-vector at right angles to $Q$, $Q'$, then the vectors.

\textbf{41.} There is a kind of quaternion operator $Q^{-1}(\ )Q$, or, what is the same thing, if $\Lambda = i\lambda + j\mu + k\nu$, $(1 - \Lambda)(\ )(1 + \Lambda)$, which is the symbol of a rotation; viz. the operand, say the vector $ix + jy + kz$, is to be placed within the vacant $( )$, say $Q^{-1}(\ )Q \cdot (ix + jy + kz)$ means $Q^{-1}(ix + jy + kz)Q$; and this being so, the result is a vector $ix_1 + jy_1 + kz_1$, where the $x_1$, $y_1$, $z_1$ are what the $x$, $y$, $z$ become by a rotation of the vector $ix + jy + kz$ about an axis and through an angle determined by the quaternion $Q$; viz. if as above then writing $Q = 1 + i\lambda + j\mu + k\nu$, $\lambda$, $\mu$, $\nu = \tan\tfrac{1}{2}\theta \cos f$, $\tan\tfrac{1}{2}\theta \cos g$, $\tan\tfrac{1}{2}\theta \cos h$, where $\cos^2 f + \cos^2 g + \cos^2 h = 1$, and therefore $\lambda^2 + \mu^2 + \nu^2 = \tan^2\tfrac{1}{2}\theta$, then $f$, $g$, $h$ are the inclinations of the axis, and $\theta$ is the angle of rotation.

The actual formula is
\begin{multline*}
x_1 = \frac{1}{1 + \lambda^2 + \mu^2 + \nu^2} [(1 + \lambda^2 - \mu^2 - \nu^2)x + 2(\lambda\mu + \nu)y + 2(\lambda\nu - \mu)z, \\
+ 2(\lambda\mu - \nu)x + (1 - \lambda^2 + \mu^2 - \nu^2)y + 2(\mu\nu + \lambda)z \\
+ 2(\lambda\nu + \mu)x + 2(\mu\nu - \lambda)y + (1 - \lambda^2 - \mu^2 + \nu^2)z]
\end{multline*}
viz. the form on the right-hand side is $i(\alpha x + \alpha' y + \alpha'' z) + j(\beta x + \beta' y + \beta'' z) + k(\gamma x + \gamma' y + \gamma'' z)$.

\subsection*{The Ausdehnungslehre: Grassmann.}

Art. Nos.~43--63.

\textbf{43. Grassmann.} \textit{Die lineale Ausdehnungslehre, ein neuer Zweig der Mathematik}, Leipzig, 1844; 2nd edition, with the title \textit{Die Ausdehnungslehre von 1844}, Leipzig, 1878. --- \textit{Die Ausdehnungslehre, vollst\"andig und in strenger Form bearbeitet}, Berlin, 1862, referred to as \textit{Die Ausdehnungslehre von 1862}.

\textbf{44. Plane Geometry.} The representation of a point is that employed by M\"obius in the Barycentrische Calcul (1827), viz. considering in the plane three fixed points $A_1$, $A_2$, $A_3$, and in regard to the triangle formed by these points, taking $x_1$, $x_2$, $x_3$ as the areal coordinates of a point $x$ ($x_1$, $x_2$, $x_3$ are equal to the areas of the triangles $xA_2A_3$, $xA_3A_1$, $xA_1A_2$, each divided by the area of the triangle $A_1A_2A_3$; whence $x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 1$), we write $x = x_1e_1 + x_2e_2 + x_3e_3$ as the representation of the point $x$. Here $e_1$, $e_2$, $e_3$ are extraordinaries, or they may also be regarded as points, viz. as the points $A_1$, $A_2$, $A_3$ respectively.

\textbf{45.} The product of the two points is the line joining them; the product of three points is the area of the triangle formed by them. We have thus a system of multiple algebra in which the sum is a point, but the product of two points is a line, and the product of three points is an area; this is a case of the peculiarity referred to in No.~12.

\textbf{46.} In the Ausdehnungslehre von 1844, the multiplication is what Grassmann calls ``linear'' multiplication; viz. the product of the two points $x = x_1e_1 + x_2e_2 + x_3e_3$ and $y = y_1e_1 + y_2e_2 + y_3e_3$ is the line represented by
\[
xy = (x_1y_2 - x_2y_1)e_1e_2 + (x_2y_3 - x_3y_2)e_2e_3 + (x_3y_1 - x_1y_3)e_3e_1,
\]
where $e_1e_2$, $e_2e_3$, $e_3e_1$ are the lines $A_1A_2$, $A_2A_3$, $A_3A_1$ respectively.

\textbf{47.} In the Ausdehnungslehre von 1862, the multiplication is what Grassmann calls ``outer'' or ``extensive'' multiplication; the product is in this case the same as in the linear multiplication, but the theory is presented in a different form.

\textbf{48.} The product of three points $x$, $y$, $z$ is the area of the triangle formed by them; it is a quantity of the third order, and is expressed as a multiple of the fundamental area $e_1e_2e_3$ (the area of the triangle $A_1A_2A_3$).

\textbf{49.} Grassmann considers also what he calls ``inner'' or ``regressive'' multiplication; this is a multiplication in which the product of two lines is a point, the product of a line and a point is an area, \&c. The theory is very elegant, but I do not attempt to explain it here.

\textbf{50.} The Ausdehnungslehre contains also a theory of functions of multiple quantities; viz. Grassmann considers functions of points, lines, areas, \&c., and develops a theory of differentiation and integration for such functions. This part of the theory is very interesting, but it is too extensive to be here considered.

\textbf{51.} The Ausdehnungslehre extends to space of any number of dimensions; in particular, for three-dimensional space, we have four fundamental points $A_1$, $A_2$, $A_3$, $A_4$, and the coordinates $x_1$, $x_2$, $x_3$, $x_4$ of a point $x$ are the tetrahedral coordinates (volumes of the tetrahedra $xA_2A_3A_4$, \&c., each divided by the volume of the tetrahedron $A_1A_2A_3A_4$; whence $x_1 + x_2 + x_3 + x_4 = 1$). The product of two points is the line joining them; the product of three points is the plane through them; the product of four points is the volume of the tetrahedron formed by them.

\section*{866. Note on Kiepert's $L$-Equations, in the Transformation of Elliptic Functions}

\begin{center}
[From the \textit{Mathematische Annalen}, t.~xxx. (1887), pp.~75--77.]
\end{center}

It appears, by comparison with Klein's paper ``Ueber die Transformation u.~s.~w.,'' \textit{Math.~Annalen}, t.~xiv. (1878), see p.~144, that Kiepert's $L$ made use of in the Memoir ``Ueber Theilung und Transformation der elliptischen Functionen,'' \textit{Math.~Annalen}, t.~xxvi. (1886), pp.~369--454, is, in fact, the square of the multiplier, ``f\"ur das durch $\sqrt[12]{\Delta}$ normirte Integral,'' viz. considering the general quartic function $(a, \ldots)(x, 1)^4 = (a, b, c, d, e)(x, 1)^4$, and the transformed function $(a_1, \ldots)(y, 1)^4$, then we have
\[
\frac{L^2 \sqrt[12]{\Delta}\, dx}{\sqrt{(a, \ldots)(x, 1)^4}} = \frac{\sqrt[12]{\Delta_1}\, dy}{\sqrt{(a_1, \ldots)(y, 1)^4}},
\]
where if
\[I = ae - 4bd + 3c^2, \quad J = ace - ad^2 - b^2e + 2bcd - c^3,\]
and similarly $I_1$, $J_1$, are the invariants of the two functions, then $\Delta$, $\Delta_1$ are the discriminants
\[\Delta = I^3 - 27J^2, \quad \Delta_1 = I_1^3 - 27J_1^2,\]
and the $\gamma_2$, $\gamma_3$ of Kiepert's equations are
\[\gamma_2 = I \div \sqrt[3]{\Delta}, \quad \gamma_3 = J \div \sqrt{\Delta},\]
whence
\[\gamma_2^3 - 27\gamma_3^2 = 1.\]

In particular, if the forms are
\[1 - x^2 \cdot 1 - k^2x^2, \quad \text{and} \quad 1 - y^2 \cdot 1 - \lambda^2 y^2,\]
then $\Delta$, $\Delta_1$ are the $M$ and if as usual $k = u^4$, $\lambda = v^4$, and $M$ is the multiplier for the form
\[
\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1 - x^2 \cdot 1 - k^2x^2}} = \frac{M\,dy}{\sqrt{1 - y^2 \cdot 1 - \lambda^2y^2}},
\]
then we have
\begin{align*}
I &= \tfrac{1}{12}(1 + 14u^8 + u^{16}), \\
J &= \tfrac{1}{216}(1 + u^8)(1 - 34u^8 + u^{16}), \\
\Delta &= \tfrac{1}{16}u^8(1 - u^8)^4, \quad \Delta_1 = \tfrac{1}{16}v^8(1 - v^8)^4,
\end{align*}
\[
\gamma_2 = \tfrac{1}{6}\sqrt[3]{2}\, \frac{1 + 14u^8 + u^{16}}{u^{\frac{8}{3}}(1 - u^8)^{\frac{4}{3}}}, \quad
\gamma_3 = \tfrac{1}{54}\, \frac{(1 + u^8)(1 - 34u^8 + u^{16})}{u^4(1 - u^8)^2},
\]
and thence
\[L^2 = \frac{v^{\frac{8}{3}}(1 - v^8)^{\frac{4}{3}}}{u^{\frac{8}{3}}(1 - u^8)^{\frac{4}{3}}} \cdot \frac{1}{M},\]
which last equation is the expression for $L^2$ in terms of the Jacobian symbols $u$, $v$, $M$.

As an easy verification in a particular case, suppose $n = 5$. We have here
\begin{align*}
L^2 &= \frac{v^{\frac{8}{3}}(1 - v^8)^{\frac{4}{3}}}{u^{\frac{8}{3}}(1 - u^8)^{\frac{4}{3}}} \cdot \frac{1}{M}, \quad
M = \frac{v(1 - uv^5)}{v - u^5}, \quad \Bigl(= \frac{v + u^5}{5u(1 + u^3v)}\Bigr), \\
u^6 - v^6 &+ 5u^2v^2(u^2 - v^2) + 4uv(1 - u^4v^4) = 0,
\end{align*}
\[\gamma_2 = \tfrac{1}{6}\sqrt[3]{2}\, \frac{1 + 14u^8 + u^{16}}{u^{\frac{8}{3}}(1 - u^8)^{\frac{4}{3}}};\]
and it should be possible, by eliminating $u$, $v$, $M$, to deduce hence the $L$-equation
\[L^{12} + 10L^6 - 12\gamma_2 L^2 + 5 = 0. \quad \text{(Kiepert, p.~428.)}\]
It does not seem in any wise easy to do this in the case of an arbitrary modulus; but writing the modular equation in the form
\[(u^2 - v^2)(u^4 + 6u^2v^2 + v^4) + 4uv(1 - u^4v^4) = 0,\]
we satisfy this by
\[uv - 1 = 0, \quad u^4 + 6u^2v^2 + v^4 = 0,\]
or say by
\[v = \frac{1}{u}, \quad u^8 + 6u^4 + 1 = 0,\]
and the equation may be verified for this particular modulus.

We have
\[1 + 14u^8 + u^{16} = 48u^8, \quad (1 - u^8)^2 = 32u^8,\]
and consequently
\[\gamma_2 = \tfrac{1}{6}\sqrt[3]{2} \cdot \frac{48u^8}{u^{\frac{8}{3}}(32u^8)^{\frac{4}{3}}} = \frac{1}{2 \cdot 3} \cdot 2^{\frac{1}{3}} \cdot \frac{2^4 \cdot 3u^8}{u^{\frac{8}{3}} \cdot 2^{\frac{10}{3}} \cdot u^{\frac{16}{3}}} = 1, \quad (\text{whence also } \gamma_3 = 0).\]

Moreover
\[M = \frac{\tfrac{1}{u} - u}{\tfrac{1}{u} - u^5} = -\frac{1}{u^2}\, \frac{1 - u^2}{1 - u^6} = \frac{-1}{u^2(1 + u^2 + u^4)},\]
and thence
\[L^2 = \frac{\tfrac{1}{u^{\frac{8}{3}}}(1 - \tfrac{1}{u^8})^{\frac{4}{3}}}{u^{\frac{8}{3}}(1 - u^8)^{\frac{4}{3}}} \cdot \frac{1}{M} = \frac{(u^8 - 1)^{\frac{4}{3}}}{u^4(1 - u^8)^{\frac{4}{3}}} \cdot \frac{1}{M} = -\frac{1}{u^4M},\]
that is,
\[L^2 = 1 + u^2 + u^{-2}.\]
But we have
\[u^4 = -3 + 2\sqrt{2},\]
and thence
\[u^2 = i(1 - \sqrt{2}), \quad u^{-2} = i(1 + \sqrt{2}),\]
and
\[L^2 = 1 + 2i,\]
whence
\[L^{12} + 10L^6 - 12\gamma_2 L^2 + 5 = (117 + 44i) + 10(-11 - 2i) - 12(1 + 2i) + 5 = 0,\]
or the $L$-equation is satisfied.

\section*{867. Note on the Jacobian Sextic Equation}

\begin{center}
[From the \textit{Mathematische Annalen}, t.~xxx. (1887), pp.~78--84.]
\end{center}

In the Jacobian sextic equation
\[(z - a)^6 - 4a(z - a)^5 + 16b(z - a)^3 - 4c(z - a) + 5b^2 - 4ac = 0,\]
if $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$, $E$, $F$ are the square roots (each with a determinate sign) of the roots $z_0$, $z_1$, $z_2$, $z_3$, $z_4$, $z_\infty$ of the equation, and if $\epsilon$ be an imaginary fifth root of unity, such that $\epsilon + \epsilon^4 - \epsilon^2 - \epsilon^3 = \sqrt{5}$, then we have between the square roots a system of three linear equations
\begin{align*}
0 &= -A\sqrt{5} + B + C + D + E + F, \\
0 &= \phantom{-A\sqrt{5} +}\, B + \epsilon^3 C + \epsilon D + \epsilon^4 E + \epsilon^2 F, \\
0 &= \phantom{-A\sqrt{5} +}\, B + \epsilon^2 C + \epsilon^4 D + \epsilon E + \epsilon^3 F,
\end{align*}
see Brioschi's \textit{Funzioni ellittiche} (Milan, 1880), third appendix, p.~402.

The sextic equation is irreducible, and there is thus nothing to distinguish any one square root from any other square root. But in the foregoing system of equations, $A$ is distinguished from the other square roots; hence the system must be one of 6~systems, wherein the place occupied by $A$ is occupied by $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$, $E$, $F$ respectively. Observe however that the letters being square roots, it may very well happen, and (as will appear) it does in fact happen, that the passage from the first to another system implies a change of sign in certain of the letters $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$, $E$, $F$.

The selection of one of the square roots as $= A$, does not determine which of the other square roots shall be $= B$, $C$, $D$, $E$, $F$ respectively: and in fact each system of equations may be represented in 10~different forms: viz. by multiplying the second and third equations by $\epsilon$, $\epsilon^2$, $\epsilon^3$, $\epsilon^4$ respectively, we obtain four new forms: we have thus five forms: and then in each of them transposing the second and third equations, we have five other forms: we have thus in all 10~forms.

Write
\begin{align*}
\Omega &= -A\sqrt{5} + B + C + D + E + F, \\
0 &= \phantom{-A\sqrt{5} +}\, B + \epsilon^3 C + \epsilon F + \epsilon D + \epsilon^4 E, \\
0 &= \phantom{-A\sqrt{5} +}\, B + \epsilon^2 C + \epsilon^4 F + \epsilon^3 D + \epsilon E,
\end{align*}
the 10~forms of the system are
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|l}
$A\ B\ C\ D\ E\ F$ & $A\ B\ F\ E\ D\ C$ \\
$A\ C\ D\ E\ F\ B$ & $A\ C\ B\ F\ E\ D$ \\
$A\ D\ E\ F\ B\ C$ & $A\ D\ C\ B\ F\ E$ \\
$A\ E\ F\ B\ C\ D$ & $A\ E\ D\ C\ B\ F$ \\
$A\ F\ B\ C\ D\ E$ & $A\ F\ E\ D\ C\ B$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
where observe that the five forms on the left are connected by cyclical substitutions in the last five letters; and that the forms in the same column are connected by two inversions of the last four letters.

It is hardly necessary to remark that $ABFEDC$ means the system of three equations similar to the original equations; and similarly that $ACDEFB$ means the system of three equations where the first equation is the same with the first equation, and the second and third equations only differ by a factor from the second and third equations respectively of the original form. And similarly as regards the others of the 10~forms.

I say that we have a second system $BCAFDE$ (where $\bar{D}$, $\bar{E}$ mean $D$, $E$ respectively): in fact, this denotes the system of three equations
\begin{align*}
0 &= -B\sqrt{5} + C + A + F - D - E, \\
0 &= \phantom{-B\sqrt{5} +}\, C + \epsilon^3 A + \epsilon^2 F - \epsilon D - \epsilon^4 E, \\
0 &= \phantom{-B\sqrt{5} +}\, C + \epsilon^2 A + \epsilon^4 F - \epsilon^3 D - \epsilon E,
\end{align*}
which are deducible from the original three equations.

\section*{868. On the Intersection of Curves}

\begin{center}
[From the \textit{Mathematische Annalen}, t.~xxx. (1887), pp.~85--90.]
\end{center}

It is only recently that I have studied Bacharach's paper ``Ueber den Cayley'schen Schnittpunktsatz,'' \textit{Math.~Ann.}, t.~xxvi. (1885), pp.~275--299: his theorem in regard to the case where the $\delta$~points lie on a curve of the order $\gamma - 3$ is a very interesting and valuable one, but I consider it rather as an addition than as a correction to my original theorem; and I cannot by any means agree that the method by counting of constants is to be rejected as not trustworthy; on the contrary, it seems to me to be the proper foundation of the whole theory; it must of course be employed with due consideration of special cases. I reproduce the theorem in what appears to me the complete form.

Writing with Bacharach
\[r \geqq m, \quad r \geqq n, \quad r \geqq m + n - 3, \quad \gamma = m + n - r, \quad \delta = \tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma - 1)(\gamma - 2),\]
and assuming $n \geqq m$, (these equations and inequalities are to be attended to throughout the present paper), I consider two curves of the orders $m$, $n$ respectively meeting in $\delta$~points $B$, and in $(mn - \delta)$ points $A$; and I state the theorem as follows:

$1^\circ$. The $mn - \delta$ points $A$ are in general such that a curve of the order $r$, which passes through $mn - \delta - 1$ of these points, does not of necessity pass through the remaining point; and in this case the general curve of the order $r$, which passes through the $mn - \delta$ points $A$, has for its form of equation
\[0 = L_{r-m}P_m + M_{r-n}Q_n,\]
where $P_m = 0$, $Q_n = 0$ are the equations of the given curves and $L_{r-m}$, $M_{r-n}$ denote functions of the orders $r - m$, $r - n$ respectively; and it thus appears that the curve of the order $r$ through the $mn - \delta$ points $A$ passes also through the $\delta$~points~$B$.
