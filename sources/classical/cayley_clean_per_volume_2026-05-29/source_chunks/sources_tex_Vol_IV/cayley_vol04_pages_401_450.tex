\textbf{16.} The last equation shows that $(a,..\!\qform{\xi}{\eta\zeta' - \eta'\zeta,\ \zeta\xi' - \zeta'\xi,\ \xi\eta' - \xi'\eta})^{2}$, considered as a function of $(\xi', \eta', \zeta')$, breaks up into factors. Or since the expression is not altered by interchanging $(\xi', \eta', \zeta')$ and $(\xi, \eta, \zeta)$, the same expression, considered as a function of $(\xi, \eta, \zeta)$, breaks up into factors. It is in fact easy to see that any quantic whatever, $(*\!\qform{\xi}{\eta\zeta' - \eta'\zeta,\ \zeta\xi' - \zeta'\xi,\ \xi\eta' - \xi'\eta})^{m}$, considered as a function of $(\xi, \eta, \zeta)$, breaks up into linear factors; for in virtue of the equation $\xi'(\eta\zeta' - \eta'\zeta) + \eta'(\zeta\xi' - \zeta'\xi) + \zeta'(\xi\eta' - \xi'\eta) = 0$, any one of the quantities $\eta\zeta' - \eta'\zeta$, $\zeta\xi' - \zeta'\xi$, $\xi\eta' - \xi'\eta$ can be expressed as a linear function of the other two; so that the quantic can be expressed as a linear function of any two of the three quantities; and \textit{qu\`a} homogeneous function of two quantities, it of course breaks up into factors, linear functions of these two quantities.

We may in all the formul\ae\ interchange $(x', y', z')$ and $(x, y, z)$, writing $(a', b', c', f', g', h')$ in the place of $(a, b, c, f, g, h)$.

\textbf{17.} Putting, in like manner,
\[
(A, B, C, F, G, H \!\qform{}{yz' - y'z,\ zx' - z'x,\ xy' - x'y})^{2}
= (\mfA, \mfB, \mfC, \mfF, \mfG, \mfH \!\qform{}{x', y', z'})^{2},
\]
so that
\begin{alignat*}{3}
\mfA &= \phantom{-}Cy^{2} + Bz^{2} - 2Fyz, &\qquad
\mfB &= \phantom{-}Az^{2} + Cx^{2} - 2Gzx, \\
\mfC &= \phantom{-}Bx^{2} + Ay^{2} - 2Hxy, \\
\mfF &= -Ayz - Fx^{2} + \phantom{-}Gxy + Hzx, \\
\mfG &= -Bzx + Fxy - \phantom{-}Gy^{2} + Hyz, \\
\mfH &= -Cxy + Fzx + \phantom{-}Gyz - Hz^{2},
\end{alignat*}
we obtain
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
\mfA, & \mfH, & \mfG \\
\mfH, & \mfB, & \mfF \\
\mfG, & \mfF, & \mfC
\end{vmatrix}
\!\qform{}{x, y, z} = (0, 0, 0),
\]
viz.
\[
\mfA x + \mfH y + \mfG z = 0,\ \&\mathrm{c}.
\]
Also
\begin{multline*}
(\mfB\mfC - \mfF^{2},\ \mfC\mfA - \mfG^{2},\ \mfA\mfB - \mfH^{2},\ \\
\mfG\mfH - \mfA\mfF,\ \mfH\mfF - \mfB\mfG,\ \mfF\mfG - \mfC\mfH) \\
= (x, y, z)^{2} \cdot K(a, b, c, f, g, h \!\qform{}{x, y, z})^{2};
\end{multline*}
that is
\[
\mfB\mfC - \mfF^{2} = x^{2}\, K(a, b, c, f, g, h \!\qform{}{x, y, z})^{2},\ \&\mathrm{c}.;
\]
whence also
\begin{align*}
(\mfB\mfC - \mfF^{2},..\!\qform{}{\lambda, \mu, \nu})^{2}
&= (\lambda x + \mu y + \nu z)^{2} \cdot K(a,..\!\qform{}{x, y, z})^{2},\\
(\mfB\mfC - \mfF^{2},..\!\qform{}{\lambda, \mu, \nu}\!\qform{}{\lambda', \mu', \nu'})
&= (\lambda x + \mu y + \nu z)(\lambda' x + \mu' y + \nu' z) \cdot K(a,..\!\qform{}{x, y, z})^{2};
\end{align*}
and moreover
\[
\mfA\mfB\mfC - \mfA\mfF^{2} - \mfB\mfG^{2} - \mfC\mfH^{2} + 2\mfF\mfG\mfH = 0.
\]

[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.~III. (1860),
pp.~16---22.]

Some very beautiful results in relation to the Wave Surface have been recently
obtained by Herr~Zech, in the Memoirs ``Die Eigenschaften der Wellenfl\"achen der
zweiaxigen Krystalle mittels der h\"ohern Geometrie abgeleitet,'' \textit{Crelle}, t.~LII.
pp.~243---254 (1856), and ``Die Kr\"ummungslinien der Wellenfl\"ache zweiaxiger
Krystalle,'' \textit{Crelle}, t.~LIV. pp.~72---77 (1857). According to the former of Fresnel's
two modes of generation, the wave surface is the envelope of a plane whose
perpendicular distance $v$ is a certain given function of the direction cosines
$l$, $m$, $n$. For the same system of direction cosines, there are in fact two
values of the perpendicular distance: call these $v$ and $w$, and let the
corresponding planes (parallel of course to each other) be called P and~Q.
Then the entire system of the planes P and~Q envelope the wave surface, viz.\ the
planes P may be considered as enveloping one sheet and the planes Q the other
sheet of the surface. But if instead of considering the entire system of planes we
consider only the planes P and the parallel planes Q, for which the perpendicular on
the plane P has a given constant value $v$, then the planes P will envelope a
developable F, and the planes Q will envelope a developable G, these two
developables F and~G respectively touching the wave surface along two series of
curves. And it is shown in the second of the Memoirs above referred to that these
curves are the curves of curvature of the wave surface.

The developable F is obtained as the envelope of the plane P, whose equation is
\[
\xi x + \eta y + \zeta z = v,
\]
where $v$ has a given constant value and $\xi$, $\eta$, $\zeta$ are parameters which vary,
subject to the two conditions
\[
\xi^{2} + \eta^{2} + \zeta^{2} = 1,
\]
\[
\frac{\xi^{2}}{a^{2} - v^{2}} + \frac{\eta^{2}}{b^{2} - v^{2}} + \frac{\zeta^{2}}{c^{2} - v^{2}} = 0.
\]

And in like manner the developable G is obtained as the envelope of the plane Q,
whose equation is
\[
\xi x + \eta y + \zeta z = w
= \frac{abc}{v}\sqrt{\frac{\xi^{2}}{a^{2}} + \frac{\eta^{2}}{b^{2}} + \frac{\zeta^{2}}{c^{2}}},
\]
where $w$ has a given constant value and $\xi$, $\eta$, $\zeta$ are parameters which vary,
subject to the two conditions
\[
\xi^{2} + \eta^{2} + \zeta^{2} = 1,
\]
\[
\frac{\xi^{2}}{a^{2} - w^{2}} + \frac{\eta^{2}}{b^{2} - w^{2}} + \frac{\zeta^{2}}{c^{2} - w^{2}} = 0.
\]

(It is hardly necessary to remark that if in the last-mentioned system of
equations, the parameter $w$ is also treated as variable, we obtain the wave surface:
in fact $w$, $v^{2}$ being the roots of the equation
\[
\frac{\xi^{2}}{a^{2} - \theta} + \frac{\eta^{2}}{b^{2} - \theta} + \frac{\zeta^{2}}{c^{2} - \theta} = 0,
\]
we have, attending to the condition $\xi^{2} + \eta^{2} + \zeta^{2} = 1$, the identical equation
\[
v^{2}w^{2} - (v^{2} + w^{2})\theta + \&\mathrm{c}. =
(\theta - a^{2})(\theta - b^{2})(\theta - c^{2})
\left(\frac{\xi^{2}}{a^{2} - \theta} + \frac{\eta^{2}}{b^{2} - \theta} + \frac{\zeta^{2}}{c^{2} - \theta}\right),
\]
and thence
\[
vw = abc\sqrt{\frac{\xi^{2}}{a^{2}} + \frac{\eta^{2}}{b^{2}} + \frac{\zeta^{2}}{c^{2}}},
\]
which shows that $vw = abc\sqrt{\frac{\xi^{2}}{a^{2}} + \frac{\eta^{2}}{b^{2}} + \frac{\zeta^{2}}{c^{2}}}$.)

or in other words, that the planes P and Q are respectively tangents to the conic or
infinitely thin surface of the second order, which is the second of the last-mentioned
cone by the plane at infinity. Moreover it is obvious from the same equations that
the plane P is a tangent plane of the sphere whose equation is
\[
x^{2} + y^{2} + z^{2} = v^{2},
\]
and that the plane Q is a tangent plane of the ellipsoid whose equation is
\[
\frac{a^{2}b^{2}c^{2}}{a^{2}x^{2} + b^{2}y^{2} + c^{2}z^{2}} = w^{2}.
\]

Hence each of the developables F and~G is the envelope of a plane which is the
common tangent plane of two surfaces of the second order; such developables are in
general of the eighth order, see my paper ``On the Developable Surfaces which arise
from Two Surfaces of the Second Order,'' \textit{Camb.\ and Dubl.\ Math.
Journ.}, t.~V. pp.~46---57 (1850), [84], and it will be presently seen that this is in
fact the order of the developables F and~G respectively.

The before-mentioned cone
\[
\frac{\xi^{2}}{a^{2} - v^{2}} + \frac{\eta^{2}}{b^{2} - v^{2}} + \frac{\zeta^{2}}{c^{2} - v^{2}} = 0
\]
(Zech's cone K) is a cone having for its focal lines the optic axes (or normals to
the circular sections) of the ellipsoid $a^{2}x^{2} + b^{2}y^{2} + c^{2}z^{2} = 1$ (Zech's ellipsoid~E)
which is used in the theory of the Wave Surface in the place of Fresnel's Surface
of Elasticity. The complementary cone
\[
(a^{2} - v^{2})x^{2} + (b^{2} - v^{2})y^{2} + (c^{2} - v^{2})z^{2} = 0
\]
is the cone which standing on the same ellipsoid~E has for its focal lines the optic
axes of the reciprocal ellipsoid $\frac{x^{2}}{a^{2}} + \frac{y^{2}}{b^{2}} + \frac{z^{2}}{c^{2}} = 1$.

To find the equation of the developable F, writing for shortness
\[
p = v^{2} - a^{2},\quad q = v^{2} - b^{2},\quad r = v^{2} - c^{2},
\]
then the system of equations is
\begin{align*}
\xi x + \eta y + \zeta z - v &= 0,\\
\xi^{2} + \eta^{2} + \zeta^{2} - 1 &= 0,\\
\frac{\xi^{2}}{p} + \frac{\eta^{2}}{q} + \frac{\zeta^{2}}{r} &= 0,
\end{align*}
treating them by the method of arbitrary multipliers in the usual manner, we obtain
\begin{align*}
x + 2\lambda\xi + \frac{2\mu\xi}{p} &= 0,\\
y + 2\lambda\eta + \frac{2\mu\eta}{q} &= 0,\\
z + 2\lambda\zeta + \frac{2\mu\zeta}{r} &= 0,
\end{align*}
equations which give in the first instance $v + p = 0$ or $p = -v$, and then substituting
this value,
\[
\xi = \frac{x(v^{2} - a^{2})}{2v\theta},\quad
\eta = \frac{y(v^{2} - b^{2})}{2v\theta},\quad
\zeta = \frac{z(v^{2} - c^{2})}{2v\theta},
\]
and thence
\begin{align*}
\frac{x^{2}(v^{2} - a^{2})}{4v^{2}\theta^{2}} + \frac{y^{2}(v^{2} - b^{2})}{4v^{2}\theta^{2}}
+ \frac{z^{2}(v^{2} - c^{2})}{4v^{2}\theta^{2}} &= 0,\\
\frac{x^{2}(v^{2} - a^{2})^{2}}{4v^{2}\theta^{2}} + \frac{y^{2}(v^{2} - b^{2})^{2}}{4v^{2}\theta^{2}}
+ \frac{z^{2}(v^{2} - c^{2})^{2}}{4v^{2}\theta^{2}} &= \theta,
\end{align*}
the latter of which is the derived equation of the former with respect to the parameter
$\theta$; hence writing the former equation under the form
\[
\{\theta + v(v^{2} - a^{2})\}\,\{\theta + v(v^{2} - b^{2})\}\,\{\theta + v(v^{2} - c^{2})\}
- \&\mathrm{c}. = 0,
\]
or what is the same thing
\[
(A, B, C, D\!\qform{}{\theta, 1})^{3} = 0,
\]
where
\begin{align*}
A &= 3v^{2},\\
B &= (v^{2} - a^{2})(v^{2} - b^{2}) + (v^{2} - b^{2})(v^{2} - c^{2}) + (v^{2} - c^{2})(v^{2} - a^{2}),\\
C &= (v^{2} - y^{2} - z^{2})(v^{2} - b^{2})(v^{2} - c^{2}) + (v^{2} - z^{2} - x^{2})(v^{2} - c^{2})(v^{2} - a^{2})\\
&\quad + (v^{2} - x^{2} - y^{2})(v^{2} - a^{2})(v^{2} - b^{2}),\\
D &= (v^{2} - a^{2})(v^{2} - b^{2})(v^{2} - c^{2}) - x^{2}(v^{2} - b^{2})(v^{2} - c^{2})
- y^{2}(v^{2} - c^{2})(v^{2} - a^{2})\\
&\quad - z^{2}(v^{2} - a^{2})(v^{2} - b^{2}),
\end{align*}
the equation of the developable F is
\[
(AD - BC)^{2} - 4(AC - B^{2})(BD - C^{2}) = 0.
\]

The investigation for the developable G is very similar to the preceding. Write for
shortness
\[
p' = \frac{1}{w^{2}} - \frac{1}{a^{2}},\quad
q' = \frac{1}{w^{2}} - \frac{1}{b^{2}},\quad
r' = \frac{1}{w^{2}} - \frac{1}{c^{2}},
\]
then the system of equations is
\begin{align*}
\xi x + \eta y + \zeta z &= w,\\
\xi^{2} + \eta^{2} + \zeta^{2} &= 1,\\
\frac{\xi^{2}}{a^{2} - w^{2}} + \frac{\eta^{2}}{b^{2} - w^{2}} + \frac{\zeta^{2}}{c^{2} - w^{2}} &= 0,
\end{align*}
and we then have
\[
\xi = \frac{x/a^{2}}{\lambda + \mu/w^{2} + \mu(v^{2} - a^{2})},\ \&\mathrm{c}.,
\]
equations which give $p = 0$, and substituting this value, we obtain
\[
\xi = \frac{x(w^{2} - a^{2})}{wA},\quad
\eta = \frac{y(w^{2} - b^{2})}{wA},\quad
\zeta = \frac{z(w^{2} - c^{2})}{wA},
\]
where $A$ is a parameter. Substituting these values in the equations
\[
\xi^{2} + \eta^{2} + \zeta^{2} = 1,
\]
\[
\frac{x^{2}(w^{2} - a^{2})}{w^{2}A^{2}} + \frac{y^{2}(w^{2} - b^{2})}{w^{2}A^{2}}
+ \frac{z^{2}(w^{2} - c^{2})}{w^{2}A^{2}} = 0,
\]
we find
\begin{align*}
\frac{x^{2}(w^{2} - a^{2})^{2}}{w^{2}A^{2}} + \frac{y^{2}(w^{2} - b^{2})^{2}}{w^{2}A^{2}}
+ \frac{z^{2}(w^{2} - c^{2})^{2}}{w^{2}A^{2}} &= A,\\
\frac{x^{2}(w^{2} - a^{2})}{w^{2}} + \frac{y^{2}(w^{2} - b^{2})}{w^{2}}
+ \frac{z^{2}(w^{2} - c^{2})}{w^{2}} &= A^{2} - (w^{2} - a^{2})(w^{2} - b^{2})(w^{2} - c^{2}),
\end{align*}
and multiplying the first equation by $-\frac{1}{w^{2}}$ and the second equation by $A^{2}$ and adding,
we obtain
\[
\frac{x^{2}(w^{2} - a^{2})}{a^{2}} + \frac{y^{2}(w^{2} - b^{2})}{b^{2}}
+ \frac{z^{2}(w^{2} - c^{2})}{c^{2}} = \frac{abc}{w},
\]
of which equation the latter of the foregoing two equations is the derived equation
with respect to the parameter~$A^{2}$.

Comparing this with the foregoing equation
\[
(AD - BC)^{2} - 4(AC - B^{2})(BD - C^{2}) = 0,
\]
we see that it is deduced from it by writing
\[
a^{2}x^{2},\ b^{2}y^{2},\ c^{2}z^{2},\ -\frac{1}{a^{2}},\ -\frac{1}{b^{2}},\ -\frac{1}{c^{2}},\ w^{2},\ A^{2}
\]
in the place of
\[
x^{2},\ y^{2},\ z^{2},\ v^{2} - a^{2},\ v^{2} - b^{2},\ v^{2} - c^{2},\ v^{2},\ \theta
\]
respectively, and the equation of the developable G is therefore
\[
(A'D' - B'C')^{2} - 4(A'C' - B'^{2})(B'D' - C'^{2}) = 0,
\]
where $A'$, $B'$, $C'$, $D'$ are what $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ become when
\[
x^{2},\ y^{2},\ z^{2},\ v^{2} - a^{2},\ v^{2} - b^{2},\ v^{2} - c^{2},\ v^{2},\ \theta
\]
are replaced by
\[
a^{2}x^{2},\ b^{2}y^{2},\ c^{2}z^{2},\ -\frac{1}{a^{2}},\ -\frac{1}{b^{2}},\ -\frac{1}{c^{2}},\ w^{2},\ A^{2}.
\]

The equations of the developables F and~G, although radically distinct from each
other, are consequently similar in form, and each is at once deducible from the other.

\section*{[278]}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{278. Note on the Singular Solutions of Differential Equations}
\begin{center}
{\large NOTE ON THE SINGULAR SOLUTIONS OF DIFFERENTIAL EQUATIONS.}
\end{center}
\markboth{NOTE ON THE SINGULAR SOLUTIONS OF DIFFERENTIAL EQUATIONS.}{NOTE ON THE SINGULAR SOLUTIONS OF DIFFERENTIAL EQUATIONS.}

[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.~III. (1860),
pp.~36, 37.]

The following investigation (which has been in my possession for a good many
years) affords I think a simple explanation of the theory of the singular solutions of
differential equations.

Let the primitive equation be
\[
c^{n} + Pc^{n-1} + Qc^{n-2} + \ldots = 0,
\]
where $c$ is the arbitrary constant and $P$, $Q$, \ldots\ are any functions of $x$, $y$; then the
differential equation is obtained by eliminating $c$ from the foregoing equation and the
derived equation
\[
n'c^{n-1} + Q'c^{n-2} + \ldots = 0,
\]
and the result may be represented by
\[
F(P, Q, \ldots,\ P', Q', \ldots) = 0.
\]

Assume now
\[
c^{n} + Pc^{n-1} + Qc^{n-2} + \ldots = (c + X)(c + Y)(c + Z)\ldots,
\]
then we have
\begin{align*}
P &= X + Y + Z + \&\mathrm{c}.,\\
Q &= XY + XZ + YZ + \&\mathrm{c}.,\\
&\ \;\vdots
\end{align*}
and consequently
\begin{align*}
P' &= X' + Y' + Z' + \&\mathrm{c}.,\\
Q' &= (Y + Z + \&\mathrm{c}.)X' + \&\mathrm{c}.,\\
&\ \;\vdots
\end{align*}

and substituting these values in the function $F(P, Q, \ldots,\ P', Q', \ldots)$ it is clear that we
shall have $F(P, Q, \ldots,\ P', Q', \ldots) = UX'Y'Z'\ldots$ where $U$ is a symmetrical function of
$X$, $Y$, $Z$, \&c., and therefore a function of $P$, $Q$, \ldots; and this equation will be identically
true whatever values we attribute to $X'$, $Y'$, $Z'$, \ldots, hence putting these quantities
respectively equal to unity, we have
\begin{align*}
P' &= n,\\
Q' &= (n - 1)P,\\
R' &= (n - 2)Q,\\
&\ \;\vdots
\end{align*}
and with these values
\[
U = F(P, Q, \ldots,\ n,\ (n - 1)P,\ \ldots),
\]
that is $U = 0$ is the result obtained by eliminating $c$ from the primitive equation and
the equation
\[
nc^{n-1} + (n - 1)Pc^{n-2} + \ldots = 0,
\]
which is the equation obtained by differentiating the primitive equation with respect to
the arbitrary constant $c$: that is, $U = 0$ being the singular solution, the differential
equation is
\[
UX'Y'Z'\ldots = 0.
\]

It is to be remarked that ($P$, $Q$, \&c.\ being rational and integral functions) then
if the roots $X$, $Y$, $Z$, \&c.\ are also rational and integral functions, the differential
equation contains $U^{n}$ as a separable rational and integral factor, but if the roots are
irrational then the differential equation does not really contain the rational and integral
factor $U$, but $X'Y'Z'\ldots$ is here a rational fraction containing $U$ in the denominator
and $UX'Y'Z'\ldots$ is an indecomposable rational and integral function. This is easily
verified \`a~\textit{posteriori} for a quadratic equation.

2, Stone Buildings, W.C., 28th January, 1858.
\end{flushright}

\section*{[279]}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{279. On a Theorem Relating to Spherical Conics}
\begin{center}
{\large ON A THEOREM RELATING TO SPHERICAL CONICS.}
\end{center}
\markboth{ON A THEOREM RELATING TO SPHERICAL CONICS.}{ON A THEOREM RELATING TO SPHERICAL CONICS.}

[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.~III. (1860),
p.~53.]

The following theorem was given by Prof.~MacCullagh: ``If three lines at right
angles to each other pass through a fixed point~$O$ so that two of them are confined
to given planes: the third line traces out a cone of the second order whose sections
parallel to the given planes are circles, and the plane containing the other two lines
envelopes a cone of the second order whose sections by planes parallel to the given
planes are parabolas.''

Referring the figure to the sphere we have a trirectangular triangle $XYZ$, of which
two angles $X$, $Y$ lie on fixed arcs $A$, $B$. The angle~$Z$ generates a spherical conic~$U$
having $A$, $B$ for its cyclic arcs. The side~$XY$ envelopes a spherical conic~$V$ touched
by the arcs $A$, $B$. The conic~$V$ is evidently the supplementary conic of~$U$, hence
the poles of $A$, $B$ are the foci of~$U$. We may drop altogether the consideration of
the triangle $XYZ$ and consider only the side~$XY$, we have then the theorem:
\textit{If a quadrantal arc $XY$ slides between the two fixed arcs $A$, $B$, the envelope of
$XY$ is a spherical conic~$U$ touched by the fixed arcs $A$, $B$, and which has for its
foci the poles of these same arcs $A$, $B$.}

It is worth while to notice the great reduction of order which takes place in
consequence of the arc~$XY$ being a quadrant. If $XY$ had been an arc of a given
magnitude~$\theta$, the envelope would have been a spherical curve of an order certainly
higher than~6. For considering the corresponding problem in piano, the envelope in
the particular case where the fixed lines $A$, $B$ are at right angles to each other
is a curve of the sixth order, and in the general case where the two fixed lines are
not at right angles the order is higher: the problem in piano corresponds of course,
not to the general problem on the sphere, but to that in which $\theta$ is indefinitely small.
