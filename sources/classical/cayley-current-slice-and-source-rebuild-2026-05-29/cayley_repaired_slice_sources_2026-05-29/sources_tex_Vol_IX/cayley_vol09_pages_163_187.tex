\textbf{34.} Next $n=5$. Here, starting from $\dfrac{1}{M}=\dfrac{v-u^3}{v(1-uv^3)}$,
the changes give
\[
\frac{1}{M}=\frac{v-u^3}{v(1-uv^3)},\quad
5M=\frac{u+v^3}{v(1+u^3 v)},\quad
\frac{v^3}{u^3 M}=1+\frac{2v}{u^3},\quad
\frac{u^3}{v^3}\,5M=1-\frac{2u}{v^3}\,;
\]
viz.\ the third and the fourth forms agree with the first and the second forms
respectively; that is, there are only two independent forms, and the elimination
of $M$ from these gives
\[
5uv\,(1-uv^3)(1+u^3 v) - (v-u^3)(u+v^3) = 0,
\]
which is a form of the modular equation.

\textbf{35.} In the case $n=7$, starting from $\dfrac{1}{M}=\dfrac{-7u\,(1-uv)(1-uv+u^2 v^2)}{u-v^3}$
(as to this see \emph{post}, No.~43), the forms are
\begin{align*}
\frac{1}{M} &= \frac{-7u\,(1-uv)(1-uv+u^2 v^2)}{u-v^3}\quad\ldots(1),\\[4pt]
-7M &= \frac{-7v\,(1-uv)(1-uv+u^2 v^2)}{v-u^3}\quad\ldots(2),\\[4pt]
\frac{v^3}{u^3 M} &= \frac{-7v^3(1-uv)(1-uv+u^2 v^2)}{u^3(v-u^3)}\quad\ldots(3),\\[4pt]
-\frac{u^3}{v^3}\,7M &= \frac{-7u^3(1-uv)(1-uv+u^2 v^2)}{v^3(v-u^3)}\quad\ldots(4)\,;
\end{align*}
so that here again the third and the fourth forms are identical with the second
and the third forms respectively; there are thus only two forms, and the
elimination of $M$ gives
\[
(u-v^3)(v-u^3) - 4uv\,(1-uv)(1-uv+u^2 v^2)^2 = 0,
\]
which is a form of the modular equation.

\textbf{36.} If in the foregoing equation
\[
F'v\cdot\frac{1}{M}=R(u,\,v),
\]
we make the change $u,\,v,\,\frac{1}{M}$ into $v,\,\pm u,\,\pm nM$, it becomes
\[
\pm F'u\cdot nM = R(v,\,\pm u)\,;
\]
combining these equations, we have
\[
\pm nM^2\cdot\frac{F'u}{F'v}=\frac{R(v,\,\pm u)}{R(u,\,v)}\,;
\]
or, substituting herein the foregoing value
\[
M^2=-\frac{1}{n}\,\frac{(1-v^3)\,vF'v}{(1-u^3)\,uF'u}\,,
\]
this becomes
\[
\mp\frac{v\,(1-v^3)}{u\,(1-u^3)}=\frac{R(v,\,\pm u)}{R(u,\,v)}
\quad
\begin{array}{l}+ \text{ for } n\equiv 3 \text{ or } 5\ (\modd.\ 8),\\
- \text{ for } n\equiv 1 \text{ or } 7\ (\modd.\ 8),\end{array}
\]
which must agree with the modular equation. Thus in the last-mentioned case $n=3$,
we have
\[
\tfrac{1}{3}\,F'v\cdot\tfrac{1}{M}=3\,(v^2 u^2+2u^3 v+1)\,u,
\]
or, say
\[
R(u,\,v) = (v^2 u^2 + 2u^3 v + 1)\,u,
\]
and therefore
\[
R(v,\,-u) = (v^2 u^2 - 2u v^3 + 1)\,v\,;
\]
the equation is
\[
+\frac{v\,(1-v^3)}{u\,(1-u^3)} = \frac{(v^2 u^2 - 2u v^3 + 1)\,v}{(v^2 u^2 + 2u^3 v + 1)\,u}\,,
\]
which is right; because Jacobi, p.~82, [\textit{Ges.\ Werke}, t.~\textsc{i}, p.~137], for
the modular equation, gives
\[
1 - u^3 = (1 - u^3 v^3)(v^2 u^2 + 2u^3 v + 1),\quad
1 - v^3 = (1 - u^3 v^3)(v^2 u^2 - 2u v^3 + 1).
\]
Observe that the general equation
\[
\mp\frac{v\,(1-v^3)}{u\,(1-u^3)} = \frac{R(v,\,\pm u)}{R(u,\,v)}
\]
no longer contains the functions $F'v,\,F'u$, which enter into Jacobi's
expression of $M^2$.

\noindent\textbf{\textit{Theorem in connexion with the multiplication of elliptic functions.}\quad
Art.\ Nos.\ 37 to 40.}

\medskip

\textbf{37.} The theory of multiplication gives an important theorem in regard to
transformation. Starting with the white transformation
\[
\frac{1-y}{1+y} = \frac{1-x}{1+x}\,\frac{\bigl(\alpha'-\beta x+\gamma x^2-\ldots\bigr)^2}{\bigl(\alpha+\beta x+\gamma x^2+\ldots\bigr)^2}\,,
\quad
= \frac{1-x}{1+x}\,\biggl(\frac{P-Qx}{P+Qx}\biggr)^{\!2},
\]
we may form a like transformation,
\[
\frac{1-z}{1+z} = \frac{1-y}{1+y}\,\frac{\bigl(\alpha'-\beta' y+\gamma' y^2-\ldots\bigr)^2}{\bigl(\alpha+\beta' y+\gamma' y^2+\ldots\bigr)^2}\,,
\quad
= \frac{1-y}{1+y}\,\biggl(\frac{P'-Q' y}{P'+Q' y}\biggr)^{\!2}\!,
\]
such that the combination of the two gives a multiplication, viz.\ for the relation
between $y,\,z$, deriving $w$ from $z$ as $v$ from $u$, we have $w=u$; and instead of $M$ we
have $M'$, $=\pm\dfrac{1}{nM}$; that is, we have
\[
\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2\cdot 1 - u^2 x^2}} = \frac{Mdy}{\sqrt{1-y^2\cdot 1 - v^2 y^2}}\,,
\]
\[
\frac{dy}{\sqrt{1-y^2\cdot 1 - v^2 y^2}} = \frac{M'dz}{\sqrt{1-z^2\cdot 1 - w^2 z^2}}\,,
\]
and thence
\[
\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2\cdot 1 - u^2 x^2}} = \frac{\pm\,\dfrac{1}{n}\,dz}{\sqrt{1-z^2\cdot 1 - u^2 z^2}}\,;
\]
or, writing $x=\sin\theta$, we have $z=\pm\sin n\theta$; $\pm$ is here $(-)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}$,
viz.\ it is $-$ for $n\equiv 3$ or $7\ (\modd.\ 8)$, and $+$ for $n\equiv 1$ or $5\ (\modd.\ 8)$.

Now in part effecting the substitution, we have
\[
\frac{1-z}{1+z} = \frac{1-x}{1+x}\,\biggl(\frac{P-Qx}{P+Qx}\biggr)^{\!2}\biggl(\frac{P'-Q' y}{P'+Q' y}\biggr)^{\!2},
\]
where $y$ denotes its value in terms of $x$.

And from the theory of elliptic functions, replacing $\sin n\theta$, $\sin\theta$
by their values $\pm z$, $x$, we have an equation
\[
\frac{1-z}{1+z} = \frac{1-x}{1+x}\,\frac{\bigl(A-Bx+Cx^2-\ldots\bigr)^2}{\bigl(A+Bx+Cx^2+\ldots\bigr)^2}\,,
\]
where $A-Bx+Cx^2-\ldots,\;A+Bx+Cx^2+\ldots$ are given functions each of the order
$\tfrac{1}{2}(n^2-1)$; viz.\ the coefficients are given functions of $k$, or, what is the
same thing, of $u$. Comparing the two results, we see that in the sthic transformation
the sought-for function, $\alpha+\beta x+\gamma x^2+\ldots$ of the order $\tfrac{1}{2}(n-1)$, is a factor of a given function
$A+Bx+Cx^2+\ldots$ of the order $\tfrac{1}{2}(n^2-1)$.

\medskip

\textbf{38.} Considering the modular equation as known, then by what precedes we have
\[
\alpha+\beta x+\gamma x^2+\ldots = \alpha\biggl\{1+\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\,x+\ldots+\frac{u^m}{v}\,x^{\frac{1}{2}(n-1)}\biggr\}\,;
\]
that is, the given function $A+Bx+Cx^2+\ldots$ has a factor $1+\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\,x+\ldots+\dfrac{u^m}{v}\,x^{\frac{1}{2}(n-1)}$,
of which one (the last) coefficient $\dfrac{u^m}{v}$ is known, and we are hence able theoretically to

\bigskip

\medskip

\noindent
determine all the other coefficients rationally in terms of $u,\,v$; that is, the
modular equation being known, we can theoretically complete the solution of the
transformation problem. I do not, however, see the way to obtaining a convenient
solution in this manner.

\textbf{39.} The formula in question for $n=3$ is
\[
\frac{1+\sin 3\theta}{1-\sin 3\theta} = \frac{1-\sin\theta}{1+\sin\theta}\,\frac{(1+2\sin\theta-2k^2\sin^3\theta-k^2\sin^4\theta)^2}{(1-2\sin\theta+2k^2\sin^3\theta-k^2\sin^4\theta)^2}\,,
\]
which, putting therein $x=\sin\theta$, $z=-\sin 3\theta$, and replacing $k$ by $u^3$, may be written
\[
1 + z\,(+) = (1+x)(1 - 2x + 2u^3 x^3 - u^4 x^4)^2\,(+),
\]
where the signs (+) indicate denominators which are obtained from the numerators by
changing the signs of $x,\,z$ respectively.

The theorem in regard to $n=3$ thus is, $1+\dfrac{u^3}{v}\,x$ is a factor of
$1 - 2x + 2u^3 x^3 - u^4 x^4$; viz.\ writing in the last-mentioned function $x=-\dfrac{v}{u^3}$,
we ought to have
\[
0 = 1 + 2\,\frac{v}{u^3} - 2\,\frac{v^3}{u^6} - \frac{v^4}{u^4}\,,
\]
that is,
\[
u^4 + 2uv - 2u^3 v^3 - v^4 = 0,
\]
which is in fact the modular equation.

\textbf{41.} The cubic transformation, $n=3$.

I reproduce the results already obtained; since there are only two coefficients
$\alpha,\,\beta$, these are also the last but one and last coefficients $\rho,\,\sigma$.
Hence, from the values of $\alpha,\,\beta,\,\rho,\,\sigma$, we have
\[
\alpha = 1,\qquad 2\alpha = \tfrac{v^2}{u}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{M}-\tfrac{u^3}{v^3}\right),
\]
\[
2\beta = \tfrac{1}{M} - 1,\qquad \beta = \tfrac{u^3}{v}\,;
\]
the two values of $\dfrac{1}{M}$ are thus $\dfrac{1}{M}=2\,\dfrac{u^3}{v}+\dfrac{v^3}{u^3}\,, =1+\dfrac{2u^3}{v}$,
giving the modular equation
\[
v^4 + 2v^3 u^3 - 2uv - u^4 = 0,
\]
and we then have
\[
\frac{1-y}{1+y} = \frac{1-x}{1+x}\,\biggl(\frac{v-u^3 x^2}{v+u^3 x^2}\biggr)^{\!2}.
\]

\textbf{42.} The quintic transformation, $n=5$.

Here there are the three coefficients $\alpha,\,\beta,\,\gamma$, or $\beta,\,\gamma$
are the last but one and last coefficients $\rho,\,\sigma$; we have
\[
\alpha = 1,\qquad 2\beta = v^2 u\!\left(\frac{1}{M}-\frac{u^5}{v^5}\right),
\]
\[
2\beta = \frac{1}{M} - 1,\qquad \gamma = \frac{u^2}{v}\,.
\]
Comparing the two values of $\beta$, we have
\[
\frac{1}{M} = \frac{v - u^5}{v\,(1-v^4 u)}\,, \quad\text{and then}\quad
\alpha = 1,\quad 2\beta = \frac{u\,(v^2 - u^4)}{v\,(1-v^4 u)},\quad \gamma = \frac{u^2}{v}\,,
\]
so that only the modular equation remains to be determined.

The unused equation is
\[
2\alpha\gamma + 2\alpha\beta + \beta^2 = \frac{v^2}{u^2}\,(2\alpha\gamma + 2\beta\gamma + \beta^2),
\]
which, putting therein $\alpha = 1$, may be written
\[
2u v^2\,(u^2 - v^2) = 2\beta\,(\gamma v^2 - u^2)\,;
\]
attending to the value of $\beta$, this divides by $v^2 - u^2$; in fact the equation
may be written
\[
2v + \beta^2 = -\frac{u}{v\,(v^2 - u^4)}\,(\gamma v^2 - u^2)\,;
\]
and then completing the substitution, and integralizing, this becomes
\[
[8m^2(1-v^4 u)^2 + (v^2 - u^4)^2] = 4uv\,(u^4 + v^2)\,(1 - u^4 v)\,(1-uv^4)\,,
\]
viz.\ this is
\[
4\,(1 - v^4 u)\,uv\,[2u^4 (1 - v^4 u) - (u^4 + v^2)\,(1 - uv^4)] + (v^2 - u^4)^2 = 0\,;
\]
and the term in $[\ ]$ being $=-(v^2 - u^4)\,(1 + uv^3)$, the whole again divides by
$v^2 - u^4$, and the equation thus becomes
\[
(v^2 + u^4)\,(v^2 - u^4) - 4uv\,(1 - v^4 u)\,(1 + uv^3) = 0,
\]
which is the modular equation.

\textbf{43.} The septic transformation, $n=7$.

I do not propose to complete the solution directly from the fundamental equations
for $\alpha,\,\beta,\,\gamma,\,\delta$, but resort to the known modular equation, and to an
expression of $M$ which I obtain by means thereof.

The modular equation is
\[
(1 - u^7)\,(1 - v^7) - (1 - uv)^8 = 0,
\]

\bigskip

\noindent\textbf{[Book p.~149]}

\medskip

\noindent
which may also be written
\[
(v - u^7)(u - v^7) + 7uv\,(1 - uv)\,(1 - uv + u^2 v^2)^2 = 0,
\]
as can be at once verified; but it also follows from Cauchy's identity
\[
(x + y)^7 - x^7 - y^7 = 7xy\,(x+y)(x^2 + xy + y^2)^2.
\]
We then have
\[
M = -\frac{1}{n}\,\frac{(1 - v^4)\,vF'v}{(1 - u^4)\,uF'u}
= -\frac{v^4}{1 - u^4}\,(1 - uv)^2\,,
\]
\[
= -\frac{u^4}{1 - u^4}\,u\,(1 - v^4)^2\,.
\]
Moreover
\[
uF'u = -u^4\,(1 - v^4) + uv\,(1 - uv)^2
= -\frac{u^4}{1 - u^4}\,(1 - uv)^2 + uv\,(1 - uv)^2
\]
\[
= \frac{(1 - uv)^2}{1 - u^4}\,u\,(v - u^7)\,;
\]
and similarly
\[
F'v = \frac{(1 - uv)^2}{1 - v^4}\,v\,(u - v^7)\,;
\]
whence
\[
\frac{1}{M} = \frac{-7u\,(v - u^7)}{u - v^7}.
\]
Writing this under the form
\[
\frac{1}{M^4} = \frac{-7uv\,(v - u^7)\,(u - v^7)}{(u - v^7)^2}
= \frac{49u^2\,(1 - uv)^4\,(1 - uv + u^2 v^2)^4}{(u - v^7)^2},
\]
I find, as will appear, that the root must be taken with the sign $-$, and that we
thus have
\[
\frac{1}{M} = \frac{7u\,(1 - uv)(1 - uv + u^2 v^2)}{u - v^7},\quad\text{whence also } \frac{M}{1} = \frac{u\,(1 - uv)(1 - uv + u^2 v^2)}{u - v^7}.
\]

\noindent\textit{The general theory by $q$-transcendents.\quad Art.\ Nos.\ 52 to 71.}

\medskip

\textbf{52.} I recur to the formula
\[
\frac{1-y}{1+y} = \frac{1-x}{1+x}\,\biggl(\frac{\alpha-\beta x+\gamma x^2-\ldots\pm \sigma x^{\frac{1}{2}(n-1)}}{\alpha+\beta x+\gamma x^2+\ldots+\sigma x^{\frac{1}{2}(n-1)}}\biggr)^{\!2}\,,
\]
and seek to express the ratios $\alpha:\beta:\ldots:\sigma$ in terms of $q$. Writing with Jacobi
\[
\omega = \frac{mK + m'iK'}{n}\,,
\]
we have in general
\[
\alpha + \beta x + \gamma x^2 + \ldots + \sigma x^{\frac{1}{2}(n-1)} = \alpha\!\left(1+\frac{x}{\sin 2\omega}\right)\!\left(1+\frac{x}{\sin 4\omega}\right)\ldots\!\left(1+\frac{x}{\sin(n-1)\omega}\right);
\]
\[
(\sin\omega = \sin\omega\ \text{am};\ \text{viz.\ sine } 2\omega = \sin\,(K-2\omega),\ \text{etc.});
\]
and the values of $\alpha,\,\beta,\,\ldots,\,\theta$ which correspond to the moduli $v,\,u,\,\ldots,\,v_*$ or
say the values $(\alpha,\,\beta_1,\,\ldots,\,\theta_1)$, $(\alpha,\,\beta_2,\,\ldots,\,\theta_2)$, $(\alpha_n,\,\beta_n,\,\ldots,\,\theta_n)$
are obtained by giving to $\omega$ the values
\[
\omega_1,\quad \omega_2,\quad \omega_3,\quad \ldots,\quad \omega_n,
\]
\[
=\frac{2K}{n}\,,\quad \frac{2K + iK'}{n}\,,\quad \frac{4K + iK'}{n}\,,\quad \ldots,\quad \frac{iK'}{n}\,,
\]
viz.\ the cases $\omega_1,\,\omega_n$ correspond to Jacobi's first and second real transformations,
and the others to the imaginary transformations.

I remark that $\omega=\omega_1$ gives for $\sin 2p\omega$ an expression which is rational as
regards $q$, but $\omega=\omega_n$ gives an expression involving $q^{\frac{1}{n}}$, the real $n$th root of $q$;
the other values $\omega_2,\,\omega_3,\,\ldots$ give the like expressions, involving $\alpha q^{\frac{1}{n}},\,\alpha^2 q^{\frac{1}{n}},\,\ldots$
($\alpha$ an imaginary $n$th root of unity, the imaginary $n$th roots of $q$).
