\noindent 4. For the cubic equation $(a, b, c, d \Cdel v, 1)^3$ the equation for $\theta\,(=\zeta^2(\alpha, \beta))$ is, $0 =$
\[

\noindent 5. For the quartic equation $(a, b, c, d, e \Cdel v, 1)^4 = 0$;
\[
   \theta = \zeta^2(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) = -(\alpha - \beta)(\alpha - \gamma)(\beta - \gamma).
\]
The roots are
\[
\begin{aligned}
   \theta_1 &=  \zeta^4(\beta, \gamma, \delta),\\
   \theta_2 &= -\zeta^4(\gamma, \delta, \alpha),\\
   \theta_3 &=  \zeta^4(\delta, \alpha, \beta),\\
   \theta_4 &= -\zeta^4(\alpha, \beta, \gamma),
\end{aligned}
\]
the signs being in this case (and indeed for an equation of any even order) alternately positive and negative; in fact, if the equation is represented by $\varphi v = 0$, then the roots divided by $\zeta^2(\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta)$ should respectively be $\varphi'\alpha,\ \varphi'\beta,\ \varphi'\gamma,\ \varphi'\delta$, and this will be the case if the signs are taken as above.

\medskip

\noindent 6. Putting now $\delta = 0$ (and therefore $e = 0$) the roots become
\[
\begin{aligned}
   \theta_1 &=  \beta\gamma\,(\beta - \gamma),\\
   \theta_2 &=  \gamma\alpha\,(\gamma - \alpha),\\
   \theta_3 &= \zeta^4(\delta, \alpha, \beta) \;\;\text{(see text)},\\
   \theta_4 &= -\zeta^4(\alpha, \beta, \gamma),
\end{aligned}
\]
where $(\alpha, \beta, \gamma)$ are the roots of $(a, b, c, d \Cdel v, 1)^3 = 0$. Let $\square$ denote the discriminant of the cubic function, then $\zeta^4(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) = -\,\tfrac{1}{a^2}\sqrt{-\,\square}$, and we have thus the linear equation; the cubic equation is
\[
   \Pi_1\bigl(\theta - \beta\gamma(\beta - \gamma)\bigr) = 0,
\]
the coefficients of which can be calculated by the method of symmetric functions (see Annex No.~1).

\noindent 7. The cubic equation being thus obtained, we have the two equations
\[
\begin{aligned}
   & \theta^3\,a^2 + \theta^2\cdot 0 + \theta\,(3ac - b^2) + c\sqrt{-\,\square} = 0,\\[2pt]

\[
\theta^6\cdot a^4 + \theta^5\cdot 0 + \theta^4\,a^2\bigl(-9a^2 d^2 + 4abcd - b^2 d + \square\bigr) + \theta^3\cdot a^2\bigl(-8a^2 d + 4abc - b^3\bigr) d \sqrt{-\,\square} + \cdots = 0,

\noindent 15. As a verification of this result, I remark that, taking for the quintic equation $v^5 + v^4 + v^3 + v^2 + v + 1 = 0$, the roots of this equation are $-1, \omega, \omega^2, -\omega, -\omega^2$, where $\omega$ is an imaginary cube root of unity ($\omega^2 + \omega + 1 = 0$). We ought to have $\zeta^4(\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta, \varepsilon) = -\sqrt{\square} = -36$; and this will be the case if, for instance, $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta, \varepsilon$ are respectively $-1, \omega, \omega^2, -\omega^2, -\omega$. We have then
\[
\begin{aligned}
\zeta^4(\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta) &= -1-\omega\cdot-1-\omega^2\cdot-1+\omega^2\cdot\omega-\omega^2\cdot\omega+\omega^2\cdot 2\omega^2 = 6,\\
\zeta^4(\beta, \gamma, \delta, \varepsilon) &= \omega-\omega^2\cdot\omega+\omega^2\cdot 2\omega\cdot 2\omega^2\cdot\omega^2+\omega\cdot-\omega^2+\omega = -12,\\
\zeta^4(\gamma, \delta, \varepsilon, \alpha) &= 2\omega^2\cdot\omega+\omega\cdot\omega^2+1\cdot-\omega^2-\omega\cdot-\omega^2+1\cdot-\omega+1 = +6\,(\omega - \omega^2),\\
\zeta^4(\delta, \varepsilon, \alpha, \beta) &= -\omega^2+\omega\cdot-\omega^2+1\cdot-\omega^2-\omega\cdot-\omega+1\cdot-2\omega\cdot-1-\omega = -6\,(\omega - \omega^2),\\
\zeta^4(\varepsilon, \alpha, \beta, \gamma) &= -\omega+1\cdot-2\omega\cdot-\omega-\omega^2\cdot-1-\omega\cdot-1-\omega\cdot\omega-\omega^2 = 6.
\end{aligned}
\]

\noindent 16. For the cubic, if the assumed equation is $v^3 + v^2 + v + 1 = 0$, the roots whereof are $-1, i, -i$ ($i^2 = -1$), then we should have $\zeta^4(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) = \sqrt{-\,\square} = 4i$, which will be the case if $\alpha, \beta, \gamma = -1, i, -i$ respectively, and the roots $\beta - \gamma,\ \gamma - \alpha,\ \alpha - \beta$ of the equation in $\theta$ then are $2i,\ -i + 1,\ -i - 1$, so that the equation in $\theta$ is $(\theta^2 + 2i\theta - 2)(\theta - 2i) = 0$, or
\[
   (1, 0, 2, -4i \Cdel \theta, 1)^3 = 0,
\]
which (observing that $\sqrt{-\,\square} = 4i$) is what the formula for the equation in $\theta$ becomes for the equation $(1, 1, 1, 1 \Cdel v, 1)^3 = 0$.

\medskip

\noindent 17. For the quartic equation, taking this to be $v^4 + v^3 + v^2 + v + 1 = 0$, the roots are $\omega, \omega^2, \omega^3, \omega^4$, where $\omega$ is an imaginary fifth root of unity ($\omega^4 + \omega^3 + \omega^2 + \omega + 1 = 0$), and putting $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta$ equal to $\omega, \omega^2, \omega^3, \omega^4$ respectively, we have
\[
   -\sqrt{\square} = \zeta^4(\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta) = -5\,(\omega + \omega^4 - \omega^2 - \omega^3),
\]
giving, as it should do, $\square = 125$. The equation in $\theta$ is therefore by the formula
\[
   \bigl(1, 0, 0, -25(\omega + \omega^4 - \omega^2 - \omega^3),\ 125\,? \Cdel \theta, 1\bigr)^4 = 0.
\]
But the roots are
\[
\begin{aligned}
\theta_1 &= \zeta^4(\beta, \gamma, \delta) = -\,\omega^2 - \omega^3\cdot\omega^2 - \omega^4\cdot\omega^3 - \omega^4 = 2 - \omega + \omega^2 - 2\omega^3 = 2 - X,\\
\theta_2 &= -\zeta^4(\gamma, \delta, \alpha) = -\,\omega^3 - \omega^4\cdot\omega^3 - \omega\cdot\omega^4 - \omega = -1 + 3\omega + 2\omega^2 + \omega^3 = -1 + Y,\\
\theta_3 &= \zeta^4(\delta, \alpha, \beta) = \omega^4 - \omega\cdot\omega^4 - \omega^2\cdot\omega - \omega^2 = -4 - 3\omega - 2\omega^2 - \omega^3 = -4 - Y,\\
\theta_4 &= -\zeta^4(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) = -\,\omega - \omega^2\cdot\omega - \omega^3\cdot\omega^2 - \omega^3 = 3 + \omega - \omega^2 + 2\omega^3 = 3 + X,
\end{aligned}
\]
if, for shortness,
\[
   X = \omega - \omega^2 + 2\omega^3,\qquad Y = 3\omega + 2\omega^2 + \omega^3.
\]
The equation in $\theta$ is therefore
\[
   (\theta - 2 + X)(\theta + 1 - Y)(\theta + 4 + Y)(\theta - 3 - X) = 0,
\]
where the left-hand side is the product of the factors, and
\[
\begin{aligned}
   (\theta - 2 + X)(\theta - 3 - X) &= \theta^2 - 5\theta + 6 - X - X^2 = \theta^2 - 5\theta + 10 - 5\,(\omega + \omega^4),\\
   (\theta + 1 - Y)(\theta + 4 + Y) &= \theta^2 + 5\theta + 4 - 3Y - Y^2 = \theta^2 + 5\theta + 10 - 5\,(\omega^2 + \omega^3),
\end{aligned}
\]
and the equation in $\theta$ is, therefore, as it should be,
\[
   \bigl(1, 0, 0, -25(\omega + \omega^4 - \omega^2 - \omega^3),\ 125\,? \Cdel \theta, 1\bigr)^4 = 0.
\]

\medskip

\noindent The Porism referred to is as follows, viz.\ two conics may be so related to each other, that a polygon may be inscribed in the one, and circumscribed about the other conic, in such manner that any point whatever of the circumscribing conic may be taken for a vertex of the polygon. \]
where $m$ is an arbitrary parameter. Suppose that the conic touches a given line, we have for the determination of $m$ a quadratic equation; and conversely, if the roots of this quadratic equation are given, the line is also given; that is, the two roots may be considered as parameters which determine the particular line.

\medskip

\noindent Let $k$ be a given value of $m$; the parameters of any tangent of the conic $U + kV = 0$ are $k, p$, but as $k$ is common to all the tangents, we may consider the particular tangent as determined by the single parameter $p$. And a point of the conic $U + kV = 0$ may be considered as determined by the same parameter $p$ which determines the tangent at that point.

\medskip

\noindent As regards the conic $V = 0$, the common parameter for all its tangents is $\infty$, and we may consider any other tangent of this conic as determined by the parameter $\theta$, and a point of the conic as determined by the same parameter $\theta$.

\medskip

\noindent Suppose, in the first instance, that the two conics are
\[
\begin{aligned}
   U &= a x^2 + b y^2 + c z^2 = 0,\\
   V &= x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 0;
\end{aligned}
\]
the equation of the tangent of $U + kV = 0$, the parameter whereof is $p$ (in fact a common tangent of the conics $U + kV = 0$, $U + pV = 0$), is easily found to be
\[
   x\,\sqrt{b - c}\,\sqrt{a + k}\,\sqrt{a + p}\;+\;y\,\sqrt{c - a}\,\sqrt{b + k}\,\sqrt{b + p}\;+\;z\,\sqrt{a - b}\,\sqrt{c + k}\,\sqrt{c + p}\;=\;0;
\]
and if this meet the conic $V = 0$ in the points $P, P'$, the parameters whereof are $k, \theta$ and $k, \theta'$, or say $\theta$ and $\theta'$ respectively, then the coordinates of the point $P$ are given by
\[
   x : y : z = \sqrt{b - c}\,\sqrt{a + \theta} \;:\; \sqrt{c - a}\,\sqrt{b + \theta} \;:\; \sqrt{a - b}\,\sqrt{c + \theta};
\]
and substituting these values in the foregoing equation, we have
\[
   (b - c)\,\sqrt{a + k}\,\sqrt{a + p}\,\sqrt{a + \theta} + (c - a)\,\sqrt{b + k}\,\sqrt{b + p}\,\sqrt{b + \theta} + (a - b)\,\sqrt{c + k}\,\sqrt{c + p}\,\sqrt{c + \theta} = 0,
\]
as an equation connecting the parameters $p$ and $\theta$. This equation may be replaced by
\[
\begin{aligned}
   \sqrt{(a + k)(a + p)(a + \theta)} &= \lambda + \mu a,\\
   \sqrt{(b + k)(b + p)(b + \theta)} &= \lambda + \mu b,\\
   \sqrt{(c + k)(c + p)(c + \theta)} &= \lambda + \mu c.
\end{aligned}
\]

\medskip

\noindent from which $\lambda, \mu$ are to be eliminated; and squaring and reducing, we have
\[
\begin{aligned}
   \lambda^2 &= a b c + k p\theta,\\
   -\,2\lambda\mu &= b c + c a + a b - (p\theta + k p - k\theta),

and thence
\[
   (b c + c a + a b - p\theta - k p - k\theta)^2 - 4\,(a + b + c + k + p + \theta)(a b c + k p\theta) = 0
\]
as the rational form of the original equation. But the same rational equation would, it is clear, be obtained from the system
\[
\begin{aligned}
   \sqrt{(k + a)(k + b)(k + c)} &= L + M k,\\
   \sqrt{(p + a)(p + b)(p + c)} &= L + M p,\\
   \sqrt{(\theta + a)(\theta + b)(\theta + c)} &= L + M \theta,
\end{aligned}
\]
by the elimination of $L$ and $M$. And it follows from Abel's theorem (but the result might be verified by means of Euler's fundamental theorem for the addition of elliptic functions), that if
\[
   \Pi\xi = \int\!\frac{d\xi}{\sqrt{(\xi + a)(\xi + b)(\xi + c)}},
\]
then the last-mentioned system is equivalent to the transcendental equation
\[
   \Pi\theta = \Pi p \pm \Pi k,
\]
in which the arbitrary constant which should have been inserted, and the sign of $\Pi k$, are determined by the consideration that for $k = \infty$ (which gives $\Pi k = 0$) we ought to have $\theta = p$, and therefore $\Pi\theta = \Pi p$.

\medskip

\noindent There is of course a similar equation in $\theta'$, and the terms with $\Pi k$ must be taken with opposite signs, and we have thence the theorem:

\medskip

\noindent ``If $\theta, \theta'$ are the parameters of the points $P, P'$ in which the conic $V = 0$ is intersected by the tangent, the parameter whereof is $p$, of the conic $U + kV = 0$, then the equations
\[
\begin{aligned}
   \Pi\theta  &= \Pi p - \Pi k,\\
   \Pi\theta' &= \Pi p + \Pi k,
\end{aligned}
\]
determine the parameters $\theta, \theta'$ of the points in question.'' And again:

\medskip

\noindent ``If the two variable parameters $\theta, \theta'$ are connected by the equation
\[
   \Pi\theta' - \Pi\theta = 2\Pi k,
\]
then the line $P P'$ will be a tangent of the conic $U + kV = 0$.''

\medskip

\noindent The foregoing demonstration relates to the particular forms $U = a x^2 + b y^2 + c z^2$, $V = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$; but observing that the function $\sqrt{(\xi + a)(\xi + b)(\xi + c)}$, which enters under the integral sign in the transcendental function $\Pi\xi$, is the square root of the discriminant of $U + \xi V$, the theory of covariants shows at once that the conclusions apply to any forms whatever of $U, V$, the expression for the transcendental function being
\[
   \Pi\xi = \int\!\frac{d\xi}{\sqrt{\operatorname{disct.}(U + \xi V)}}.
\]

\medskip

\noindent Consider now a triangle inscribed in the conic $V = 0$, and with its sides touching the conics
\[
   U + k V = 0,\quad U + k' V = 0,\quad U + k'' V = 0,
\]
then if $\theta, \theta', \theta''$ are the parameters of the angles, we have
\[
\begin{aligned}
   \Pi\theta'  - \Pi\theta   &= 2\Pi k,\\
   \Pi\theta'' - \Pi\theta'  &= 2\Pi k',\\
   \Pi\theta   - \Pi\theta'' &= 2\Pi k'',
\end{aligned}
\]
and thence
\[
   \Pi k + \Pi k' + \Pi k'' = 0,
\]
as the relation which must subsist between the parameters $k, k', k''$ of the conics touched by the sides; and similarly for a polygon of $n$ sides, the relation between the parameters is
\[
   \Pi k_1 + \Pi k_2 + \cdots + \Pi k_n = 0.
\]
But by Abel's theorem, this transcendental equation is equivalent to an algebraical one.

\medskip

\noindent In fact, calling the radical $\sqrt{D\xi}$, then if $\varphi x, \chi x$ are rational and integral functions of $x$ with arbitrary coefficients, and if
\[
   \varphi^2 x - y^2\chi^2 x\cdot D x = A\,(x - k_1)(x - k_2)\cdots(x - k_n),

(this implies that $\varphi x$ is of a degree not exceeding $n$ and $\chi x$ of a degree not exceeding $n - 3$; that is, for $n$ even the degrees of $\varphi, \chi$ are $\tfrac{1}{2}n,\ \tfrac{1}{2}(n - 4)$; and for $n$ odd they are $\tfrac{1}{2}(n - 1),\ \tfrac{1}{2}(n - 3)$), then the algebraical equation is that obtained by the elimination of the arbitrary coefficients from the system of equations
\[
\begin{aligned}
   \varphi k_1 + \chi k_1\,\sqrt{D k_1} &= 0,\\
   \varphi k_2 + \chi k_2\,\sqrt{D k_2} &= 0,\\
   \vdots\\
   \varphi k_n + \chi k_n\,\sqrt{D k_n} &= 0;
\end{aligned}
\]

which is the theorem above referred to.
