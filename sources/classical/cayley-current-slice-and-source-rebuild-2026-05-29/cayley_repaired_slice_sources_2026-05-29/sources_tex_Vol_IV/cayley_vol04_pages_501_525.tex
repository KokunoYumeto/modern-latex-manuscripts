\begin{center}
\textbf{\large 292.}\\[1ex]
\textsc{A THEOREM IN CONICS.}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
\textit{[From the Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics, vol. \textsc{iv}.\ (1861),
pp. 131--133.]}
\end{center}

\medskip

The following theorem is given in Todhunter's \textit{Conic Sections}, [Ed.~7, p.~304], ``If
ellipses be inscribed in a triangle, each with one focus in a fixed straight line, the
locus of the other focus is a conic section through the angular points of the triangle.''
A focus is the intersection of tangents to the conic from the circular points at infinity;
and instead of the circular points at infinity we may substitute any two points whatever.
This being so, let the equations of the sides of the triangle be $x=0$, $y=0$, $z=0$, and
let a pair of tangents to the curve from the points $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$, $(\alpha',\beta',\gamma')$ meet in the
point $(\xi,\eta,\zeta)$, and the other pair of tangents from the same two points meet in the
point $(X,Y,Z)$. I find that we have the very simple relation
\[ X\xi : Y\eta : Z\zeta = \alpha\alpha' : \beta\beta' : \gamma\gamma', \]
and consequently, when the locus of the point $(\xi,\eta,\zeta)$ is given, that of the point
$(X,Y,Z)$ is at once determined by substituting in the equation of the first-mentioned
locus, in the place of $\xi$, $\eta$, $\zeta$, the values $\dfrac{\alpha\alpha'}{\xi}$, $\dfrac{\beta\beta'}{\eta}$, $\dfrac{\gamma\gamma'}{\zeta}$, or as we may express it, the
second locus is derived from the first by the method of reciprocal trilinear substitutions.
And, in particular, when the first locus is a line, the second locus is a conic through
the angular points of the triangle, which is Mr Todhunter's theorem. I have con-%
sidered some of the properties of this substitution in a Memoir ``Sur quelques
transmutations des Courbes,'' \textit{Liouville}, t.\ xiv.\ (1849), pp.\ 40--46 and t.\ xv.\ (1850), pp.\
351--356, [80 and 81].

To demonstrate the theorem, I take for the equation of the conic
\[ \sqrt{(lx)}+\sqrt{(my)}+\sqrt{(nz)}=0, \]

\[ l:m:n = AA'\xi : BB'\eta : CC'\zeta, \]

of the tangent is assumed to be
\[ px+qy+rz=0, \]

\[ p\alpha+q\beta+r\gamma=0, \]

\[ X\xi : Y\eta : Z\zeta = \alpha\alpha' : \beta\beta' : \gamma\gamma', \]
which is the property in question. In the particular case, where the points $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$,
$(\alpha',\beta',\gamma')$ are the foci, the theorem is an immediate consequence of the well-known
proposition that the product of the perpendiculars let fall from the two foci upon
any tangent of the conic is a constant.

\textsc{ON A CERTAIN SYSTEM OF FUNCTIONAL SYMBOLS.}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
\textit{[From the Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics, vol. \textsc{iv}.\ (1861),

\medskip

M. Hermite, in the Memoir ``Sur la R\'esolution de l'\'equation du cinqui\`eme
degr\'e,'' \textit{Comptes Rendus}, t.\ xlvi.,\ (1858), p.~508, has given for equations of the fifth
degree a solution by means of elliptic functions, analogous to the well-known
trigonometrical solution of (the irreducible case of) the cubic equation. He has for
this purpose to consider the effect of transforming certain functions $\phi\omega$, $\psi\omega$, connected
with the elliptic functions, by replacing therein the argument $\omega$ by a linear fraction
of the form $\dfrac{c+d\omega}{a+b\omega}$, where $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ are integer numbers satisfying the condition
\[ ad-bc=1, \]
and he finds for $\phi\!\left(\dfrac{c+d\omega}{a+b\omega}\right)$ (and the like would apply to $\psi\!\left(\dfrac{c+d\omega}{a+b\omega}\right)$), six different
expressions according to the evenness, or values in relation to the modulus 2, of the
coefficients $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$, viz., the congruence
\[ ad-bc\equiv 1\ (\modd 2) \]
admits of the six solutions shown by the table
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cccc}
 & $a$ & $b$ & $c$ & $d$ \\ \hline
I.   & 1 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\
II.  & 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\
III. & 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 \\
IV.  & 1 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\
V.   & 1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\
VI.  & 0 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

(where 0 or 1 denotes that the coefficient to which it refers is even or odd), and
to each solution there corresponds a distinct value of $\phi\!\left(\dfrac{c+d\omega}{a+b\omega}\right)$ or $\psi\!\left(\dfrac{c+d\omega}{a+b\omega}\right)$.

It appeared to me that the formulae in question were not only of the highest
importance in the theory of elliptic functions, but that they were very remarkable in
and for themselves, and that the question might be looked at in the following
manner.

Suppose that $A$, $B$ are functional symbols, or, so to term them, substitutions, the
laws of operation being
\[ A(X,Y) = \left(\theta\dfrac{X}{Y},\ \dfrac{1}{Y}\right), \]
\[ B(X,Y) = (Y,\,X), \]
where $\theta$ is a constant. The equations mean that the effect of $A$ is to change $X$
into $\theta\dfrac{X}{Y}$, and $Y$ into $\dfrac{1}{Y}$, and the effect of $B$ to change $X$ into $Y$, and $Y$ into $X$.
We have
\[ A^{2}X = \theta\dfrac{\theta X/Y}{1/Y} = \theta^{2}X,\quad A^{2}Y=\dfrac{1}{1/Y}=Y, \quad\text{or}\quad A^{2}(X,Y)=(\theta^{2}X,\,Y), \]
\[ B^{2}X=X,\quad B^{2}Y=Y,\quad\text{or}\quad B^{2}(X,Y)=(X,Y); \]
thus $B$ is periodic of the second order; and in the particular case $\theta=1$, $A$ is also
periodic of the second order.

This being so, let $K$ denote any one of the compound symbols $A^{p}$, $BA^{p}$, $A^{q}B$,
$A^{q}BA^{p}$, \&c.; I say that the species of $K$ is determined by connecting with each of the
symbols in question, a certain continued fraction as follows:
\[ \begin{array}{ll}
A^{p}, & p+\omega, \\[4pt]
BA^{p}, & p-\dfrac{1}{\omega}, \\[6pt]
A^{q}B, & -\dfrac{1}{q+\omega}, \\[6pt]
BA^{q}B, & -\dfrac{1}{q-\dfrac{1}{\omega}}, \\[10pt]
A^{q}BA^{p}, & p-\dfrac{1}{q+\omega}, \\[10pt]
BA^{q}BA^{p}, & p-\dfrac{1}{q-\dfrac{1}{\omega}}, \\[12pt]
A^{r}BA^{q}BA^{p}, & p-\dfrac{1}{q-\dfrac{1}{r+\omega}}, \\[14pt]
\&c., & p-\dfrac{1}{q-\dfrac{1}{r-\dfrac{1}{\omega}}},
\end{array} \]

\[ K(X,Y) = \left\{
\begin{array}{ll}

applied to the most simple case, where $\theta=1$.

\[ K=BA^{q}BA^{p}\ldots, \]

Let $\Theta=\dfrac{c+d\omega}{a+b\omega}$ be the continued fraction which corresponds to $K$, and $\Theta'=\dfrac{c'+d'\omega}{a'+b'\omega}$
the continued fraction which corresponds to $K'$, then $\Theta'$ is deduced from $\Theta$ by putting

\[ \dfrac{c+d(\omega+r)}{a+b(\omega+r)}=\dfrac{c'+d'\omega}{a'+b'\omega}, \]
or
\[ c'=c+dr,\ d'=d,\qquad a'=a+br,\ b'=b, \]
and therefore when $r$ is even, or $\equiv 0\ (\modd 2)$, then
\[ c'=c,\ d'=d,\quad a'=a,\ b'=b,\quad (\modd 2), \]
or $K'$ is of the same species as $K$. But in this case since $A^{r}(X,Y)=(X,Y)$ and
$r$ is even, we ought to have $K'$ equivalent to $K$; and hence $r$ being even, the
theorem, assumed to be true for $K$, is also true for $K'$.

But if $r$ be odd, or $\equiv 1\ (\modd 2)$, then
\[ c'=c+1,\ d'=d,\quad a'=a+1,\ b'=b,\quad (\modd 2), \]
whence, according as
\[ K\ \text{is of species}\ \text{I., II., III., IV., V., VI., so} \]
\[ K'\ \phantom{\text{is of species}}\ \text{V., IV., VI., II., I., III.} \]

But if from $K(X,Y)$ we derive $AK(X,Y)$, we find that of whatever species $K$ may
be, the species of $AK$ is identical with the corresponding species of $K'$, and in the
case in question, where $r$ is odd, it is clear that we ought to have $K'$ equivalent to
$AK$. Hence in this case also, and therefore generally, if the theorem be true for $K$
it will be also true for $K'$.

In a very similar manner it may be shown that if the theorem is true for
$K'=A^{r}BA^{q}BA^{p}\ldots$, it will be true for $BA^{r}BA^{q}BA^{p}\ldots$, and the foregoing cases include
the case where the symbol is of the form $BA^{r}BA^{q}B$ or $A^{r}BA^{q}B$ (terminating in a $B$),
for it is only necessary to assume that the last index of $A$ is zero; hence the
theorem, if true in any case, as it obviously is for $A^{0}(X,Y)=(X,Y)$, is true
universally.

The employment of a new kind of coordinates for the analytical representation
of curves in space is suggested in my former paper under the same title \textit{Journal},
