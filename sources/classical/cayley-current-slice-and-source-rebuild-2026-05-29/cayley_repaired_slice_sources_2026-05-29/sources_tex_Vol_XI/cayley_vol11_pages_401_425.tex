\textbf{775.}\\[4pt]
\textsc{TABLES OF COVARIANTS OF THE BINARY SEXTIC.}\\[4pt]
[Written in 1894: \textit{now first published}.]
\end{center}

\bigskip

% ----- PDF p401 (printed 377) -----
\noindent
The binary sextic has in all (including the sextic itself and the
invariants) 26 covariants which I have represented by the capital
letters $A,B,C,\ldots,Z$.  The leading coefficients of the covariants
$A$ to $R$ (of course for an invariant this means the invariant
itself) are given in my paper ``Tables for the binary sextic,''
\textit{Amer.\ Math.\ Jour.}\ vol.\ \textsc{iv}.\ (1881), pp.\ 379--384,
[774]; the two invariants $W$ and $Z$ (Salmon's invariants $D$ and
$E$) had been already calculated.  But I did not in my values of the
leading coefficients, nor did Salmon in his values of the two
invariants, insert the literal terms with zero coefficients: as
remarked in my paper [143] ``Tables of the covariants $M$ to $W$ of
the binary quintic,'' it is very desirable to have in every case the
complete series of literal terms, and I have accordingly in the
expressions of the covariants $A$ to $R$ obtained for the leading
coefficients, and in the expressions obtained from Salmon for the
invariants $W$ and $Z$, inserted in each case the complete series of
literal terms.

\medskip
I give a list of the 26 covariants nearly in the form of that given
in the latter paper [143] for the covariants of the quintic, only
instead of a separate column of deg-weights I insert these in the
body of the symbol; thus
\[
  C = (3,3,4,3,3)^{2}\;\text{4 to 8}\;(x,y)^{4},
\]
the 5 coefficients of the quartic function contain respectively
$3,3,4,3,3$ terms (some of them it may be with zero coefficients),
are of the degree~$2$, and of the weights $4,5,6,7,8$ respectively.

\begin{center}
\textbf{776.}\\[4pt]
\textsc{ON THE JACOBIAN SEXTIC EQUATION.}\\[4pt]
[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied
Mathematics}, vol.\ \textsc{xviii}.\ (1882), pp.\ 52--65.]
\end{center}

\bigskip

% ----- PDF p413 (printed 389) -----
\noindent
The Jacobian sextic equation has been discussed under the form
\[
  (z-\alpha)^{6}-4a(z-\alpha)^{5}+10b(z-\alpha)^{3}-4c(z-\alpha)+5b^{2}-4ac=0,
\]
(see references at end of paper), but the connexion of this form
with the general sextic equation has not, so far as I am aware,
been considered.  And although this is probably known, I do not find
it to have been explicitly stated that the group of the equation is
the positive half-group, or group of the 60 positive substitutions
out of the 120 substitutions, which leave unaltered Serret's
6-valued function of six letters.

\bigskip

\begin{center}
\textit{Invariantive Property of the Jacobian Sextic.}
\end{center}

\medskip\noindent
Taking $z-\alpha$ as the variable, and comparing the equation with
the general sextic equation
\[
  =(a,b,c,d,e,f,g\mid z-\alpha,1)^{6}\,0,
\]
we have
\[
  a,\;b,\;c,\;d,\;e,\;f,\;g\;=\;1,\;-\tfrac{2}{3}a,\;0,\;\tfrac{1}{2}b,\;0,\;-\tfrac{2}{3}c,\;5b^{2}-4ac;
\]
the Jacobian equation is thus an equation
\[
  (a,b,c,d,e,f,g\mid x,y)^{6}=0,
\]
for which $c=0$, $e=0$, $ag+9bf-20d^{2}=0$; but of course any
equation, which can be by a linear transformation upon the
variables brought into this form, may be regarded as a Jacobian
equation.

\medskip
Hence, using henceforward the small italic in place of the small
roman letters, the Jacobian sextic may be regarded as an equation
\[
  =(a,b,c,d,e,f,g\mid x,y)^{6}\,0,
\]
linearly transformable into the form
\[
  =(a,b,0,d,0,f,g\mid x,y)^{6}\,0,
\]

\newpage
% ----- PDF p414 (printed 390) -----
\noindent
where $ag+9bf-20d^{2}=0$.  It is to be shown, that this implies a
single relation between the four invariants $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$
of the sextic function.

\medskip
I call to mind that the general sextic has five invariants
$A,B,C,D,E$ of the orders $2,4,6,10,15$ respectively; the last of
them $E$ is not independent, but its square is equal to a rational
and integral function of $A,B,C,D$; and instead of $D$, we consider
the discriminant $\Delta$ which is an invariant of the same order
10.  The values of $A,B,C$ are given.  Table Nos.\ 31, 34, and 35 of
my Third Memoir on Quantics, \textit{Phil.\ Trans.}, vol.\
\textsc{cxlvi}.\ (1856), pp.\ 627--647, [144]; those of $D$,
$\Delta$, $E$ were obtained by Dr Salmon, see his \textit{Higher
Algebra}, second ed.\ 1866, where the values of $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$,
$\Delta$, $E$ are all given; only those of $A$, $B$, $C$, are
reproduced in the third edition, 1876.

\begin{center}
\textit{The Group of the Jacobian Sextic.}
\end{center}

\medskip\noindent
The solution of the Jacobian sextic equation depends upon that of a
quintic; in fact, calling the roots $z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4},z_{5},z_{6}$,

from the general form (see my Second Memoir on Quantics, [141], or
Salmon's \textit{Higher Algebra}, third edition, p.\ 209) putting
therein $\mathrm{b}=0$, $\mathrm{d}=0$, is
\[
\begin{array}{l}
D=+\mathrm{a}^{4}\mathrm{f}^{4}\cdot 1,\\
\quad+\mathrm{a}^{3}\mathrm{ce}^{2}\mathrm{f}+160,\\
\quad\mathrm{a}^{3}\mathrm{e}^{5}+256,\\
\quad\mathrm{a}^{2}\mathrm{c}^{3}\mathrm{ef}^{2}-1440,\\
\quad\mathrm{a}^{2}\mathrm{c}^{3}\mathrm{e}^{3}-2560,\\
\quad\mathrm{ac}^{5}\mathrm{f}+3456,\\
\quad\mathrm{ac}^{4}\mathrm{e}^{2}+6400,
\end{array}
\]
and writing for $\mathrm{a},\mathrm{c},\mathrm{e},\mathrm{f}$ their
values $1$, $2\sqrt{\delta}$, $9(-\beta+4\delta)$, $216h$, the
value becomes
\begin{align*}
D\;=\;& (216)^{2}\cdot h^{2}\cdot\\
& +432h\sqrt{\delta}\cdot 12960\,(\beta-4\delta)^{2}\\
& \quad+34560\,(\beta-4\delta)\,\delta\\
& \quad+55296\,\delta^{2}\\
& -256\cdot 9^{5}\cdot-(\beta-4\delta)^{5}\\
& -10240\cdot 9^{4}\cdot(\beta-4\delta)^{4}\,\delta\\
& -102400\cdot 9^{3}\cdot(\beta-4\delta)^{3}\,\delta^{2}.
\end{align*}
The whole divides by $(216)^{2}$, and we thus obtain
\[
\begin{array}{l}
\Lambda=h^{2}+2h\sqrt{\delta}\cdot 60\,(-\beta+4\delta)^{2}\,\cdot\,+(-\beta-4\delta)^{5}\cdot\,324\,(\beta-4\delta)^{4}\\
\qquad\qquad-240\,(-\beta+4\delta)\,\delta\,\quad\quad\quad\quad+1440\,(\beta-4\delta)\,\delta\\
\qquad\qquad+256\,\delta^{2}\,\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+1600\,\delta^{2},
\end{array}
\]
which is, in fact, equal to the foregoing value of $\Lambda$.

\noindent
The conclusion is that, starting from the Jacobian sextic
\[
  f=(a,b,0,d,0,f,g\mid z,1)^{6}=0,
\]
where $ag+9bf-20d^{2}=0$, and effecting upon it the
Tschirnhausen-transformation
\[
  X=-az^{2}-6bz-10d,
\]
so as to obtain from it a sextic equation in $X$, this sextic
equation in $X$ is the resolvent sextic of the quintic equation
\[
  (1,0,\mathrm{c},0,\mathrm{e},\mathrm{f}\mid x,1)^{5}=0,
\]
where
\[
  \mathrm{c}=2d,\;\mathrm{e}=-9bf+36d^{2},\;\mathrm{f}=\sqrt{(216h)},
\]
and, $\Delta$ being the discriminant of the Jacobian sextic, then
\[
  h=\frac{1}{5\sqrt{5}}\sqrt{(-\Delta)},\quad
  =a^{2}f^{2}+b^{2}g^{2}+60b^{2}d^{2}f^{2}-240bd^{4}f+256d^{6}.
\]
