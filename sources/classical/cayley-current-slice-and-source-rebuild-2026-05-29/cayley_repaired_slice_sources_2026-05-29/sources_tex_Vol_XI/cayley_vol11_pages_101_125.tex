\textbf{729.}\\[4pt]
\textsc{ON A THEOREM RELATING TO CONFORMABLE FIGURES.}\\[6pt]
\small[From the \textit{Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society}, vol.~x.\ (1879), pp.~143--146.\\
Read May 8, 1879.]
\end{center}

\bigskip

\noindent
\artno{}Consider two plane figures, say the figure of the points $P$ referred to axes
$Ox,\,Oy$, and that of the points $P'$ referred to axes $Ox',\,Oy'$; and let $x,\,y$ be the
coordinates of $P$, and $x',\,y'$ those of $P'$. If the figures correspond to each other in
any manner whatever, $P$ and $P'$ being corresponding points, then we have $x',\,y'$
each of them a function of $x,\,y$; and we may consider the second figure as derived
from the first by altering the distance $OP$ in the ratio $\sqrt{dx'^2 + dy'^2}:\sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}$, and by
rotating it through the angle $\tan^{-1}\dfrac{dy'}{dx'} - \tan^{-1}\dfrac{dy}{dx}$; say the Extension- and
the Rotation-ratios.

\medskip

\noindent
and by the Rotation $\tan^{-1}\dfrac{dy'}{dx'} - \tan^{-1}\dfrac{dy}{dx}$, where the Extension and the Rotation are
each of them a determinate function of $x,\,y$, the coordinates of $P$.

\medskip

\noindent
Passing from the point $P$ to a consecutive point $Q$, the coordinates of which
are $x + dx,\, y + dy$ (the ratio $dy:dx$ being arbitrary), then the coordinates of the
corresponding point $Q'$ will be $x' + dx',\, y' + dy'$, where
\[
dx' = \frac{dx'}{dx}dx + \frac{dx'}{dy}dy,\qquad dy' = \frac{dy'}{dx}dx + \frac{dy'}{dy}dy.
\]

\noindent
Writing $\dfrac{dx'}{dx}$ and $\dfrac{dy'}{dx}$ instead of $dx' + dx$ and $dy' + dx$, the equations
\[
\sqrt{dx'^2 + dy'^2} = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2},\qquad \tan^{-1}\frac{dy'}{dx'} = \tan^{-1}\frac{dy}{dx}
\]
will in general have values depending upon that of the arbitrary ratio $dy:dx$. But
they may be independent of this ratio: viz.\ this is the case when $x',\,y'$ are functions
of $x,\,y$ such that
\[
\frac{dx'}{dy} = -\frac{dy'}{dx},\qquad \frac{dx'}{dx} = +\frac{dy'}{dy}.
\]

\newpage

% ============================================================
% PDF p103 (printed 79)
% ============================================================
\noindent
and the two figures are then conformable (or conjugate) figures; that is, figures similar
as regards corresponding infinitesimal elements of area. We have, in this case,
\[
\sqrt{dx'^2 + dy'^2} = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2},\qquad \tan^{-1}\frac{dy'}{dx'} - \tan^{-1}\frac{dy}{dx},
\]
each a determinate function of $x,\,y$, the coordinates of $P$; and we pass from the
element $PQ$ to the corresponding element $P'Q'$ by altering the length in the ratio
$\sqrt{dx'^2 + dy'^2}:\sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}$, and rotating the element through the angle $\tan^{-1}\dfrac{dy'}{dx'} - \tan^{-1}\dfrac{dy}{dx}$;
say, this ratio and this angle are the Aconic and the Strehlonic respectively, these
being, as already mentioned, functions of $x,\,y$ only.

\medskip

\noindent
Considering now any two conformable figures, say the figure of the points $P$,
and that of the points $P'$; we have the theorem that we can from the first figure
obtain a third conformable figure by means of an Aconic and a Strehlonic which
are respectively equal to the Extension and the Rotation by which the second figure
is derived from the first.

\medskip

\noindent
In fact, if in the three figures respectively we take $x,\,y,\,x',\,y',$ and $x'',\,y''$, for
the coordinates of the corresponding points $P,\,P',\,P''$, the first and second figures
are conformable: and we have therefore
\[
\frac{dx'}{dy} = -\frac{dy'}{dx},\qquad \frac{dx'}{dx} = \frac{dy'}{dy};
\]
the third figure is to have the Aconic $\sqrt{dx'^2 + dy'^2}:\sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}$, and the Strehlonic
\[
\tan^{-1}\frac{dy'}{dx'} - \tan^{-1}\frac{dy}{dx},
\]
viz.\ writing $r$ for $\sqrt{dx'^2 + dy'^2}$, we ought to have
\[
dx'' = \frac{dx' + dy' \cdot dy}{r}dx,\qquad dy'' = \frac{-dx' \cdot dy + dy' \cdot dx}{r}dx,
\]
and it is therefore to be shown that there exist $x'',\,y''$ functions of $x,\,y$ satisfying
these relations; for, this being so, we have
\[
\frac{dx''}{dy} = -\frac{dy''}{dx},\qquad \frac{dx''}{dx} = \frac{dy''}{dy};
\]
and the third figure is then conformable with the first.

\medskip

\noindent
Writing, for shortness,
\[
A = \frac{x'dx + y'dy}{r^2},\qquad B = \frac{-x'dy + y'dx}{r^2},
\]

\newpage

% ============================================================
% PDF p104 (printed 80)
% ============================================================
\noindent
the equations are
\[
dx'' = A\,dx - B\,dy,\qquad dy'' = B\,dx + A\,dy,
\]
or the conditions for the existence of the functions $x'',\,y''$ are
\[
\frac{dA}{dx} + \frac{dB}{dy} = 0,\qquad \frac{dA}{dy} - \frac{dB}{dx} = 0.
\]

\noindent
We, in fact, have
\[
\frac{dA}{dx} + \frac{dB}{dy} = \frac{1}{r^2}\left[\left(\frac{dx'}{dx} + \frac{dy'}{dy}\right)dx + \left(\frac{dy'}{dx} - \frac{dx'}{dy}\right)dy\right] + 2y\,\Bigl\{ - \frac{2}{r^4}\bigl[(xx' + yy')dx + (xy' - x'y)dy\bigr]\Bigr\},
\]
\[
\qquad = \frac{2y}{r^2} - \frac{2}{r^4}\bigl(x^2 + y^2\bigr)y,
\]

\noindent
and similarly
\[
\frac{dA}{dy} - \frac{dB}{dx} = \frac{1}{r^2}\left[\left(\frac{dx'}{dy} + \frac{dy'}{dx}\right)dx + \left(\frac{dy'}{dy} - \frac{dx'}{dx}\right)dy\right] + 2x\,\Bigl\{- \frac{2}{r^4}\bigl[(xx' + yy')dy - (xy' - x'y)dx\bigr]\Bigr\},
\]
\[
\qquad = \frac{2x}{r^2} - \frac{2}{r^4}\bigl(x^2 + y^2\bigr)x,
\]
which proves the theorem.

\medskip

\noindent
The theorem is closely connected with the theory of the function of an imaginary
variable; for, writing the problem for the conformable figures in the form
\[
\frac{dx'}{dx} = \frac{dy'}{dy} = F,\qquad \frac{dx'}{dy} = -\frac{dy'}{dx} = G,
\]
we have
\[
dx' = F\,dx - G\,dy,\qquad dy' = G\,dx + F\,dy,
\]
that is,
\[
dx' + i\,dy' = (F + iG)(dx + i\,dy);
\]
whence $F + iG$ is a function of $x + iy$, and thus by integration $x' + iy'$ is also a
function of $x + iy$. In one point of view, any function such as $\phi(x,\,y) + i\,\psi(x,\,y)$ is
a function of $x + iy$, for the quantity $x + iy$ is only known by means of its real
components $x,\,y$; that is, knowing $x + iy$, we know $x,\,y$, and therefore the real
function $\phi(x,\,y) + i\,\psi(x,\,y)$.

\medskip

\noindent
and Cauchy, adopting this definition, introduced the expression ``function monog\`ene''
of $x + iy$, to denote that which is in the more restricted (and the ordinary) sense
termed a function of $x + iy$. And MM. Briot and Bouquet, in their ``Th\'eorie des
fonctions elliptiques'' (Paris, 1875), although not using Cauchy's expression \textit{fonction
monog\`ene}, but the simple term \textit{fonction}, do this under the qualification stated p.~8: ``\textit{Pour
ces fonctions} (or qui sont, sous un autre point de vue, des fonctions qui admettent
\textit{une d\'eriv\'ee}).'' Now, a function admitting of a derivative (that is, in the ordinary
\newpage

% ============================================================
% PDF p105 (printed 81)
% ============================================================
\noindent
sense, a function) of the imaginary variable $x + iy$, is a function such that, for a
commutative value $x' = x + iy + dx + i\,dy$, we have a function $f(x') = f(x + iy)$,
the condition is rather that $x' + iy'$ may be a function of $x + iy$;

\medskip

\noindent
=a quantity independent of the ratio of the real components $dx,\,dy$ of the increment
$dx + i\,dy$ of the imaginary variable. Or, what is the same thing, writing $f(x) = x' + iy'$,
the condition is rather that $x' + iy'$ may be a function of $x + iy$;
\[
dx' + i\,dy' = (F + iG)(dx + i\,dy);
\]
where $F$ and $G$ are functions of $x$ and $y$. It is not part of the condition that
$F + iG$ shall be a function of $x + iy$; nor is it only a long way further on that the
authors prove that this is the case (see the definition of a ``fonction holomorphe,''
p.~14; and the proof, p.~137). The just-mentioned equation
\[
dx' + i\,dy' = (F + iG)(dx + i\,dy),
\]
where $F$ and $G$ are only assumed to be functions of $x$ and $y$, has of course in the
present paper not been used to define $x',\,y'$ as conformable figures of $x,\,y$; for by
means of the point $P'$ with coordinates $x',\,y'$, the geometrical interpretation that the
figures of the points $P$ and $P'$ are conformable figures, that is, figures similar as
regards their infinitesimal elements. The foregoing theorem in regard to the Aconics
and the Strehlonics is that we can, by means of $F$ and $G$, construct a third conformable
figure, viz.\ in fact, knowing $x + iy$ as a function of $F + iG$ and $\tan^{-1}\dfrac{G}{F}$ respectively;
and, using these as an Extension and a Rotation, we have the third conformable figure
$x'' + iy'' = (F + iG)(x + iy)$; that is, $(F + iG)(x + iy)$, and therefore also $F + iG$, is a
function of $x + iy$---and we have thus the derivative of a function of $x + iy$ as itself
a function of $x + iy$.

\medskip

\noindent
It is to be remarked that, although the theorem of the Aconics and the Strehlonics,
considered as a property of conformable figures, is now by any means geometrically
self-evident, yet the foregoing analytical proof is only a proof conducted by means of
real quantities; of when (admitting the theory of imaginary quantities) is in fact
self-evident, viz.\ the analytical conclusion really is that, $F + iG$ denoting any function
$x,\,y$, then, if $dx' + i\,dy' = (F + iG)(dx + i\,dy)$, that is, if $(F + iG)(dx + i\,dy)$ be a complete
differential, then $F + iG$ is a function of $x + iy$.

\textbf{731.}\\[4pt]
\textsc{ON THE BINOMIAL EQUATION $x^p - 1 = 0$; TRISECTION AND QUARTISECTION.}\\[6pt]
\small[From the \textit{Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society}, vol.~xi.\ (1880), pp.~4--17.\\
Read November 13, 1879.]
\end{center}

\bigskip

\noindent
\artno{}\textsc{The} solution of the binomial equation $x^p - 1 = 0$, $p$ a prime number, or, what is the same, of the equation
\[
x^{p-1} + x^{p-2} + \cdots + x + 1 = 0,
\]
depends upon the Jacobian function
\[
F_n x = x + a x^n + a^2 x^{n^2} + \cdots + a^{p-2} x^{n^{p-2}},
\]
where $a$ is a prime root of $p$, $n$ any root whatever of the equation $x^{p-1} - 1 = 0$. Taking
$a$ a factor of $p - 1$, and $f$ for the supplementary factor (that is, $p - 1 = af$), then, if
$n$ be a root of $a$, that is, root of $x^a - 1 = 0$, we have
\[
F\eta = X_0 + \eta X_1 + \cdots + \eta^{a-1} X_{a-1},
\]
where $X_0,\, X_1,\, \ldots,\, X_{a-1}$ denote each of them a period of $f$ terms, viz.
\[
X_k = x^{n^k} + x^{n^{k+a}} + x^{n^{k+2a}} + \cdots + x^{n^{k+(f-1)a}}.
\]

\noindent
$X_k = -1$,\qquad $\eta^a \ldots \eta^{a(p-1)}$
$X_k$ \quad $-1$,\qquad $\xi^a \ldots \xi^{a(p-1)}$.

\noindent
(mod $p$); the values being roots of $a^a = 1$, and so for the other functions).

\medskip

\noindent
We have, of course, $F(1) = X_0 + X_1 + \cdots + X_{a-1}$, the sum of all the roots = $-1$;
and, further, the general property that there is (independently of the value of $a$ chosen
periods is expressible as a sum
\[
a_1 X_1 + a_2 X_2 + \cdots + a_{a-1} X_{a-1},
\]
with known coefficients
\[
a_1,\, a_2,\, \ldots,\, a_{a-1}.
\]

\newpage

% ============================================================
% PDF p109 (printed 85)
% ============================================================
\noindent
The several cases $a = 2,\,3,\,4,\,\ldots$ may be termed those of the bisection, trisection,
quartisection, \&c., of the equation; viz.

$a = 2$, there are two periods, $X,\,Y,$ and $F(=1+x+X+Y)=-1$;

$a = 3$, three periods, $X,\,Y,\,Z,$ and $F\eta = X + \eta Y + \eta^2 Z$, $\eta$ a root of $x^3 - 1 = 0$;

$a = 4$, four periods, $X,\,Y,\,Z,\,W,$ and $F\theta = X + \theta Y + \theta^2 Z + \theta^3 W$, if $\theta$ be a root of $x^4 - 1 = 0$.

\medskip

\noindent
It is sufficient to attend to the prime roots which lie within the period $p$, and call
these roots, $\eta_1,\,\eta_2,\,\ldots,\,\eta_{p-1}$ in respectively; and the relation between them is determined
respectively, viz.\ if $\eta$ be a $b$-th, we have always $F(\eta) = -1$; and if $b$ be the of the
periods for the bisection. The prime roots $b$ are of course $1$ and $-1$; and we have
\[
F(1) = X + Y - 1 = -1,
\]
\[

respectively.

\medskip

\noindent
As regards the bisection, it is known that $(X - Y)^2 = (-)^{(p-1)/2}p$, which is $+p$ or $-p$,
according as $p$ is $\equiv 1$ or $3 \pmod 4$; and the values of $X,\,Y$ are thus determined.
In what follows, I assume the case $p = 1 \pmod 4$, for which $X,\,Y$ are thus determined,
$X,\,Y$ are the values $\frac{1}{2}(-1 \pm \sqrt{p})$.

\textbf{732.}\\[4pt]
\textsc{A THEOREM IN SPHERICAL TRIGONOMETRY.}\\[6pt]
\small[From the \textit{Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society}, vol.~xi.\ (1880), pp.~48--50.\\
Read January 8, 1880.]
\end{center}

\bigskip

\noindent
\artno{}In a spherical triangle, where $a,\,b,\,c$ are the sides, and $A,\,B,\,C$ their opposite
angles, we have
\begin{align*}
\tan\tfrac{1}{2}a\,\tan\tfrac{1}{2}c &= \tan\tfrac{1}{2}b\sin(A - B) + \tan\tfrac{1}{2}b\sin A - \tan\tfrac{1}{2}a\sin B,\\
\tan\tfrac{1}{2}c\,(1 - \tan\tfrac{1}{2}a\,\tan\tfrac{1}{2}b\,\cos(A - B)) &= \tan\tfrac{1}{2}b\,\cos A + \tan\tfrac{1}{2}a\,\cos B,
\end{align*}
which are both included in the form
\[
\tan\tfrac{1}{2}c\cos B = \frac{\tan\tfrac{1}{2}b\sin(A - B)\,(\cos A + i\sin A)}{1 + \tan\tfrac{1}{2}a\,\tan\tfrac{1}{2}b\,(\cos A + i\sin A)}.
\]

\noindent
For the first of the two identities: from
\begin{align*}
\cos a &= \frac{\cos A + \cos B\,\cos C}{\sin B\,\sin C},\\
\cos b &= \frac{\cos B + \cos C\,\cos A}{\sin C\,\sin A},
\end{align*}
we deduce
\begin{align*}
\cos a - \cos b &= \frac{1}{\sin A}\Bigl(\frac{\cos A}{\sin C\,\cos B} - \frac{\cos B}{\sin C\,\cos A}\Bigr) - \frac{1}{\sin B}\Bigl(\frac{\cos B}{\sin C\,\cos A} - \frac{\cos A}{\sin C\,\cos B}\Bigr),\\
&= \frac{1}{\sin A}\,\frac{(\sin A\sin B - \sin B\sin A)\,(\cos(A - B) - \cos A\sin B)}{\sin C\sin A\sin B},\\
&= \frac{\sin(A - B)}{\sin A\sin B\sin C}\,\bigl(\cos A\sin B - \cos B\sin A\bigr),\\
&= -\frac{\sin(A - B)}{\sin C}\,(\cos A\sin B - \cos B\sin A),\\
&= \frac{\sin(A - B)}{\sin C}(\cos(B + A) - 1),
\end{align*}

\noindent
that is,
\[
-\sin(A - B) = \frac{\sin C}{\sin a\sin b}\bigl(\cos a\sin b - \cos b\sin a\bigr);
\]
or, what is the same thing,
\[
-\tan\tfrac{1}{2}a\tan\tfrac{1}{2}b\sin(A - B) = \frac{\sin C}{\sin a\sin b}(\cos a\sin b - \cos b\sin a),
\]
\medskip

\noindent
Here $\cos a - \cos b = (1 + \cos a) - (1 + \cos b)$; substituting for $\dfrac{\sin a}{\sin b}$ correspondingly $\dfrac{\sin A}{\sin B}$
and $\dfrac{\sin B}{\sin A}$, the right-hand side is
\[

\]
\[
\qquad = \cot\tfrac{1}{2}a - \cot\tfrac{1}{2}b,
\]
whence, multiplying each side by $\tan\tfrac{1}{2}a\,\tan\tfrac{1}{2}b,$ we have the relation in question.

\medskip

\noindent
For the second identity which is
\[
\tan\tfrac{1}{2}c\,[1 - \tan\tfrac{1}{2}a\,\tan\tfrac{1}{2}b\,\cos(A - B)] = \tan\tfrac{1}{2}b\,\cos A + \tan\tfrac{1}{2}a\,\cos B;
\]
if on the right-hand side we substitute for $\sin A,\,\sin B$ their values
\[
\frac{\cos a + \cos b\cos c}{\sin a\sin b}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\cos b + \cos c\cos a}{\sin b\sin a},
\]
the right-hand side becomes
\[
\frac{1}{\sin C}\bigl[\cos a - \cos b + \cos C(\cos a\cos b - \cos b\cos a)\bigr],
\]
whence, multiplying the whole equation by $\sin C$, it becomes
\[
(1 - \cos c)(1 + \cos a)(1 - \cos b) = \tan\tfrac{1}{2}b\cos(A - B)

\[
\qquad = (1 - \cos c)(\cos a - \cos b) + (1 + \cos c)(\sin a\cos b - \cos a\sin b).
\]

\noindent
We have here
\[
\cos(A - B) = \frac{\cos a\sin b\cos B + \sin a\cos B}{\sin a\sin b};
\]
by substituting for $\sin a$ and $\sin b$ their values, and so for $A,\,B$ their values
\[
\frac{\cos b}{\sin c\cos a + \cos c}\quad\text{and}\quad\text{etc.}\quad\text{where}
\]
\[
\cos a + \cos b + \cos C = -\cos a + \cos b + \cos a\cos b + \cos C,
\]
or
\[
\square = 1 - \cos^2 a - \cos^2 b - \cos^2 c + 2\cos a\cos b\cos c.

\noindent
The numerator is
\[
\cos C\sin b - \cos\,(\cos^2 a + \cos^2 c)\sin a\,\sin b + \cos a\sin b
\]
\[
\qquad = 1 - \cos^2 c - (\cos^2 a + \cos^2 c)\sin a\,\sin b + \cos a\sin b + 2\cos a;

viz.\ this is

having the factor $1 + \cos a$, which is also a factor of $\sin^2 a, = 1 - \cos^2 a$, in the

\bigskip

\noindent
\artno{}\textsc{Consider} the equations
\[
(a,\,\ldots \mid \xi,\,\eta)^p = 0,\qquad (A,\,\ldots \mid \xi,\,\eta)^q = 0,
\]
where $a,\,\ldots,\,A,\,\ldots$ are functions of coordinates. To fix the ideas, suppose that each
of these coefficients is a linear function of the four coordinates $x,\,y,\,z,\,w$. Then,
eliminating $\theta$, we obtain $\nabla = 0$, the equation of a nodal-surface (the form of $\nabla$ is known); this
surface has a nodal curve.

\medskip

\noindent
It is easy to obtain the equations of the nodal curve in the case where one of
the equations, say the second, is a quadric: the process is substantially the same
whatever may be the order of the other equation, and I take it to be a cubic:
the two equations therefore are
\[
(a,\,b,\,c,\,d \mid \xi,\,\eta)^3 = 0,
\]
\[
(A,\,B,\,C \mid \xi,\,\eta)^2 = 0;
\]
giving rise to an equation
\[
\nabla\, = (a,\,b,\,c,\,A,\,B,\,C)^2 = 0.
\]

\medskip

\noindent
And it is required to perform the elimination so as to put in evidence the nodal
line of this surface.

\medskip

\noindent
Take $\theta_1,\,\theta_2$ the roots of the second equation, or write
\[
(A,\,B,\,C \mid \theta,\,1)^2 = A(\theta - \theta_1)(\theta - \theta_2);
\]
that is,
\[
\theta_1 + \theta_2 = -\frac{2B}{A},\qquad \theta_1\theta_2 = \frac{C}{A}.
\]

\[
\nabla = A^4\,\Theta_1\,\Theta_2.
\]

viz.\ on the right-hand side, replacing the symmetrical functions of $\theta_1,\,\theta_2$ by their
