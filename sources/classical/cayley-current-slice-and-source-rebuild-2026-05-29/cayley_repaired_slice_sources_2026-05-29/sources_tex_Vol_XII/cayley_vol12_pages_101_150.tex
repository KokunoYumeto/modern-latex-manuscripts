\section*{819. Two Cases of the Quadric Transformation between Two Planes (concl.)}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{819. Two Cases of the Quadric Transformation (concl.)}

\noindent\textit{[p.~101]} Writing for convenience $(a, b, c)$ for the coordinates of the fixed point, and $(x_1,\,y_1,\,z_1)$, $(x_2,\,y_2,\,z_2)$ for those of the other two points, the formul\ae{} with $A$, $B$, $C$ give thus the correspondence
\[
x_2 : y_2 : z_2 = bcx_1^{\,2} - a^2y_1z_1 \;:\; ca y_1^{\,2} - b^2 z_1 x_1 \;:\; ab z_1^{\,2} - c^2 x_1 y_1,
\]
which is the first of the two cases in question. These equations give reciprocally
\[
x_1 : y_1 : z_1 = b c x_2^{\,2} - a^2 y_2 z_2 \;:\; c a y_2^{\,2} - b^2 z_2 x_2 \;:\; a b z_2^{\,2} - c^2 x_2 y_2,
\]
or the correspondence is a $(1,\,1)$ quadric correspondence.

The formul\ae{} with $P$, $Q$, $R$ give in like manner
\[
x_2 : y_2 : z_2 = a(a x_1^{\,2} + h y_1^{\,2} + g z_1^{\,2}) + (a' y_1 z_1 + a'' z_1 x_1 + a''' x_1 y_1),\;\&\text{c.},
\]
or for shortness
\[
x_2 : y_2 : z_2 = a\Omega_1 + a\Omega_2,\quad y_2 = b\Omega_1 + b\Omega_2,\quad z_2 = c\Omega_1 + c\Omega_2,
\]
then
\[
x_1 : y_1 : z_1 = a\Omega_1' + a\Omega_2',\quad \&\text{c.},
\]
which is the second of the two cases. We have reciprocally
\[
x_1 : y_1 : z_1 = a\Omega_{1'} + a\Omega_{2'},\quad y_1 = b\Omega_{1'} + b\Omega_{2'},\quad z_1 = c\Omega_{1'} + c\Omega_{2'},
\]
where
\[
\Omega_1 = aa x_1^{\,2} + b y_1^{\,2} + c z_1^{\,2},\quad \Omega_2 = a' y_1 z_1 + b' z_1 x_1 + c' x_1 y_1,
\]
and the correspondence is thus in this case also a $(1,\,1)$ quadric correspondence.

\section*{820. On a Problem of Analytical Geometry}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{820. On a Problem of Analytical Geometry}

\begin{center}
\small[From the \textit{Johns Hopkins University Circulars}, No.~15 (1882), p.~203.]
\end{center}

\bigskip

\noindent\textit{[p.~102]} The object of the present note is only to call attention to a problem of Analytical Geometry which presents itself in connexion with the reduction of an algebraical integral, and which is solved, pp.~21, 22 of Clebsch and Gordan's \textit{Theorie der Abel'schen Functionen} (Leipzig, 1866); viz.\ the problem is considering a line drawn through any given point of a curve $f=0$ of the order $n$, to find the value $u$ of $\alpha$ such that which the remaining $n - 2$ points of intersection of the line with the curve $f$, through the intersection of $\alpha = 0$ are required for the determination of the curve. It is to be shown that we have indirectly an expression for $\alpha$, and to render the line, in the quadric curve which passes through the remaining two intersections of the line with $\Omega$, and through three given points. Take $(\xi,\,\eta,\,\zeta)$, $(\xi_1,\,\eta_1,\,\zeta_1)$ the coordinates of the two given points on the curve $f$ ($f$ is, say of the order $n = 4$); $\theta(x,\,y,\,z) = 0$ the equation of the required curve. By the equation of suppositions originally of the form, that the line $x = x' + \rho x''$, \&c.\ joining $(x',\,y',\,z')$, $(x'',\,y'',\,z'')$ meets the curve $\theta$ where $\theta = 0$ in two points $\Lambda$, which when this factor is thrown out becomes
\[
a\Lambda^{\,2} + 2b\rho\Lambda + c\rho^2 = 0,
\]
where
\[
a = (\xi_1,\,\eta_1,\,\zeta_1)^2 \theta,\quad b = (\xi_1,\,\eta_1,\,\zeta_1)(\xi,\,\eta,\,\zeta)\theta,\quad c = (\xi,\,\eta,\,\zeta)^2 \theta,
\]
\[
\gamma = (\xi,\,\eta,\,\zeta)^2 \theta,\quad \beta = (\xi_1,\,\eta_1,\,\zeta_1)(\xi,\,\eta,\,\zeta)\theta,\quad \alpha = (\xi_1,\,\eta_1,\,\zeta_1)^2 \theta,
\]
where for shortness $f$, $f_1$ are written to denote $f(\xi,\,\eta,\,\zeta)$, $f(\xi_1,\,\eta_1,\,\zeta_1)$ respectively.

\bigskip\noindent\textit{[p.~103]} The condition as to the two points obviously is that, making the same substitution $x$, $y$, $z = b\xi + a\xi_1$, $b\eta + a\eta_1$, $b\zeta + a\zeta_1$ in the equation $\Omega = (a, ..., b)(x, y, z)^2 = 0$, we must obtain the same quadric equation in $a:b$, the equation in $(a, b) = 0$. We have therefore three conditions, which, introducing an indeterminate multiplier $\theta$, are expressed by the three equations
\begin{align*}
(\alpha, \ldots, \xi)\xi_1, \eta_1, \zeta_1)^2 &= \theta a,\\
(\alpha, \ldots, \xi)\xi_1\xi,\, \eta_1\eta,\, \zeta_1\zeta) &= \theta b,\\
(\alpha, \ldots, \xi)\xi,\, \eta,\, \zeta)^2 &= \theta c.
\end{align*}
The conditions as to the three points are obviously
\begin{align*}
(\alpha, \ldots, \xi)(x_1,\,y_1,\,z_1)^2 &= 0,\\
(\alpha, \ldots, \xi)(x_2,\,y_2,\,z_2)^2 &= 0,\\
(\alpha, \ldots, \xi)(x_3,\,y_3,\,z_3)^2 &= 0,
\end{align*}
and these equations determine the ratios of $a,\,b,\,c,\,f,\,g,\,h$. But to complete the solution the convenient course is to regard the function $\Omega$, $= (a, \ldots)(x,\,y,\,z)^2$ as a quantity to be determined, and consequently to join to the foregoing the equation
\[
(\alpha, \ldots)(x,\,y,\,z)^2 = \Omega;
\]
we have then seven equations from which $(a,\,b,\,c,\,f,\,g,\,h)$ may be eliminated, the result being expressed by means of a determinant of the seventh order
\[
\left|
	ext{[determinant]}
\right| = 0,
\]
viz.\ this is an equation of the form $A\Omega = \theta V$, where $A$ is a constant determinant of the sixth order (i.e.\ a determinant not involving $x$, $y$, $z$) $V$ a determinant of the seventh order, a quadric function of $(x,\,y,\,z)$, obtained from the foregoing determinant by writing therein $\Omega = 0$ and $\theta = 1$; the multiplier $\theta$ is and remains arbitrary; but it is convenient to take it to be $= 1$, viz.\ we then say only that the equation $\Omega = 0$, of the required conic, but we put a determinate value for $\Omega$, say $\Omega = A^{-1}V$, which is, in fact, an equation $\Omega = 0$, $= A^{-1}V$ arbitrary.

\bigskip

\section*{821. On the Geometrical Representation of an Equation between Two Variables}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{821. On the Geometrical Representation of an Equation between Two Variables}

\begin{center}
\small[From the \textit{Johns Hopkins University Circulars}, No.~15 (1882), p.~210.]
\end{center}

\bigskip

\noindent\textit{[p.~104]} An equation between two variables cannot be represented in a satisfactory manner by a curve, for this serves only to represent the corresponding real values of the variables; to represent the imaginary values the natural course is to represent each variable by a point in a plane; viz.\ the variable $x$, $= x + iy$, will be represented by a point the coordinates of which are the components $x$ and $y$ of the variable, and similarly the variable $x'$, $= x' + iy'$, by a point the coordinates of which are the components $x'$ and $y'$ of the variable; the equation between the two variables will then establish a correspondence between the two points respectively, and to a point on the plane of $x$ there correspond two or more points on the plane of $x'$. But to complete the solution the convenient course is to regard $x$, $x'$ as fixed, and respectively, the planes which contain the two points respectively; and it is this correspondence of the two planes which is the proper geometrical representation of the equation between the two variables: to exhibit this correspondence we may in either of the planes draw a network of curves or planes, and then draw in the other plane the network of corresponding curves. This well-known theory [see {\&}c.] illustrated by the notion of a Riemann's surface; taking in the infinite half-plane $y$ positive about the origin as centre a system of semi-circles, to these correspond in the infinite plane of $x'y'$ a series of luminate-shaped curves; and by means of these it is easy to show in the second plane the path corresponding to a given path of the point $x$, $= x + iy$, in the first half-plane.

\bigskip

\section*{822. On Associative Imaginaries}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{822. On Associative Imaginaries}

\begin{center}
\small[From the \textit{Johns Hopkins University Circulars}, No.~15 (1882), pp.~211, 212.]
\end{center}

\bigskip

\noindent\textit{[p.~105]} The imaginary $(x,\,y)$ defined by the equations
\begin{align*}
x^2 &= ax + by,\\
xy &= cx + dy,\\
yx &= \alpha x + \beta y,\\
y^2 &= px + qy,
\end{align*}
will not be in general associative; to make them so, we must have 8 double relations corresponding to the combinations $x^2.x$, $xy.x$, $yx.x$, $xy.y$, $y^2.x$ respectively; viz.\ the first of these gives $x.x.x = x.x.x$, that is, $\theta = x(ax + by) - (ax + by).x$, $= b(xy - yx)$, $= b(c - \alpha) x + b(d - \beta) y$, and similarly for each of the other terms. We thus obtain by way of the 8 equations between the 6 coefficients $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$, $\alpha$, $\beta$, $p$, $q$, viz.\ the relations so obtained are
\begin{align*}
b(c - \alpha) &= 0\;(twice),\quad b(d - \beta) = 0,\quad p(d - \beta) = 0\;(twice),\\
bp - ad &= 0\;(twice),\quad bp - q^2 = 0\;(twice),\\
c^2 + dp - c\alpha - p\beta &= 0,\quad d^2 + bc - bd - dq + f\beta + q\alpha = 0,\\
c^2 + cq - c\alpha - q\beta &= 0,\quad b(f - d) - q(\alpha - b) = 0.
\end{align*}
From the first four equations it appears that either $b = 0$ and $p = 0$, or else $c = \alpha$ and $d = \beta$; the latter solution may be set aside for the moment, giving the commutative system
\begin{align*}
x^2 &= ax + by,\\
xy = yx &= cx + dy,\\
y^2 &= px + qy.
\end{align*}

\noindent\textit{[p.~106]} In order that this may be associative, we must still have the relations
\begin{align*}
bp - cd &= 0,\\
c^2 + dp - cq - dk &= 0,\\
d^2 + bc - ad - bk &= 0,
\end{align*}
which are all three of them satisfied by $p = \frac{cd}{b}$, $k = \frac{d^2 + bc - ad}{b}$, viz.\ we thus have the associative and commutative system
\begin{align*}
x^2 &= ax + by,\\
xy = yx &= cx + dy,\\
y^2 &= \frac{cd}{b}\,x + \frac{d^2 + bc - ad}{b}\,y,
\end{align*}
I did not perceive how to identify this system with any of the double algebras of B. Peirce's \textit{Linear Associative Algebra}, see pp.~120--122 of the Reprint, \textit{American Journal of Mathematics}, t.~iv.\ (1881); but it has been pointed out to me by Mr C.\ S.\ Peirce that my system, in the general case $ad - bc \neq 0$, is reducible to the form $(a)$, see p.~122 (l.c.). The object of the present Note is to exhibit in the simple case of two imaginaries the whole system of relations which must subsist between the coefficients in order that the imaginaries may be associative; that is, the system of equations which are solved implicitly by the establishment of the several multiplication tables of the memoir just referred to.

\bigskip

\section*{824. Note on the Formul\ae{} of Trigonometry}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{824. Note on the Formul\ae{} of Trigonometry}

\begin{center}
\small[From the \textit{Johns Hopkins University Circulars}, No.~17 (1882), p.~241.]
\end{center}

\bigskip

\noindent\textit{[p.~108 cont.]} The equations $a = c\cos B + b\cos C$, $b = a\cos C + c\cos A$, $c = b\cos A + a\cos B$, which connect together the sides $a$, $b$, $c$ and the angles $A$, $B$, $C$ of a plane triangle, may be presented in an algebraical rational form, by introducing in place of the angles $A$, $B$, $C$ the functions $\cos A + i\sin A$, $\cos B + i\sin B$, $\cos C + i\sin C$ calling these $\frac{x}{y'},\,\frac{y'}{z'},\,\frac{z'}{x'}$ respectively, or, what is the same thing, writing $\Sigma \cos A = \frac{x}{y'} + \frac{x'}{y}$, $\Sigma \sin A = \frac{x}{y'} - \frac{x'}{y}$, then the foregoing equations may be written
\begin{align*}
(- \Sigma x + y' + z')(- \Sigma x' + y + z) + i\Sigma y\Sigma y' &= 0,\\
(c\Sigma x' + xz - \Sigma x x') - i\Sigma x(y'z' + y'z) &= 0,\\
(c\Sigma y' + x'y' - \Sigma x x') - i\Sigma y' &= 0;
\end{align*}
that is, as a system of bipartite equations in $(a, b, c)$ and cubic in $(x, y, z)$.

Similarly to Spherical Trigonometry, writing as above for the angles, and for the sides writing in like manner $2\cos a = \frac{l}{l'} + \frac{l'}{l}$, $2\cos b = \frac{m}{m'} + \frac{m'}{m}$, $2\cos c = \frac{n}{n'} + \frac{n'}{n}$, we have a system of bipartite equations separately homogeneous in regard to $(x, y, z, x'$, $y'$, $z')$ and $(a, B, b, q, R)$ respectively.

\section*{825. A Memoir on the Abelian and Theta Functions}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{825. A Memoir on the Abelian and Theta Functions}

\begin{center}
\small[Chapters I to III, \textit{American Journal of Mathematics}, t.\ v.\ (1882), pp.~137--179;\\
Chapters IV to VII, ib., t.\ vii.\ (1885), pp.~101--167.]
\end{center}

\bigskip

\noindent\textit{[p.~109]} THE present memoir is based upon Clebsch and Gordan's \textit{Theorie der Abel'schen Functionen}, Leipzig, 1866 (here cited as C.\ and G.); the employment of differential rather than of integral equations is a novelty; but the chief addition to the theory consists in the determination which I have made for the cubic curve, and also (but not as yet in a perfect form) for the quartic curve, of the differential expression $d\Pi_n$ (or as I write it $\bar{d}\Pi$); is the integral of the third kind $\int_{1,2}\!\bar{d}\Pi_n$ in the dual normal form (collide Nuessellen) for which we have (p.~117) $\int_{1,2}\!\bar{d}\Pi_n = \int_n \bar{d}\Pi_n$, the limits and parametric points interchangeable. The want of this determination presented itself to me as a barrier in the theory during the course of lectures on the subject which I had the pleasure of giving in the Johns Hopkins University, Baltimore, U.S.A., in the months January to June, 1882, and I succeeded in effecting it for the cubic curve; but it was not until shortly after my return to England that I was able partially to effect the like determination in the first more difficult case of the quartic curve. The memoir contains, with additional developments, a reproduction of the course of lectures just referred to, I have endeavoured to simplify as much as possible the notations and demonstrations of Clebsch and Gordan's admirable treatise; to bring some of the geometrical results into greater prominence, and to illustrate the theory by examples in regard to the cubic, the nodal quartic, and the general quartic curves respectively. The various chapters are: I, Abel's Theorem; II, Proof of Abel's Theorem; III, The Major Function (continued); V, Miscellaneous Investigations; VI, The Nodal Quartic; VII, The Functions $T$, $U$, $V$, $\Theta$. The paragraphs of the whole memoir will be numbered continuously.

\subsection*{Chapter I.\ Abel's Theorem.}

\textit{Proposition.}\quad Art.\ Nos.\ 1 to 5.

\noindent\textit{[p.~110]} 1.\ Starting from the equation $\phi = (x, \ldots, \xi, y)^n = 0$ of the variable curve, we have
\[
\Sigma\,(x,\,y,\,1)^{n-2}\,du = 0.
\]

\textit{The Differential Pure and Affected Theorems.}\quad Art.\ Nos.\ 1 to 5.

1.\ We have a fixed curve and a variable curve, and the differential pure theorem consists in a set of linear relations between the displacements of the intersections of the two curves; in the affected theorem, a linear function of the displacements is equated to another differential expression. I state the two theorems, giving afterwards the necessary explanations.

The pure theorem is
\[
\Sigma\,(x,\,y,\,1)^{n-2}\,du = 0.
\]
The affected theorem is
\[
\Sigma\,(x,\,y,\,1)^{n-2}\,du = -\frac{b\phi_n}{\phi_n}\;\frac{d\phi_n}{\phi_n}\;\frac{d\phi_n}{\phi_n}.
\]
2.\ We have a fixed curve $f = 0$, or say the curve $f$, or simply the fixed curve; of the order $n$, with $\delta$ dpps, and therefore of the deficiency $\frac{1}{2}(n - 1)(n - 2) - \delta$. The expression ``the dpp'' means always the $\delta$ dpp of $f$.

And we have a variable curve $\phi = 0$, or say the curve $\phi$, or simply the variable curve, of the order $m$, passing through the dps and besides meeting the fixed curve in $mn - 2\delta$ variable points.

Moreover, $du$ is the displacement of the current point $\theta$, coordinates $(x, y, z)$, on the fixed curve, viz.\ the equation $f = 0$ being satisfied, we have
\[
\frac{df}{dx}\,dx + \frac{df}{dy}\,dy + \frac{df}{dz}\,dz = 0,
\]
and we thence have
\[
\frac{df}{dx} : \frac{df}{dy} : \frac{df}{dz} = y \,dx - x \,dy : z \,dx - x \,dz : x \,dy - y \,dx,
\]
so that we have three equal values each of which is put $= du$, viz.\ we write
\[
\frac{y \,dz - z \,dy}{\frac{df}{dx}} = \frac{z \,dx - x \,dz}{\frac{df}{dy}} = \frac{x \,dy - y \,dx}{\frac{df}{dz}} = du,
\]
and so as thus defined is the displacement.

\noindent\textit{[p.~111]} 3.\ $(x, y, z)^{n - 3} = 0$ is the minor curve, viz.\ the general curve of the order $n - 3$, which passes through the dpp's, and the general curve of the order $n - 3$, $z$ contains $\frac{1}{2}n(n - 3) + 1$ terms (viz.\ $\frac{1}{2}n(n - 3) - 1$ terms, but $y$ thereto for the dpps), respectively; and $\theta(t)$ denotes the determinant
\[
\theta(t) =
\left|
	ext{[determinant]}
\right|,
\]
so that $\theta(t) = 0$ is the equation of the line joining the points 1 and 2; viz.\ this is the line $(123)$ in the great curve of order $n - 2$ passing through the points of $1$, $2$.

\bigskip\noindent\textit{[p.~112]} 4.\ It is of course important to show, and it will be shown, that the number of independent displacement-relations given by the theorem is equal to the number of independent integral relations between the variable intersections.

5.\ Observe that the pure theorem gives all the displacement-relations between the variable intersections; for instance, we may help to see the latter equal to the number of independent linear relations between the displacements $\Sigma\,(x,\,y,\,z)^{n-2}\,du = 0$ for the variable intersections.

\textit{Examples of the Pure Theorem---The Fixed Curve a Cubic.}\quad Art.\ Nos.\ 6 to 13.

6.\ The pure theorem is next applicable to the case $n = 2$, the fixed curve a conic; it is here given no displacement-relation, and this is as it should be, for the variable intersections of $\phi$ with the conic are not so independent.

The next case is $n = 3$, $\delta = 0$, the fixed curve a cubic. For proper simplicity the equation is to be taken in the form $f = ax^3 + by^3 + cz^3 + 6lxyz = 0$.

For the variable curve we may take the equation
\[
du = \frac{dy}{f_x} = -\frac{dx}{f_y};
\]
the two cases being of course in Cartesian form, may then be written
\[
du = \frac{dx}{-f_y} = -\frac{dy}{f_x};
\]
the first curve here may be a cubic, let the variable curve $\phi$, $= 0$, be a cubic of order $m$, $= 1, 2, 3,$ \&c.\ as the case may be. For the simplest theorem, the curve $\phi$ is a line.

\noindent\textit{[p.~113]} The corresponding integral equation is the equation which expresses that the points $1$, $2$, $3$ are in a line, viz.\ considering $y_1$, $y_2$, $y_3$ as given functions of $x_1$, $x_2$, $x_3$, respectively, this is
\[
\left|
	ext{[determinant]}
\right| = 0,
\]
or, in the notation already made use for such a determinant, $123 = 0$.

\bigskip

7.\ The equation $du_1 + du_2 + du_3 = 0$, here $du = \frac{dx}{ay^2 + 2lzx}$, or as a particular interpretation, if there is a peculiar interpretation of the corresponding two as arbitrary constants, thereby the the curve $f = 0$, an arbitrary constant; consider then the cubic equation $f = ax^3 + by^3 + cz^3 + 6lxyz = 0$.
