\section*{Paper 350 (continued): On the Classification of Cubic Curves}

We may fix the absolute magnitudes of the coordinates by writing $x + z = 1$; and the equation then becomes
\[
(x^{2}+y^{2})(1-x) + 4k\bigl\{(\lambda - \nu)x + \mu y + \nu\bigr\} \;=\; 0.
\]

The origin is at the asymptote-point, or intersection of the imaginary asymptotes; the equation of the real asymptote is $z = 1$; that of the imaginary asymptotes is $x^{2}+y^{2}=0$.

If $x$ and $y$ are ordinary rectangular coordinates then the pair of lines represented by this equation will be an indefinitely small circle, and conversely, if the Asymptote-Point be an indefinitely small circle, then $x$ and $y$ will be rectangular coordinates; and we may without loss of generality assume that this is so.

\medskip
\textbf{73.}\quad The equation of the envelope is
\[
\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}(x+yi)} + \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}(x-yi)} + \sqrt{z} \;=\; 0;
\]
this gives successively
\[
\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}(x+yi)} + \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}(x-yi)} \;=\; -\sqrt{z},
\]
\[
\tfrac{1}{2}(x+yi) + \tfrac{1}{2}(x-yi) + 2\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}(x+yi)\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}(x-yi)} \;=\; z,
\]
that is
\[
x + 2\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4}(x^{2}+y^{2})} \;=\; z, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad x + \sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}} \;=\; z;
\]
and proceeding further,
\[
(x+z)^{2} + x^{2}+y^{2} - 2(x+z)\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}} \;=\; 4z\bigl(x + \sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\bigr),
\]
\[
(x-z)^{2} + x^{2}+y^{2} \;=\; 2(x+3z)\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}},
\]
that is
\[
\bigl\{(x-z)^{2} + x^{2}+y^{2}\bigr\}^{2} \;=\; 4(x+3z)^{2}(x^{2}+y^{2});
\]
\[
(x-z)^{4} + 2(x^{2}+y^{2})\bigl\{(x-z)^{2} - 2(x+3z)^{2}\bigr\} + (x^{2}+y^{2})^{2} \;=\; 0.
\]
Putting $z = 1 - x$, and therefore $x - z = 2x - 1$, and $x + 3z = 3 - 2x$, this becomes
\[
(2x-1)^{4} + 2(x^{2}+y^{2})\bigl\{(2x-1)^{2} - 2(3-2x)^{2}\bigr\} + (x^{2}+y^{2})^{2} \;=\; 0,
\]
that is
\[
(x^{2}+y^{2})^{2} - 2(x^{2}+y^{2})(4x^{2} - 20x + 17) + (2x-1)^{4} \;=\; 0,
\]
which may also be written
\[
y^{4} - 2y^{2}(3x^{2} - 20x + 17) + 9x^{4} + 8x^{3} - 10x^{2} - 8x + 1 \;=\; 0,
\]
or, what is the same thing,
\[
y^{4} - 2y^{2}(x-1)(3x-17) + (x-1)(9x-1)(x+1)^{2} \;=\; 0,
\]
for the equation of the envelope. The solution of the equation in $y$ gives
\[
y^{2} \;=\; (x-1)(3x-17) \;\pm\; (2x-3)\sqrt{-32(x-1)}.
\]

\medskip
\textbf{74.}\quad The original curve $\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{y} + \sqrt{z} = 0$ had the three nodes
\[
(2,\,-\tfrac{1}{2},\,-\tfrac{1}{2}),\qquad (-\tfrac{1}{2},\,2,\,-\tfrac{1}{2}),\qquad (-\tfrac{1}{2},\,-\tfrac{1}{2},\,2);
\]
and thence writing $\tfrac{1}{2}(x+yi)$, $\tfrac{1}{2}(x-yi)$, $z$ for $x$, $y$, $z$, we find the nodes
\[
(x = \tfrac{3}{2},\; y = \tfrac{5}{2} i),\qquad (x = \tfrac{3}{2},\; y = -\tfrac{5}{2} i),\qquad (x = -1,\; y = 0);
\]
the first and second of these are acnodes, or the curve has a pair of imaginary acnodes; the third is a crunode; and to find the directions at this point, if in the equation of the curve we write $x - 1$ for $x$, the equation becomes
\[
y^{4} - 2y^{2}(x-2)(3x-20) + (x-2)(9x-10)\,x^{2} \;=\; 0;
\]
the lowest terms therefore are $20(-4y^{2} + x^{2}) = 0$; and we have $y = \pm\tfrac{1}{2}(x+1)$ for the equation of the tangents at the crunode.

\medskip

$y = 0$ gives $x = 1$, $x = \tfrac{1}{9}$ and (as a twofold value) $x = -1$, which belongs to the crunode.

$x = 1$ gives $y^{4} = 0$, or the line $x = 1$ is a tangent of four-pointic intersection.

$x = 0$ gives $y^{4} - 34 y^{2} + 1 = 0$, that is $y^{2} = 17 \pm 12\sqrt{2}$, or $y = \pm(3 \pm 2\sqrt{2})$.

\medskip
\textbf{75.}\quad The curve has a pair of asymptotic parabolas, and taking for the equation of one of them
\[
(y - x\sqrt{3} + \beta)^{2} \;=\; -32(x + \alpha),
\]
this gives
\[
y \;=\; x\sqrt{3} - \beta + \sqrt{-32(x+\alpha)},
\]
and thence
\[
y^{2} \;=\; 3x^{2} - 2\beta x\sqrt{3} + \beta^{2} - \frac{2\beta}{\sqrt{3}}\,(\text{coefficient terms}) + 2\!\left(x - \frac{\beta}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\sqrt{-32(x+\alpha)},
\]
that is, more explicitly,
\[
y^{2} \;=\; 3x^{2} - 2\beta x\sqrt{3} + \beta^{2} + 2\!\left(x - \frac{\beta}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\sqrt{-32(x+\alpha)},
\]
which agrees with the value of $y^{2}$ in the envelope as to the terms $x^{2}$, $x\sqrt{x}$, and by properly determining $\alpha$, $\beta$, it may be made to agree as to the terms $x$ and $x^{0}$.

We have in the parabola
\[
y^{2} \;=\; 3x^{2} - 2\beta x\sqrt{3} + \beta^{2} + \sqrt{-32}\,\Bigl\{2x\sqrt{x} + (\alpha - \tfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{3}})\sqrt{x}\Bigr\},
\]
and in the curve
\[
y^{2} \;=\; 3x^{2} - 20x + 17 + \sqrt{-32}\,\bigl\{2x\sqrt{x} - 4\sqrt{x}\bigr\}.
\]

We have therefore
\[
-2\beta\sqrt{3} \;=\; -20,\qquad \alpha - \tfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{3}} \;=\; -4,
\]
giving
\[
\beta \;=\; \frac{10}{3\sqrt{3}} \;=\; \frac{10\sqrt{3}}{9},\qquad \alpha \;=\; -\tfrac{2}{9},
\]
so that the equation of the parabola is
\[
\bigl(y - (x - \tfrac{1}{3})\sqrt{3}\bigr)^{2} \;=\; -\tfrac{32}{9}(x - \tfrac{2}{9});
\]
that of the other parabola is of course obtained by merely changing the sign of $\sqrt{3}$.

\medskip
\textbf{76.}\quad From the foregoing results we may trace the curve, but this may be done somewhat more easily by means of polar coordinates, viz.\ writing $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, and therefore $z = 1 - r\cos\theta$, we have
\[
\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2} r}\cdot 2\cos\tfrac{1}{2}\theta + \sqrt{1 - r\cos\theta} \;=\; 0,
\]
that is
\[
r\bigl(\cos\theta + 8\cos^{2}\tfrac{1}{2}\theta\bigr) \;=\; 1;
\]
or since
\[
\cos\theta \;=\; 1 - 8\sin^{2}\tfrac{1}{2}\theta\cos^{2}\tfrac{1}{2}\theta
\;=\; 1 - 8\cos^{2}\tfrac{1}{2}\theta + 8\cos^{4}\tfrac{1}{2}\theta,
\]
we have
\[
\cos\theta + 8\cos^{2}\tfrac{1}{2}\theta \;=\; (1 - 4\cos^{2}\tfrac{1}{2}\theta)^{2}
\;=\; (-1 - 2\cos\tfrac{1}{2}\theta)^{2},
\]
and the equation is
\[
r \;=\; \frac{1}{(1 + 2\cos\tfrac{1}{2}\theta)^{2}}.
\]

$\theta = 0^{\circ}$ gives $r = \tfrac{1}{9}$, $\theta = 180^{\circ}$ gives $r = 1$, $\theta = 240^{\circ}$ gives $r = \infty$, $\theta = 360^{\circ}$ gives $r = 1$, values which agree with the results obtained by rectangular coordinates.

\medskip
\textbf{77.}\quad The form is shown in the figure; we see that the curve consists of a lower branch without any singularity; and of an upper branch which cuts itself in the crunode.

\medskip
\textbf{78.}\quad The equation of the twofold centre locus (making in the form $\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{y} + \sqrt{z} = 0$, the foregoing transformation) is
\[
\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}(x + yi)} + \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}(x - yi)} + \sqrt{1 - x} \;=\; 0,
\]
that is
\[
x + \sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}} \;=\; 1 - x,
\]
or
\[
\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}} \;=\; 1 - 2x,
\]
which is
\[
3x^{2} - 4x - y^{2} \;=\; -1,
\]
or finally
\[
9\!\left(x - \tfrac{2}{3}\right)^{2} - 3 y^{2} \;=\; 1,
\]
which is a hyperbola having its centre at the harmonic point $x = \tfrac{2}{3}$, $y = 0$; having $x = \tfrac{1}{3}$, $x = 1$ for the extremities of the transverse axis, and such that the asymptotes are inclined to the axis of $x$ at an angle $= 60^{\circ}$; this curve is also shown in the figure.

\medskip
\textbf{79.}\quad Similarly making the transformation in the equation of the one-with-twofold centre locus written under the form $-(-x + y + z)(x - y + z)(x + y - z) + xyz = 0$, this becomes
\[
-\bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}(x+yi) + \tfrac{1}{2}(x-yi) + z\bigr)\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}(x+yi) - \tfrac{1}{2}(x-yi) + z\bigr)\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}(x+yi) + \tfrac{1}{2}(x-yi) - z\bigr) + \tfrac{1}{4}(x+yi)(x-yi)\,z \;=\; 0,
\]
that is
\[
-(-yi + z)(yi + z)(x - z) + \tfrac{1}{4}(x + yi)(x - yi)\, z \;=\; 0,
\]
which is
\[
-\tfrac{1}{4}(y^{2}+z^{2})(x-z) + \tfrac{1}{4}(x^{2}+y^{2})\,z \;=\; 0,
\]
\[
-4 z^{2}(x-z) + x^{2} z + y^{2}(5 z - 4 x) \;=\; 0,
\]
or, what is the same thing,
\[
z(x - 2 z)^{2} + y^{2}(5 z - 4 x) \;=\; 0,
\]
or, putting for $z$ its value $= 1 - x$, this is
\[
(1 - x)(3x - 2)^{2} + y^{2}(5 - 9 x) \;=\; 0,
\]
that is
\[
y^{2} \;=\; -\frac{(x-1)(3x-2)^{2}}{9 x - 5},
\]
which is the equation of the one-with-twofold centre locus.

\medskip
\textbf{80.}\quad The curve is symmetrical in regard to the axis of $x$. And moreover
\begin{align*}
x &< \tfrac{5}{9},\quad y \text{ is impossible},\\
x &= \tfrac{5}{9},\quad y^{2} = \infty, \text{ or the line } x = \tfrac{5}{9} \text{ is an asymptote},\\
x &= \tfrac{2}{3},\quad y^{2} = 0, \text{ which is a crunode},\\
x &= 1,\quad y^{2} = 0,\\
x &> 1,\quad y \text{ is impossible}.
\end{align*}
The equation of the tangents at the crunode are $y^{2} = 3(x - \tfrac{2}{3})$, or the tangents are inclined to the axis of $x$ at angles $= 60^{\circ}$. The curve consists, as shown in the figure, of a single branch cutting itself in the crunode, and tending on each side towards the asymptote.

\medskip
\textbf{81.}\quad The equation of the harmonic conic, making the foregoing transformation in the equation $\dfrac{\lambda - \nu}{x} + \dfrac{\mu}{y} + \dfrac{\lambda - \mu}{z} = 0$, becomes
\[
\frac{\lambda - \nu + \mu i}{x + y i} + \frac{\lambda - \nu - \mu i}{x - y i} + \frac{\mu i - \mu i}{z} \;=\; 0,
\]
that is
\[
-2(\lambda - \nu)\,yi + 2\mu i\, x - \mu i\,\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{z} \;=\; 0,
\]
which is, or what is the same thing,
\[
\mu(x^{2}+y^{2}) - 2 z\bigl\{\mu x - (\lambda - \nu) y\bigr\} \;=\; 0,
\]
\[
\mu(x^{2}+y^{2} - 2 z x) + 2(\lambda - \nu)\, y z \;=\; 0,
\]
which, putting for $z$ its value $= 1 - x$, is
\[
\mu(3 x^{2} + y^{2} - 2 x) + 2(\lambda - \nu)\, y(1 - x) \;=\; 0,
\]
or developing,
\[
3 \mu x^{2} - 2(\lambda - \nu)\, x y + \mu y^{2} - 2 \mu x + 2(\lambda - \nu)\, y \;=\; 0,
\]
either of which is the equation of the harmonic conic corresponding to the satellite line $(\lambda - \nu) x + \mu y + \nu = 0$, or, since the direction is alone material, to the satellite line $(\lambda - \nu) x + \mu y = 0$. The second form shows that the conic is
\begin{center}\begin{tabular}{rl}
an ellipse for & $3\mu^{2} > (\lambda - \nu)^{2}$,\\
a parabola ,, & $3\mu^{2} = (\lambda - \nu)^{2}$,\\
a hyperbola ,, & $3\mu^{2} < (\lambda - \nu)^{2}$.
\end{tabular}\end{center}

\medskip
\textbf{82.}\quad The first form shows that the conic passes through the four points which are the intersection of the ellipse $3 x^{2} + y^{2} - 2 x = 0$, (or, as the equation may also be written, $9(x - \tfrac{1}{3})^{2} + 3 y^{2} = 1$), with the pair of lines $(x - 1) y = 0$: this is right, for the points in question are the three points $(x = 0, y = 0)$, $(x = 1, y = i)$, $(x = 1, y = -i)$, which are the vertices of the triangle formed by the asymptotes $(x^{2}+y^{2})(x - 1) = 0$; and the point $x = \tfrac{2}{3}$, $y = 0$, which is the harmonic point.

\medskip
\textbf{83.}\quad Putting for shortness $\lambda - \nu = \kappa$, so that the equation of the satellite line is $\kappa x + \mu y + \nu = 0$, and that of the corresponding harmonic conic is
\[
\mu(3 x^{2} + y^{2} - 2 x) + 2 \kappa y(1 - x) \;=\; 0,
\]
the coordinates of the centre are found from the formulae
\[
\tfrac{1}{2}(x + yi) : \tfrac{1}{2}(x - yi) : z \;=\; (\kappa + \mu i)^{2} : (\kappa - \mu i)^{2} : -4\mu^{2},
\]
whence, since $x + z = 1$, we have for the coordinates of the centre
\[
x \;=\; \frac{\mu^{2} - \kappa^{2}}{3 \mu^{2} - \kappa^{2}},\qquad y \;=\; \frac{-2 \mu \kappa}{3 \mu^{2} - \kappa^{2}},
\]
and it is easy to verify that these belong to a point on the twofold centre conic
\[
3 x^{2} - 4 x - y^{2} + 1 \;=\; 0.
\]

\medskip
\textbf{84.}\quad The asymptotes of the harmonic conic meet at the centre; and they again cut the twofold centre conic in two points, the intersections of the last-mentioned conic with the line
\[
(\kappa + \mu i)\,\tfrac{1}{2}(x + y i) + (-\kappa + \mu i)\,\tfrac{1}{2}(x - y i) - 2 \mu i\, z \;=\; 0,
\]
that is
\[
\mu x + \kappa y - 2 \mu z \;=\; 0,
\]
or, what is the same thing,
\[
3 \mu x + \kappa y - 2 \mu \;=\; 0.
\]

\medskip
\textbf{85.}\quad I remark that the equation of the asymptotes of the harmonic conic is
\[
(3\mu^{2} - \kappa^{2})\bigl[\mu(3 x^{2} + y^{2} - 2 x) + 2 \kappa y(1 - x)\bigr] + \mu(\mu^{2} + \kappa^{2}) \;=\; 0,
\]
and that the theorem for the construction of the asymptotes depends on the identical equation
\begin{align*}
(3 \mu^{2} - \kappa^{2})\bigl[\mu(3 x^{2} + y^{2} - 2 x) &+ 2 \kappa y(1 - x)\bigr] + \mu(\mu^{2} + \kappa^{2}) + 3 \mu(\mu^{2} + \kappa^{2})(3 x^{2} - 4 x + 1)\\
&= -2\bigl[-(3\mu^{2} - \kappa^{2})\,x + 2 \mu \kappa\, y + \mu^{2} + \kappa^{2}\bigr](3 \mu x + \kappa y - 2 \mu),
\end{align*}
which is easily verified; and where
\[
-(3 \mu^{2} - \kappa^{2})\, x + 2 \mu \kappa\, y + \mu^{2} + \kappa^{2} \;=\; 0
\]
is the equation of the tangent of the twofold centre conic $3 x^{2} - y^{2} - 4 x + 1 = 0$ at the centre of the harmonic conic.

\medskip
\textbf{86.}\quad On account of the symmetry in regard to the axis of $x$, it will be sufficient to attend to the series of curves corresponding to a direction $y = x$ of the satellite line, for which the ratio $-\dfrac{\kappa}{\mu}$ has a given sign; and the inclination of the satellite line to the asymptote will pass from $90^{\circ}$ to $0^{\circ}$ according as the value of the ratio $-\dfrac{\kappa}{\mu}$ passes from $0$ to $\infty$.

\medskip
\textbf{87.}\quad First, if $-\dfrac{\kappa}{\mu}$ is $= 0$, that is, if the satellite line be perpendicular to the asymptote, then the harmonic conic is the ellipse
\[
3 x^{2} + y^{2} - 2 x \;=\; 0,
\]
or, as it may also be written,
\[
9(x - \tfrac{1}{3})^{2} + 3 y^{2} \;=\; 1.
\]

As $-\dfrac{\kappa}{\mu}$ increases from $0$ to $\sqrt{3}$, that is, as the inclination of the satellite line diminishes from $90^{\circ}$ to $30^{\circ}$, the harmonic conic becomes an ellipse of greater and greater excentricity, and ultimately a parabola.

\medskip
\textbf{88.}\quad I notice the particular case $-\dfrac{\kappa}{\mu} = \tfrac{1}{2}$, which corresponds to the direction parallel to one of the nodal tangents of the envelope: the harmonic conic is in this case the ellipse
\[
3 x^{2} + x y + y^{2} - 2 x - y \;=\; 0,
\]
which, it will be observed, passes through the point $(x = 0, y = 1)$ which is one of the points of intersection of the twofold centre locus or hyperbola $3 x^{2} - 4 x - y^{2} = -1$ by the line $x = 0$.

\medskip
\textbf{89.}\quad For the value $-\dfrac{\kappa}{\mu} = \sqrt{3}$ corresponding to a direction inclined at an angle $= 30^{\circ}$ to the asymptote, the harmonic conic becomes the parabola $(x\sqrt{3} - y)^{2} - 2(x - y\sqrt{3}) = 0$: this equation may also be written $(x - \tfrac{1}{2})^{2} + y^{2} = \tfrac{1}{4}(x + y\sqrt{3} - 1)^{2}$, a form which puts in evidence the focus and directrix of the parabola.

\medskip
\textbf{90.}\quad For a value $-\dfrac{\kappa}{\mu} > \sqrt{3}$, that is, when the satellite line is inclined to the asymptote at an angle $< 30^{\circ}$, the harmonic conic is a hyperbola, and ultimately when $-\dfrac{\kappa}{\mu} = \infty$, or the satellite line is parallel to the asymptote, the harmonic conic becomes the pair of lines $y(1 - x) = 0$.

\textbf{102.}\quad Taking $z = 0$ as the equation of the line infinity, and $x = 0$, $y = 0$ as the equation of any two lines through the point of intersection of the asymptotes, or ``asymptote point,'' then the equation of the cubic may be taken to be

\medskip
\textbf{103.}\quad To determine the critic centres we have
\begin{align*}
(a, b, c \mid x, y)^{2} + k z^{2} \lambda &\;=\; 0,\\
(b, c, d \mid x, y)^{2} + k z^{2} \mu &\;=\; 0,\\
2 z(\lambda x + \mu y + \nu z) + 3 z^{2} \nu &\;=\; 0,
\end{align*}
and thence
\[

\]
or as it may also be written
\[
(\mu a - \lambda b,\; \mu b - \lambda c,\; \mu c - \lambda d \mid x, y)^{2} \;=\; 0,
\]
and also
\[
2(\lambda x + \mu y) - 3 \nu z \;=\; 0,
\]
which two equations determine the critic centres for a given position of the satellite line; the first of them gives a pair of lines through the asymptote point; the latter is a line parallel to the satellite line: there are thus two critic centres.

\medskip
\textbf{104.}\quad The condition for a twofold centre is
\[
(a c - b^{2},\; b c - a d,\; b d - c^{2} \mid \lambda, \mu)^{2} \;=\; 0,
\]

\[
(b c - a d)^{2} - 4(a c - b^{2})(b d - c^{2}) \;=\; +,
\]
imaginary if
\[
(b c - a d)^{2} - 4(a c - b^{2})(b d - c^{2}) \;=\; -,
\]
that is, the twofold centres will be real or imaginary, according as the equation
\[
(a, b, c, d \mid x, y)^{3} \;=\; 0
\]
has its roots one real and two imaginary, or all three real; viz.\ the twofold centres are real for the Hyperbolas $B$ Defective; imaginary for the Hyperbolas $B$ Redundant.

And we see also that for the Hyperbolas $B$ Redundant the critic centres are always real; but that for the Hyperbolas $B$ Defective, they may be both real, or both imaginary, or may coincide together, giving a twofold centre. \textbf{112.}\quad To determine the critic centres we have
\begin{align*}

\end{align*}

\[

\[

\[

\[

\[

\[

\bigskip
\noindent\textit{As to the Division into Species: Comparison of Newton and Pl\"ucker. Article Nos.~121 and 122.}

\medskip
\textbf{121.}\quad The division into species is obtained without difficulty when the groups are once established; in fact it only remains to trace for each given form of $V$ and $s$ the series of curves $V + \mu s = 0$, as $\mu$ passes from $\infty$ to $-\infty$ through the value $0$ and the critic values which correspond to nodal curves: I have nothing to add to what has been done by Pl\"ucker, and it is unnecessary to reproduce the investigation. It may be remarked that the mere inspection of Pl\"ucker's figures is sufficient to show which of his species correspond to the same Newtonian species; the species which do so belong to the same Newtonian species in some instances closely resemble each other in form, although in others the difference of form is apparent enough: but the Pl\"uckerian species which correspond to the same Newtonian species belong for the most part to different groups and are thus distinguished from each other by the characters which distinguish the groups to which they respectively belong: thus for instance Newton's Species 1 (a hyperbola $A$ Redundant) is characterised as consisting of three hyperbolic branches, one inscribed, one circumscribed, and one ambig\`ene, with an oval. Such a curve may exist with three different positions of the satellite line in regard to the asymptotes, viz.\ the satellite line may cut the three sides produced, or it may pass through a vertex, cutting the opposite side produced, or it may cut two sides and the third side produced, not cutting the envelope --- which are the characters of Pl\"ucker's groups I, II, IV, respectively, and there belongs to the Newtonian species 1, a species out of each of these groups, viz.\ they are I.\ 1; II.\ 9, and IV.\ 18.

\medskip
It is to be noticed that (as appears by the Table) Pl\"ucker enumerates 13 species of the Divergent Parabola, viz.\ corresponding to the Parabola Pura of Newton we have five species, and to the Parabola cum Ovali three species; but to each of the other three Newtonian species (Nodata, Punctata, Cuspidata) only a single species. The difference in nowise affects Newton's before-mentioned theorem, that every cubic curve is the shadow of a Divergent Parabola; but (the characters of Pl\"ucker's species being unaffected by projection) the number of resulting kinds of cubic curves (or cones) will be five or thirteen according as the one or the other classification is adopted; but this is a subject which I do not enter upon in the present Memoir.
