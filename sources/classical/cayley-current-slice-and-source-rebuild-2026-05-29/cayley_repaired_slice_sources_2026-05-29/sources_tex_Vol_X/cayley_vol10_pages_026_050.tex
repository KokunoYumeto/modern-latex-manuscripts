\textsc{[638] On a $q$-formula leading to an expression for $E_1$ (conclusion).}\\[0.5em]
[From the \textit{Messenger of Mathematics}, vol.~vi.\ (1877), pp.~63--66; continued from preceding pages.]
\end{center}

\medskip

\noindent (Page 26.) so that
\[
q X'^r = q + 3q^2 + 6q^3 + 10q^4 + \&\text{c.},
\]
then
\[
X + 8qX = 1 + (9 + 25)q^2 + 49q^3 + 81q^4 + \&\text{c.};
\]
so that the right-hand side of $(B)$ is
\[
\frac{X + 8qX'}{X} = -1 + 8q\,\frac{X'}{X}.
\]

But (\textit{Fund.\ Nova}, p.\ 185, \textit{Ges.\ Werke}, t.\ \textsc{i}, p.\ 237),
\[
X = \frac{1 - q^3}{1 - q} \cdot \frac{1 - q^7}{1 - q^5} \cdot \frac{1 - q^{11}}{1 - q^9} \cdots,
\]
so that
\[
\frac{X'}{X} = \frac{-2q}{1 - q^3} - \frac{4q^5}{1 - q^7} - \frac{6q^9}{1 - q^{11}} - \cdots
+ \frac{1}{1 - q} + \frac{3q^5}{1 - q^9} + \frac{5q^9}{1 - q^{13}} + \cdots.
\]

And the equation $(B)$ intended to be proved thus becomes
\begin{align*}
1 + 8 \left\{ \frac{-q}{1 + q} + \frac{2q^2}{1 + q^2} - \frac{3q^3}{1 + q^3} + \cdots \right\} \\
- 16 \left\{ \frac{-q}{1 - q^3} + \frac{2q^2}{1 - q^7} - \frac{3q^3}{1 - q^{11}} + \cdots \right\} \\
= 1 + 8q \left\{ \frac{-2q}{1 - q^3} - \frac{4q^5}{1 - q^7} - \frac{6q^9}{1 - q^{11}} - \cdots \right. \\
\left. + \frac{1}{1 - q} + \frac{3q^5}{1 - q^9} + \frac{5q^9}{1 - q^{13}} + \cdots \right\};
\end{align*}

viz.\ omitting the terms unity, dividing by $8q$, and transposing, this is
\begin{align*}
& -\frac{1}{1 + q} + \frac{2q}{1 + q^2} - \frac{3q^2}{1 + q^3} + \cdots \\
& \quad + \frac{2}{1 - q^3} - \frac{4q}{1 - q^7} + \frac{6q^2}{1 - q^{11}} - \cdots \\
& \quad + \frac{2q}{1 - q^3} + \frac{4q^5}{1 - q^7} + \frac{6q^9}{1 - q^{11}} + \cdots \\
& \quad - \frac{1}{1 - q} - \frac{3q^5}{1 - q^9} - \frac{5q^9}{1 - q^{13}} - \cdots = 0.
\end{align*}

The second and third lines unite together, and the equation becomes
\begin{align*}
& -\frac{1}{1 + q} + \frac{2q}{1 + q^2} - \frac{3q^2}{1 + q^3} + \frac{4q^3}{1 + q^4} - \cdots \\
& \quad + \frac{2}{1 - q^3} + \frac{4q}{1 + q^7} + \frac{6q^2}{1 - q^{11}} + \frac{8q^3}{1 + q^{15}} + \cdots \\
& \quad - \frac{1}{1 - q} - \frac{3q^5}{1 - q^9} - \frac{5q^9}{1 - q^{13}} - \frac{7q^{13}}{1 - q^{17}} - \cdots = 0;
\end{align*}

\bigskip

\noindent (Page 27.) or, collecting and arranging,
\begin{align*}
& -\frac{1}{1 + q} + \frac{2q}{1 + q^2} - \frac{3q^2}{1 + q^3} - \frac{4q^3}{1 + q^4} - \frac{5q^4}{1 + q^5} - \cdots \\
& \qquad + \frac{1}{1 - q} + \frac{3q^2}{1 - q^3} + \frac{5q^4}{1 - q^5} + \cdots = 0,
\end{align*}

an identity which it is easy to verify to any number of terms. But to prove it directly, we have only to add the pairs of terms in the alternate columns; calling the left-hand side $Fq$, we thus obtain
\[
Fq = 2q \left\{ -\frac{1}{1 + q^2} - \frac{2q^2}{1 + q^4} - \frac{3q^4}{1 + q^6} - \cdots
+ \frac{1}{1 - q^2} + \frac{3q^4}{1 - q^6} + \cdots \right\};
\]
viz.\ this equation is $Fq = 2qF(q^2)$; and thence
\[
Fq = 2^n q^{1 + 2 + 4 + \cdots} F(q^{2^n}) = \&\text{c.};
\]

we thus have $Fq = 0$.

The equation $(B)$, or, what is the same thing, the equation $(A)$ is thus proved. Reverting to the equation $(A)$, we have
\[
(1 + 2q + 2q^4 + \cdots)^4 = \frac{4 K^2}{\pi^2}.
\]

\noindent (Jacobi, \textit{Fund.\ Nova}, p.\ 188, \textit{Ges.\ Werke}, t.\ \textsc{i}, p.\ 239),
\[
\left( \frac{q}{1 - q^2} + \frac{2q^2}{1 - q^4} + \cdots \right) = \frac{K^2}{2\pi^2} \left( 1 - \frac{E_1}{K} \right),
\]
\hfill (ib., p.\ 135; ib., p.\ 189),

\noindent if $q = e^{-\frac{\pi K'}{K}}$, and $K$, $E_1$ are the complete functions $F_1k$, $E_1k$.

The left-hand side of the equation is thus
\[
\frac{4 K^2}{\pi^2} - \frac{8 K^2}{\pi^2} \left( 1 - \frac{E_1}{K} \right) = \frac{4 K^2}{\pi^2} \left( -1 + \frac{2 E_1}{K} \right),
\]
and we have
\[
\left( -1 + \frac{2 E_1}{K} \right) = \frac{\pi^2}{4 K^2} \cdot \frac{1 - 9q^2 - 25q^6 + 40q^4 + 81q^{10} - \cdots}{1 - q^2 - q^4 + q^6 + q^{14} - \cdots},
\]
which is a new expression for $E_1$ as a $q$-function. The expression on the right-hand side presents itself, Clebsch, \textit{Theorie der Elasticit\"at} (Leipzig, 1862), p.\ 162, and must have been obtained by him as a value for $\left( -1 + \frac{2 E_1}{K} \right)$; but there is no statement that this is so, nor anything to show how this form of $q$-function was arrived at. Mr Todhunter called my attention to the passage in Clebsch.

\section*{639.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{639. An Elementary Construction in Optics}

\begin{center}
\textbf{AN ELEMENTARY CONSTRUCTION IN OPTICS.}\\[1em]
[From the \textit{Messenger of Mathematics}, vol.~vi.\ (1877), pp.\ 81, 82.]
\end{center}

\medskip

\noindent (Page 28.) \textsc{Consider} two lines meeting at a point $P$, and a point $A$; through $A$, drawn at right angles to $AP$, a line meeting the two lines in the points $U$, $V$ respectively; and through the same point $A$ draw any other line meeting the two lines in the points $U'$, $V'$ respectively; also let the points $u'$, $v'$ be the feet of the perpendiculars let fall from $U'$, $V'$ respectively on the line $UV$; then we have
\[
\frac{1}{Au'} + \frac{1}{Av'} = \frac{1}{AU} + \frac{1}{AV}.
\]

The theorem can be proved at once without any difficulty. It answers to the optical construction, according to which, if $UPV$ represents the path of a ray through a convex lens $AP$, then the thin pencil, axis $U'P$ and centre $U'$, converges after refraction to the point $V'$, where $U'V'$ are in lined with $A$ the centre of the lens; considering as usual the inclinations to the axis as small, we have approximately $AV' = Av'$, $AU' = Au'$, and the theorem is
\[
\frac{1}{AU} + \frac{1}{AV'} = \frac{1}{AU} + \frac{1}{AV},
\]
if $AF$ is the focal length of the lens.

In the original theorem, the line $UV$ need not be at right angles to $AP$, but may be any line whatever; the projecting lines $U'u'$ and $V'v'$ must then be parallel to $AP$, and the theorem remains true.

\section*{641.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{641. On the Flexure of a Spherical Surface}

\begin{center}
\textbf{ON THE FLEXURE OF A SPHERICAL SURFACE.}\\[1em]
[From the \textit{Messenger of Mathematics}, vol.~vi.\ (1877), pp.\ 88--90.]
\end{center}

\medskip

\noindent (Page 30.) \textsc{It} is known that an inextensible spherical surface, or to fix the ideas the spherical quadrilateral included between two arcs of meridian and two arcs of parallel, may be bent in such wise as to be part of a surface of revolution, the meridians and parallels of the spherical surface being meridians and parallels of the new surface, and, moreover, the radius of each parallel of the spherical surface being in the new surface altered in the constant ratio $k$ to $1$. We have, in fact, on the spherical surface, writing $p$ for the latitude and $q$ for the longitude, and the radius being unity,
\[
x = \cos p \cos q,
\]
\[
y = \cos p \sin q,
\]
\[
z = \sin p,
\]
values which give
\[
dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2 = dp^2 + \cos^2 p \, dq^2.
\]
This last equation is satisfied by the values
\[
x = \cos p \sin \tfrac{q}{k},
\]
\[
y = \cos p \sin \tfrac{q}{k},
\]
\[
z = E(k, p),
\]
where $E(k, p) = \int_0^p \sqrt{(1 - k^2 \sin^2 p)} \, dp$, is the elliptic function of the second kind; or rather, this is so when $k < 1$, but the same notation may be used when $k > 1$. These values give the deformation in question.

The two cases to be considered are $k < 1$, and $k > 1$; we take in each case a spherical quadrilateral $ABCD$ (fig.\ 1), bounded by $AB$ (an arc of the equator), the arc of parallel $CD$, and the two arcs of meridian $AD$ and $BC$. In the first case, there is no limit to the latitude $AD$, $= BC$, or taking these $= 90^\circ$, we may in place

\bigskip

\noindent (Page 31.) of the quadrilateral $ABCD$ consider the directangular triangle $ABE$; the new form of this is $A'B'E'$, where the radius $OA'$, $= k \cdot OA$, $= k$, is less than the original radius

\begin{center}
\textsc{Fig.~1.}\\\relax
[Figure: spherical quadrilateral with deformation. Original.]
\end{center}

\noindent unity, but $OE'$, $= E_1k$, is greater than the same radius unity. The surface has at $E'$ a conical point, the semi-aperture of the cone being $= \tan^{-1} \frac{1}{k'}$, if as usual $k' = \sqrt{(1 - k^2)}$; to verify this, writing for convenience $q = 0$, we have for the meridian section $x = \cos p$, $z = E(k, p)$, and thence $\frac{dx}{dz} = -\frac{\sqrt{(1 - k^2 \sin^2 p)}}{\sin p}$, which for $p = 90^\circ$ gives $\frac{dx}{dz} = -k'$. Observe also that for $p = 0$, $\frac{dx}{dz} = \infty$, viz.\ the surface of revolution cuts the plane of the equator at right angles.

There is no limit to the arc $AB$, it may be $= 360^\circ$, viz.\ we must in this case cut the hemisphere along a meridian to allow of the deformation; or it may exceed $360^\circ$, the hemisphere spherical surface being in this case conceived of as wrapping indefinitely over itself, and we may instead of the half lune $EA'B'$, consider the lune included between two meridians extending from pole to pole, and therefore the whole spherical surface, conceived of as wrapping indefinitely over itself; the result is, that this may be deformed into a surface of revolution, which, in its general form, resembles that obtained by the revolution of an arc less than a semi-circle round its chord; the half-chord being greater, and the versed-sine less than the radius of the original sphere.

If $k > 1$, there is obviously a limit to the latitude $AD$, $= BC$, of the spherical quadrilateral; viz.\ this is equal to $\sin^{-1} \frac{1}{k}$. Supposing that in the quadrilateral $ABCD$ (fig.\ 1) the latitude has this limiting value, then (see fig.~2) the new form is $A'B'C''D''$, where along the bounding arc $C''D''$ the tangent plane is horizontal; viz.\ as before $\frac{dx}{dz} = -\frac{\sqrt{(1 - k^2 \sin^2 p)}}{\sin p}$, $= 0$ for $p = \sin^{-1} \frac{1}{k}$. It is to be observed, that the radii for the parallels $A''B''$ and $C''D''$ are $k$ and $k \cos p$ respectively; the difference of

\bigskip

\noindent (Page 32.) these in $k(1 - \cos p)$, which, however great $k$ is, must be less than the arc of meridian $A''D''$, $= p$; substituting for $k$ the value $\frac{1}{\sin p}$, the condition is $\frac{1 - \cos p}{\sin p} < p$, viz.\ this

\begin{center}
\textsc{Fig.~2.}\\\relax
[Figure: bounded surface with deformation; bell-like shape.]
\end{center}

\noindent is $\tan \tfrac{1}{2} p < p$, which is true for every value up to $p = 90^\circ$. But, more than this, we should have
\[
k^2 (1 - \cos p)^2 + E^2(k, p) < p^2,
\]
viz.\ writing as before $k = \frac{1}{\sin p}$, this is
\[
E^2 \left( \frac{1}{\sin p},\ p \right) < p^2 - \tan^2 \tfrac{1}{2} p\,;
\]
this must be true, although (relating as it does to a form of $E$ for which $k$ is greater than $1$) there might be some difficulty in verifying it.

There is, as in the first case, no limit to the value of $AB$, viz.\ this may be $= 360^\circ$, the spherical zone being then cut along a meridian, or it may be greater than $360^\circ$; and, moreover, the spherical quadrilateral may extend south of the equator, but of course so that the limiting south latitude does not extend beyond the foregoing value $\sin^{-1} \frac{1}{k}$; viz.\ we may have a zone between the latitudes $\pm \sin^{-1} \frac{1}{k}$, which may be a complete zone from longitude $0^\circ$ to $360^\circ$ or to any greater value than $360^\circ$. The result is, that the zone is deformed into a surface of revolution, which in its general form resembles that obtained by the revolution of a half-circle or half-ellipse about a line parallel to and beyond its bounding diameter, the bounding half-diameter being less, and the greatest radius of rotation greater, than the radius of the original sphere.

\section*{642.}

\begin{center}
\textbf{ON A DIFFERENTIAL RELATION BETWEEN THE SIDES OF A\\QUADRANGLE.}\\[1em]
[From the \textit{Messenger of Mathematics}, vol.~vi.\ (1877), pp.\ 99--101.]
\end{center}

\medskip

\noindent (Page 33.) \textsc{Let} the sides and diagonals $YZ$, $ZX$, $XY$, $OX$, $OY$, $OZ$ of a quadrangle be $f$, $g$, $h$, $a$, $b$, $c$, and let the component triangles be denoted as follows:
\begin{align*}
A &= \triangle YZO, \quad = (b, c, f), \\
B &= \triangle ZXO, \quad = (c, a, g), \\
C &= \triangle XYO, \quad = (a, b, h), \\
\Omega &= \triangle XYZ, \quad = (f, g, h),
\end{align*}

\begin{center}
[Figure: quadrangle with vertices $X$, $Y$, $Z$, and interior point $O$; subtriangles labelled $A$, $B$, $C$ with $\Omega$ the outer triangle.]
\end{center}

\noindent viz.\ $A$, $B$, $C$, $\Omega$ are the triangles whose sides are $(b, c, f)$, $(c, a, g)$, $(a, b, h)$, $(f, g, h)$ respectively, so that $\Omega = A + B + C$. Then we have between $(a, b, c, f, g, h)$ an equation giving rise to a differential relation, which may be written
\[
\Omega \, (Aa\,da + Bb\,db + Cc\,dc) - (BCff^2 + CAgg^2 + ABh\,dh) = 0.
\]
This may be proved geometrically and analytically. First, for the geometrical proof, it is enough to prove that, when $a$ and $b$ alone vary, the relation between the increments is $Aa\,da + Bb\,db = 0$; for then $a$ and $g$ alone varying, the relation between

\bigskip

\noindent (Page 34.) the increments will be $\Omega ada - Cgdg = 0$ (as to the negative sign it is clear from the figure that $a$, $g$ will increase or diminish together); and we thence at once infer the general relation.
\[
Aada + Bbdb = 0;
\]
or, what is the same thing,
\[
a da : -bdb = XOZ : YOZ.
\]
The points $XYZ$ remain fixed; but $O$ moves through the infinitesimal arc $OO'$, centre $Z$, which may be considered as situate in the right line $OM$ drawn from $O$ at right angles to $ZO$, and meeting $XY$ produced in the point $M$. And then, writing for a moment $\angle OXY = X$, $\angle OYX = Y$, $\angle OMY = M$, we find at once
\[
da = OO' \cos (X + M),
\]
\[
-db = OO' \cos (Y - M);
\]
that is,
\[
\frac{da}{db} = \frac{\cos (X + M)}{\cos (Y - M)},\ \text{or}\ \frac{ada}{bdb} = \frac{a \cos (X + M)}{b \cos (Y - M)}.
\]
But drawing $X\alpha$, $Y\beta$ each of them at right angles to $ZO$, we have $a \cos (X + M) = X\alpha$, $b \cos (Y - M) = Y\beta$, and evidently $XOZ : YOZ = X\alpha : Y\beta$; whence the equation is $\frac{ada}{bdb} = \frac{XOZ}{YOZ}$, which is the required relation.

For the analytical proof, it is to be observed that the relation between $a$, $b$, $c$, $f$, $g$, $h$ is a quadric relation in the quantities $a^2$, $b^2$, $c^2$, $f^2$, $g^2$, $h^2$ respectively; this may be written
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
1 & a^2 & a^4 \\
1 & b^2g^2 + c^2h^2 + a^2f^2 - (b^2 + c^2)g^2h^2 - (g^2 + h^2)b^2c^2 & -(b^2 - c^2)(g^2 - h^2) \\
\nu = & + (b^2 - h^2)(c^2 - g^2) & -b^2 - c^2 - g^2 - h^2 & +1 \\
f^2 & & & \\
f^4 & & & 1
\end{vmatrix} = 0\,;
\]
say for a moment this is $A + Ba^2 + Ca^4 = 0$, where
\begin{align*}
A &= b^2g^4 + b^4g^2 + c^4h^2 + c^2h^4 + f^4(g^2 - h^2)(c^2 - g^2) \\
&\quad - (b^2 + c^2)g^2h^2 - (g^2 + h^2)b^2c^2 + f^2(-b^2 - c^2 - g^2 - h^2) + f^4, \\
B &= -(b^2 - c^2)(g^2 - h^2) + f^2(-b^2 - c^2 - g^2 - h^2) + f^4, \\
C &= 1\,;
\end{align*}
then we have as usual
\[
\frac{da}{d \cdot a^2} a da + \frac{da}{d \cdot a^2} b db + \&\text{c.} = 0,
\]

\bigskip

\noindent (Page 35.) where
\[
4 \frac{da}{d \cdot a^2} = Ca^2 + \tfrac{1}{2} B.
\]
But in virtue of $u = 0$, we have
\[
(Ca^2 + \tfrac{1}{2} B)^2 = C(Ca^4 + Ba^2 + A) + \tfrac{1}{4} (B^2 - 4AC),
\]
that is, $\frac{da}{d \cdot a^2} = \sqrt{(B^2 - 4AC)} \, \cdot$; and hence $B^2 - 4AC$ is a quartic function of $f^2$, which is easily seen to reduce itself to the form
\[
f^2 - (g + h)^2 f^2 - (g - h)^2 f^2 - (b + c)^2 f^2 - (b - c)^2;
\]
The coefficients of $bdb$, $cdc$, $\&$c., are given as expressions of the like form; substituting their values, the differential relation is
\[
\sqrt{(f^2 - (g + h)^2 f^2 - (g - h)^2 f^2 - (b + c)^2 f^2 - (b - c)^2)} \, ada + \&\text{c.} = 0,
\]
which is, in fact, the foregoing result.

It is right to notice that there are in all 16 linear factors,
\begin{align*}
&f + g + h, &\quad &b + c + f, &\quad &c + a + g, &\quad &a + b + h \quad \text{say} & d,\ f,\ g,\ h\,, \\
&-f + g + h, &\quad &-b + c + f, &\quad &-c + a + g, &\quad &-a + b + h &\quad &d',\ f',\ g',\ h'\,, \\
&f - g + h, &\quad &b - c + f, &\quad &c - a + g, &\quad &a - b + h &\quad &d'',\ f'',\ g'',\ h''\,, \\
&f + g - h, &\quad &b + c - f, &\quad &c + a - g, &\quad &a + b - h &\quad &d''',\ f''',\ g''',\ h'''\,;
\end{align*}

and this being so, the coefficients of $ada$, $bdb$, $cdc$, $fdf$, $gdg$, $hdh$, are
\begin{align*}
\sqrt{(dd'd''d''' \cdot ff'f''f''')},\ \sqrt{(gg'g''g'''\cdot hh'h''h''')},\\
\sqrt{(dd'd''d''' \cdot gg'g''g''')},\ \sqrt{(hh'h''h''' \cdot ff'f''f''')},\\
\sqrt{(dd'd''d''' \cdot hh'h''h''')},\ \sqrt{(ff'f''f''' \cdot gg'g''g''')},
\end{align*}
respectively.

We may imagine the quadrilateral $ZOXY$ composed of the four rods $ZO$, $OX$, $XY$, $YZ$ (lengths $c$, $a$, $h$, $f$ as before) jointed together at the angles, and kept in equilibrium by forces $B$, $G$ acting along the diagonals $OY (= b)$, $ZX (= a)$ respectively. We have $c$, $a$, $h$, $f$ given constants, and the relation $\phi(a, b, c, f, g, h) = 0$, which connects the six quantities $b$ and $g$; by what precedes, the differential relation $\phi y$, $dg + \phi \delta$ . $db = 0$ is equivalent to the foregoing four equations equal to the differential $dt$ of a new variable, the system of the equations being expressed by $\delta \delta b + Gdg = 0$; by virtual velocities we have as the condition of equilibrium $B db + G dg = 0$; hence, eliminating $db$, $dg$ we have
\[
\frac{B}{\Omega B b b} = \frac{G}{C A g g},
\]
or, say
\[
\frac{B}{b} = \frac{G}{g} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta X Y O \cdot \Delta Z Y O}} : \frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta X Y Z \cdot \Delta Z X O}},
\]
viz.\ the forces, divided by the diagonals along which they act, are proportional to the reciprocals of the products of the two pairs of triangles which stand on these diagonals respectively. The negative sign shows, what is obvious, that the forces must be, one of them a pull, the other a push.

\section*{643.}

\begin{center}

[From the \textit{Messenger of Mathematics}, vol.~vi.\ (1877), pp.\ 107, 108.]

\medskip

\noindent (Page 36.) \textsc{It} is a known theorem that the branches of a plane curve are even or odd; viz.\ two even branches, or an even and an odd branch (whether of the same curve or of different curves) intersect in an even number of real points; but two odd branches (of the same curve or of different curves) intersect in an odd number of real points*.

In particular, a right line is an odd branch, and hence it meets any even branch of a curve in an even number of real points, and an odd branch in an odd number of real points; or (what is the same thing) an even branch is one which can be drawn throughout) of real points; an odd branch is one which is met by any right line whatever in an odd number of real points.

A curve without nodes has at most one odd branch; for if there were two, one of them being an asymptote (it is by no means certain what has been called a \emph{complete} branch, viz.\ the partial branches which touch an asymptote at its opposite extremities are considered as parts of one and the same branch, and so in other cases. Thus a quadric curve, whether ellipse, parabola, or hyperbola, is one even branch; a cubic curve, in either one odd branch, or else it is an odd branch and an even branch; and generally a curve of an odd order has always an odd branch of odd branches, and a curve of an even order has always an even number of odd branches.

A curve without nodes has at most one odd branch; for if there were two, they would intersect in a real point, which would be a real node on the curve. In particular, a quartic curve having two odd branches must have a real node; this however may be, as in the instance about to be given, an isolated point of infinity.

A simple instance of a quartic curve with two odd branches is that represented by the equation
\[

\]

\]

\section*{647.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{647. On the Quartic Surfaces represented by the Equation, Symmetrical Determinant $= 0$}

\begin{center}
\textbf{ON THE QUARTIC SURFACES REPRESENTED BY THE EQUATION,\\SYMMETRICAL DETERMINANT $= 0$.}\\[1em]
[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.~xiv.\ (1877), pp.\ 46--52.]
\end{center}

\medskip

\noindent (Page 50.) \textsc{Consider} the equation
\[
\nabla = \begin{vmatrix} a, & h, & g, & l \\ h, & b, & f, & m \\ g, & f, & c, & n \\ l, & m, & n, & d \end{vmatrix} = 0,
\]
where for the moment $(a, b, \ldots)$ denote linear functions of the coordinates $(x, y, z, w)$. This is a quartic surface having $10$ nodes; viz.\ if we write $(A, B, \ldots)$ for the first minors of the determinant, then the cubic surfaces $A = 0$, $B = 0$, $\ldots$ have in common $10$ points which are nodes of the quartic surface.

Suppose that $(a, b, c, f, g, h)$ are linear functions of the form $(x, y)$; then, observing that every term of $\nabla$ contains as a factor
\[
\begin{vmatrix} a, & h, & g \\ h, & b, & f \\ g, & f, & c \end{vmatrix},
\]
or one of its first minors, it is clear that the line $x = 0$, $y = 0$ is a double line on the surface. But the number of nodes is now less than $10$; in fact, writing $(x = 0, y = 0)$, we make each of the first minors $A = 0$, $B = 0$, \ldots\ have in common $10$ points which are nodes of the quartic surface, which is common to the line $(x = 0, y = 0)$, and there is a diminution in the number of their common intersections. I do not pursue the enquiry, but pass to a different question.
