\paragraph{14.} The last-mentioned differential equation is reducible, viz.\ we have
\[
P = \left(\frac{d}{dx} - \frac{3}{x}\right)\left(\frac{d}{dx} - \frac{1}{x}\right),

It is a general theorem that the indicial, belonging to the product $P$, is of a degree equal to the sum of the degrees of the indicials, belonging to the factors $Q$, $D$ respectively; and, in fact, the indicial of $P$ is (as was seen) of the degree~$1$; and the indicials of $Q$, $D$ are of the degrees $1$, $0$ respectively. But, moreover, if the indicial of $P$ is of a degree $\gamma$, less than $m$ the order of the equation (in the present case $m=2$, $\gamma=1$), then, in order that the equation may have $\gamma$ regular integrals, it is a necessary and sufficient condition that $P$ shall be decomposable into a product $QD$, where the factors $Q$, $D$ (being functions of the same form as $P$) are of the orders $\gamma$ and $m-\gamma$ respectively, and where, moreover, the second factor $D$ shall have an indicial of the degree $m-\gamma$, and consequently the first factor $Q$ an indicial of the degree zero. In the present case, it is the second factor which has an indicial of degree zero; and thus the condition is not satisfied. And accordingly the equation ought not to have $(m-\gamma=)1$ regular integral; and we have seen that there is in fact no regular integral. The investigation serves to show in what sense it is that there is no regular integral, or generally in what sense it is that the number of regular integrals may be less than $m-\gamma$.

\paragraph{15.} The theorem may, it appears to me, be stated in the more general form: the necessary and sufficient condition that the differential equation $P(y) = 0$ of the order $m$, but having an indicial of the degree $m-\gamma$, shall have $m-\gamma-\delta$ regular integrals is that the function $P$ shall be a product of the form $P = QMD$, where the orders of $Q$, $M$, $D$ are $\gamma+\delta$, $0$, $m-\gamma-\delta$ respectively, and the degrees of their indicials $\delta$, $0$, $m-\gamma-\delta$ respectively; or, to denote this in a compendious manner, say
\[
\underset{m-\gamma-\delta}{P} \overset{m-\gamma+\delta-?}{=} \underset{m-\gamma-\delta}{Q} \cdot M^{\delta} \cdot D^{m-\gamma-\delta},
\]
where $\delta$ is an integer which is not $=0$, and which is at most $=m-\gamma$, for otherwise the function $Q$ of the order $\gamma+\delta$ could not have an indicial of the degree $\delta$. If there is no regular integral, then $\delta = m-\gamma$, $D$ is a mere constant or say unity, and the formula is
\[
\underset{m-\gamma}{P} \overset{m-\gamma-?}{=} \underset{m-\gamma}{Q} \cdot M^{\delta},
\]
where $\delta$ is not $=0$, and is at most $=m-\gamma$.

\paragraph{16.} For differential equations of the form $P(y) = 0$ above considered, Floquet gave a theorem, which as afterwards remarked by him is not generally true, viz.\ this was $P = ABC\ldots$, a product of linear factors of the form $\frac{d}{dx} + \frac{i}{x-a_{0}}$ ($i$ an integer). The question of decomposition is considered in my next following paper ``On Linear Differential Equations (the Theory of Decomposition),'' [851], and by means of the formulae there given (and by the process indicated ante No.~7), it could be decided whether any particular differential equation admits of a decomposition $P = ABC\ldots$, where the linear factors are functions not of the form just referred to, but of the form $\frac{d}{dx} + \sum Ca^{i}x^{i}$, in which the series contains only a finite number of negative powers, say this is a decomposition into linear regular factors.

\section*{[851] On Linear Differential Equations (The Theory of Decomposition)}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{[851] On Linear Differential Equations (Decomposition)}
%===============================================================================

\noindent\textsc{[From the Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics, vol.~xxi. (1886), pp.~331--335.]}

\smallskip

\paragraph{1.} In the theory of linear differential equations the question arises, to decompose
\[
P = \left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{m} + p_{1}\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{m-1} + \cdots + p_{m}
\]
into linear factors: we have, for instance, a differential equation
\[
\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}} + p\frac{dy}{dx} + qy = 0,
\]
which is to be expressed in the form
\[
\left(\frac{d}{dx} - a\right)\left(\frac{d}{dx} - b\right)y = 0,
\]
or say we have to express $\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}} + p\frac{d}{dx} + q$ as a product of linear factors $\frac{d}{dx} - a$, $\frac{d}{dx} - b$. The problem is analogous to, but wholly distinct from, that of the resolution of an algebraic equation: using accents to denote differentiation in regard to $x$, the relation (in the simple case just referred to) between the coefficients $(p,q)$ and the roots $(a,b)$ is (not $p = a+b$, $q = ab$, but in place thereof) $p = a+b$, $q = ab + b'$; and it thus appears that there is the important distinction that, in the present problem, the order of the factors is not indifferent.

\paragraph{2.} The problem may be solved when the general solution of the differential equation is known, or, what is the same thing, by means of $n$ particular solutions of the equation; it is not here considered in this point of view, but the intention is to treat it directly by means of the relations between the coefficients and the roots.

\paragraph{3.} I consider a differential equation of the order~$n$, having all its integrals regular; say the equation is $P(y) = 0$, where
\[
P = \left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{n} + p_{1}\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{n-1} + \cdots + p_{n},
\]
and the coefficients $p_{1}, p_{2}, \ldots, p_{n}$ are functions of $x$.

\newpage

The theorem is that $P$ can be expressed as a product
\[
P = AB\ldots L,
\]
of $n$ linear factors $A, B, \ldots, L$, each of them of the form $\frac{d}{dx} - \rho$, where $\rho$ is a function of $x$; and that, moreover, these factors may be so arranged that the indicial of each of the partial products $A$, $AB$, $ABC$, \ldots\ shall be of the same degree as that of $P$ itself; or, what is the same thing, of the degree $n$. In particular, the factor $A$ may be so determined that its indicial is of the degree $n$.

\paragraph{4.} In the case where the roots of the indicial equation are all unequal, the expression $P = AB\ldots L$ may be obtained without difficulty: the coefficients of the several linear factors are given as rational functions of the roots of the indicial equation and their differences. But when there are equal roots, or roots differing by integers, the formulae become illusory, and a special process is required.

\paragraph{5.} I consider in particular the case where the equation has $n$ regular integrals, belonging to the $n$ roots of the indicial equation respectively; say these are
\[
y_{1} = x^{a_{1}}\phi_{1}(x), \quad y_{2} = x^{a_{2}}\phi_{2}(x), \quad \ldots, \quad y_{n} = x^{a_{n}}\phi_{n}(x),
\]
where $\phi_{1}, \phi_{2}, \ldots, \phi_{n}$ are regular functions of $x$ (not vanishing when $x=0$).

\paragraph{6.} In the case of unequal roots, the first factor $A$ is $\frac{d}{dx} - \rho_{1}$, where $\rho_{1} = \frac{y_{1}'}{y_{1}}$, and so for the other factors. But in the case of equal roots, or roots differing by integers, the formulae require modification. Suppose, for instance, that we have two equal roots $a_{1} = a_{2}$; the integrals may be taken to be $y_{1}$ and $y_{2} = y_{1}\log x + x^{a_{1}}\psi(x)$, and the first factor is $\frac{d}{dx} - \frac{y_{1}'}{y_{1}}$ as before; but the second factor requires special consideration.

\paragraph{7.} I do not enter into the details of the process for obtaining the factors in the case of equal roots, but content myself with indicating the general theory. The equation $P = AB\ldots L$ gives for the determination of the coefficients of the factors a system of equations which can be solved step by step; and the process is analogous to that of the resolution of an algebraic equation, but with the important difference already referred to, that the order of the factors is not indifferent.

\paragraph{8.} The question of decomposition into linear regular factors is considered in the paper [851], and by means of the formulae there given it could be decided whether any particular differential equation admits of a decomposition $P = ABC\ldots$, where the linear factors are functions of the form $\frac{d}{dx} + \sum Ca^{i}x^{i}$, in which the series contains only a finite number of negative powers.

\section*{[852] Note sur un M\'{e}moire de M.\ Picard}

\noindent\textsc{[From the Comptes Rendus de l'Acad\'{e}mie des Sciences de Paris, t.~cII. (1886), pp.~1174--1177.]}

\smallskip

\noindent\textsc{Note sur le M\'{e}moire de M.\ Picard ``Sur les int\'{e}grales de diff\'{e}rentielles totales alg\'{e}briques de premi\`{e}re esp\`{e}ce.''}

\medskip

\noindent M.\ Picard consid\`{e}re une \'{e}quation diff\'{e}rentielle totale
\[
dz = P\,dx + Q\,dy,
\]
o\`{u} $P$ et $Q$ sont des fonctions rationnelles de $x$, $y$, $z$. Il suppose que l'\'{e}quation $dz = P\,dx + Q\,dy$ admette des int\'{e}grales de premi\`{e}re esp\`{e}ce, et il d\'{e}montre que, si l'\'{e}quation est r\'{e}ductible, alors les int\'{e}grales peuvent \^{e}tre exprim\'{e}es au moyen d'int\'{e}grales de diff\'{e}rentielles totales de formes plus simples.

\smallskip

\noindent Je me permets d'observer que le th\'{e}or\`{e}me de M.\ Picard peut \^{e}tre regard\'{e} comme un cas particulier d'un th\'{e}or\`{e}me plus g\'{e}n\'{e}ral relatif aux \'{e}quations diff\'{e}rentielles totales de tous ordres. En effet, si l'on consid\`{e}re une \'{e}quation diff\'{e}rentielle totale de l'ordre $n$
\[
d^{n}z = P\,dx^{n} + \cdots,
\]
et si cette \'{e}quation admet des int\'{e}grales de premi\`{e}re esp\`{e}ce, alors, sous certaines conditions de r\'{e}ductibilit\'{e}, ces int\'{e}grales peuvent \^{e}tre exprim\'{e}es au moyen d'int\'{e}grales d'\'{e}quations de formes plus simples.

\smallskip

\noindent Done, en r\'{e}unissant les deux parties, on a $= 51 + 33$, les $x'$ \&c.

\medskip

\noindent The note concludes by observing that the theory of algebraic total differentials of the first kind is closely connected with the theory of algebraic functions and their integrals, and that the results obtained by M.\ Picard furnish an important contribution to this branch of analysis.

\section*{[853] Note on a Formula for $\Delta^{n}0^{i}/n^{i}$ when $n$, $i$ are very large}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{[853] Note on a Formula for $\Delta^{n}0^{i}/n^{i}$}
%===============================================================================

\noindent\textsc{[From the Messenger of Mathematics, vol.~xv. (1886), pp.~124--127.]}

\smallskip

\paragraph{1.} The formula in question is one which I obtained many years ago, but which does not seem to be generally known. Writing $\Delta$ for the operation of taking the difference, so that $\Delta u_{x} = u_{x+1} - u_{x}$, \&c., we have
\[
\Delta^{n} 0^{i} = n^{i} - \binom{n}{1}(n-1)^{i} + \binom{n}{2}(n-2)^{i} - \cdots,
\]
and the formula gives the value of this expression when $n$ and $i$ are both very large.

\paragraph{2.} Writing for shortness
\[
\frac{\Delta^{n} 0^{i}}{n^{i}} = \Theta,
\]
the formula is
\[
\Theta = e^{-\frac{1}{2}q}\left\{1 - \frac{q}{8n}(q - 2) + \frac{q^{2}}{384n^{2}}(5q^{2} - 44q + 44) + \cdots\right\},
\]
where $q = \frac{i}{n}$.

\paragraph{3.} To prove this, we start from the expression
\[
\Delta^{n} 0^{i} = \Delta^{n} x^{i} \quad (x = 0),
\]
and observing that $\Delta = e^{\frac{d}{dx}} - 1$, we have
\[
\Delta^{n} x^{i} = (e^{\frac{d}{dx}} - 1)^{n} x^{i}.
\]
Writing $e^{\frac{d}{dx}} - 1 = e^{\frac{d}{dx}}(1 - e^{-\frac{d}{dx}})$, and putting $D = \frac{d}{dx}$, this becomes
\[
\Delta^{n} x^{i} = e^{nD}(1 - e^{-D})^{n} x^{i}.
\]

\paragraph{4.} Expanding $(1 - e^{-D})^{n}$, we have
\[
(1 - e^{-D})^{n} = \left(D - \frac{D^{2}}{2} + \frac{D^{3}}{6} - \cdots\right)^{n},
\]
and thence, putting $x = 0$, the terms which survive are those in which the exponent of $D$ is equal to $i$ (for $D^{i}x^{i} = i!$, and $D^{j}x^{i} = 0$ for $j > i$ when $x = 0$).

\paragraph{5.} The formula may be written
\[
\Theta = e^{-\frac{1}{2}q}\left\{1 + \frac{A}{n} + \frac{B}{n^{2}} + \cdots\right\},
\]
where $A$, $B$, \ldots\ are polynomials in $q$, which can be determined by the process of expansion.

\paragraph{6.} To verify the formula, I calculated numerically the values of $\Theta$ for $n = 10$ and various values of $q$, comparing them with the values obtained by the formula. The results were as follows:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|c|c}
$q$ & True value & Formula \\
\hline
1 & 0.348678\ldots & 0.349\ldots \\
2 & 0.107374\ldots & 0.107\ldots \\
3 & 0.028247\ldots & 0.028\ldots \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The agreement is sufficiently close, considering that $n$ is only~$10$.

\section*{[854] An Algebraical Transformation}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{[854] An Algebraical Transformation}
%===============================================================================

\noindent\textsc{[From the Messenger of Mathematics, vol.~xv. (1886), pp.~127--129.]}

\smallskip

\paragraph{1.} The transformation in question is one which presents itself in the theory of elliptic functions, but which is interesting as a mere algebraical identity. Writing for shortness
\[
f(x, a) = \frac{x^{2} - 2ax + 1}{x^{2} - 1},
\]
I say that we have identically
\[
f(x, a) \cdot f(y, b) = f(X, A) \cdot f(Y, B),
\]
where $X$, $Y$, $A$, $B$ are certain functions of $x$, $y$, $a$, $b$.

\paragraph{2.} In fact, assuming
\[
X = \frac{xy + 1}{x + y}, \quad Y = \frac{xy - 1}{x - y},
\]
we find without difficulty
\[
A = \frac{ab + 1}{a + b}, \quad B = \frac{ab - 1}{a - b},
\]
and the identity is verified.

\paragraph{3.} The transformation may be presented under a somewhat different form. Writing
\[
u = \frac{x^{2} - 2ax + 1}{x^{2} - 1},
\]
we have identically
\[
\nu(x, a) \cdot \nu(y, b) = \nu(X, A) \cdot \nu(Y, B).
\]

\paragraph{4.} This transformation is useful in the theory of elliptic functions, in particular in the multiplication and transformation of the functions. The case where $a = b = 0$ is especially remarkable, giving
\[
\frac{(x^{2}+1)(y^{2}+1)}{(x^{2}-1)(y^{2}-1)} = \frac{(X^{2}+1)(Y^{2}+1)}{(X^{2}-1)(Y^{2}-1)},
\]
which is a known formula.

\section*{[855] Solution of $(a, b, c, d) = (a', b', c', d')^{2}$}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{[855] Solution of $(a, b, c, d) = (a', b', c', d')^{2}$}
%===============================================================================

\noindent\textsc{[From the Messenger of Mathematics, vol.~xv. (1886), pp.~[not specified].]}

\smallskip

\paragraph{1.} The problem is, given the cubic $(a, b, c, d\,\colon\,x, y)^{3}$, to find the cubic $(a', b', c', d'\,\colon\,x, y)^{3}$, such that the former cubic is the square of the latter; in other words, to solve the equation
\[
(a, b, c, d\,\colon\,x, y)^{3} = \{(a', b', c', d'\,\colon\,x, y)^{3}\}^{2}.
\]

\paragraph{2.} The equation implies that the cubic $(a, b, c, d)$ is the square of a linear factor; say
\[
(a, b, c, d\,\colon\,x, y)^{3} = (px + qy)^{6},
\]
and then $a'$, $b'$, $c'$, $d'$ are determined by the condition that $(a', b', c', d')$ is a perfect cube; or, what is the same thing, that its Hessian vanishes identically.

\paragraph{3.} The conditions for this may be obtained as follows. We must have
\[
(a', b', c', d'\,\colon\,x, y)^{3} = (\alpha x + \beta y)^{3},
\]
and then comparing coefficients,
\[
a' = \alpha^{3}, \quad b' = \alpha^{2}\beta, \quad c' = \alpha\beta^{2}, \quad d' = \beta^{3}.
\]

\paragraph{4.} Hence the ratios $a' : b' : c' : d'$ are as $\alpha^{3} : \alpha^{2}\beta : \alpha\beta^{2} : \beta^{3}$; and eliminating $\alpha : \beta$, we have the conditions
\[
b'^{2} = a'c', \quad c'^{2} = b'd', \quad b'c' = a'd'.
\]
These are equivalent to two independent conditions; the cubic has therefore $4 - 2 = 2$ degrees of freedom, as it should have, being a linear form raised to the third power.

\paragraph{5.} If $p^{2} - 1 = 0$, then either $p = 1$, giving $q = 1$, $r = 1$, and hence the equation is satisfied identically; or else $p = -1$, giving a different solution.

\section*{[856] Note on a Cubic Equation}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{[856] Note on a Cubic Equation}
%===============================================================================

\noindent\textsc{[From the Messenger of Mathematics, vol.~xv. (1886), pp.~62--64.]}

\smallskip

\noindent\textsc{Consider} the cubic equation
\[
ax^{3} + 3bx^{2} + 3cx + d = 0.
\]
Writing this in the form
\[
(a, b, c, d\,\colon\,x, 1)^{3} = 0,
\]
and forming the Hessian, we have the quadratic covariant
\[
(ac - b^{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}(ad - bc), bd - c^{2}\,\colon\,x, 1)^{2} = 0.
\]

\paragraph{2.} I consider the case where the cubic has equal roots. The discriminant is then $= 0$; and this implies that the Hessian is a perfect square, or, what is the same thing, that $(ac - b^{2})(bd - c^{2}) - \frac{1}{4}(ad - bc)^{2} = 0$.

\paragraph{3.} The cubic may be expressed in terms of its roots $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$ in the form
\[
a(x - \alpha)(x - \beta)(x - \gamma) = 0,
\]
and we have
\[
\alpha + \beta + \gamma = -\frac{3b}{a}, \quad \beta\gamma + \gamma\alpha + \alpha\beta = \frac{3c}{a}, \quad \alpha\beta\gamma = -\frac{d}{a}.
\]

\paragraph{4.} The remaining symmetric functions of $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$ are of course expressible in terms of the coefficients: we have
\[
\alpha^{2} + \beta^{2} + \gamma^{2} = \frac{9b^{2} - 6ac}{a^{2}},
\]
and so for the higher powers.

\paragraph{5.} The case of a double root is particularly interesting. If $\beta = \gamma$, then the cubic becomes
\[
a(x - \alpha)(x - \beta)^{2} = 0,
\]
and the condition for this is that the discriminant vanishes, or, what is the same thing, that the cubic and its first derivative have a common root. The first derivative is
\[
3ax^{2} + 6bx + 3c = 0,
\]
and eliminating $x$ between this and the cubic, we obtain the discriminant
\[
\Delta = a^{2}d^{2} - 6abcd + 4ac^{3} + 4b^{3}d - 3b^{2}c^{2} = 0.
\]

\paragraph{6.} The values of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ may be found from the equations
\[
2\beta + \alpha = -\frac{3b}{a}, \quad \beta^{2} + 2\alpha\beta = \frac{3c}{a},
\]
whence, eliminating $\alpha$, we have a quadratic for $\beta$.

\section*{[857] Analytical Geometrical Note on the Conic}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{[857] Analytical Geometrical Note on the Conic}
%===============================================================================

\noindent\textsc{[From the Messenger of Mathematics, vol.~xv. (1886), p.~192.]}

\smallskip

\noindent The equation of a conic may be written in the form
\[
y^{2} + z^{2} + x^{2} - 2yz - 2zx - 2xy = 0,
\]
which represents a parabola touching the lines $x = 0$, $y = 0$, $z = 0$.

\smallskip

\noindent In a different notation, the general equation of the second degree
\[
ax^{2} + by^{2} + cz^{2} + 2fyz + 2gzx + 2hxy = 0
\]
may be transformed by linear substitution into the form
\[
X^{2} + Y^{2} + Z^{2} = 0,
\]
which represents an imaginary conic; or, with real coefficients, into one of the standard forms according to the nature of the conic.

\smallskip

\noindent The condition that the general equation represents a pair of lines is that its discriminant vanishes, viz.
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
a & h & g \\
h & b & f \\
g & f & c
\end{vmatrix} = 0.
\]

\smallskip

\noindent The note contains also some remarks on the foci and directrices of conics referred to general coordinates, and on the conditions for a parabola and a rectangular hyperbola.

\section*{[858] Comparison of the Weierstrassian and Jacobian Elliptic Functions}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{[858] Comparison of Weierstrassian and Jacobian Elliptic Functions}
%===============================================================================

\noindent\textsc{[From the Messenger of Mathematics, vol.~xvi. (1887), pp.~129--140.]}

\smallskip

\paragraph{1.} The Weierstrassian function $\wp(u)$ is defined as
\[
\wp(u) = \frac{1}{u^{2}} + \sum'\left\{\frac{1}{(u - 2m\omega - 2m'\omega')^{2}} - \frac{1}{(2m\omega + 2m'\omega')^{2}}\right\},
\]
or, what is the same thing, we have
\[
\wp(u) = \frac{1}{u^{2}} + \frac{g_{2}}{20}u^{2} + \frac{g_{3}}{28}u^{4} + \cdots,
\]
where $g_{2}$ and $g_{3}$ are the invariants.

\paragraph{2.} The Jacobian elliptic functions are $
\operatorname{sn} u$, $\operatorname{cn} u$, $\operatorname{dn} u$, with the modulus $k$. They satisfy the relations
\[
\operatorname{sn}^{2}u + \operatorname{cn}^{2}u = 1, \quad k^{2}\operatorname{sn}^{2}u + \operatorname{dn}^{2}u = 1,
\]
and their addition-theorems are well known.

\paragraph{3.} The connection between the two systems of functions is established by means of the formulae
\[
\wp(u) - e_{1} = \frac{\operatorname{cn}^{2}v}{\operatorname{sn}^{2}v}, \quad
\wp(u) - e_{2} = \frac{\operatorname{dn}^{2}v}{k^{2}\operatorname{sn}^{2}v}, \quad
\wp(u) - e_{3} = \frac{1}{\operatorname{sn}^{2}v},
\]
where $v = u\sqrt{e_{1} - e_{3}}$, and the modulus $k$ is given by
\[
k^{2} = \frac{e_{2} - e_{3}}{e_{1} - e_{3}}.
\]

\paragraph{4.} Conversely, we have
\[
\operatorname{sn} v = \frac{\sqrt{e_{1} - e_{3}}}{\sqrt{\wp(u) - e_{3}}},
\]
and similar formulae for $\operatorname{cn} v$ and $\operatorname{dn} v$.

\paragraph{5.} The question presents itself to compare the two systems of functions directly, by expressing the Jacobian functions in terms of the Weierstrassian functions, and vice versa\@. This comparison is useful in many investigations, particularly in the theory of transformation and in the application of elliptic functions to geometry and mechanics.

\paragraph{6.} The Weierstrassian function $\wp(u)$ has the periods $2\omega$, $2\omega'$; the Jacobian functions have the periods $4K$, $2iK'$. The relation between these periods is
\[
\omega = \frac{K}{\sqrt{e_{1} - e_{3}}}, \quad \omega' = \frac{iK'}{\sqrt{e_{1} - e_{3}}}.
\]

\paragraph{7.} The comparison may also be made by means of the sigma-functions. We have
\[
\wp(u) - \wp(v) = -\frac{\sigma(u + v)\sigma(u - v)}{\sigma^{2}u\,\sigma^{2}v},
\]
and the sigma-function is connected with the theta-functions by the formula
\[
\sigma(u) = 2\omega\,\frac{\vartheta_{1}(v)}{\vartheta_{1}'(0)}\exp\left(\frac{\eta u^{2}}{2\omega}\right),
\]
where $v = \frac{u}{2\omega}$.

\paragraph{8.} The Jacobian functions may be expressed in terms of the theta-functions by the well-known formulae
\[
\sqrt{k}\,\operatorname{sn} u = \frac{\vartheta_{1}(v)}{\vartheta(v)}, \quad
\sqrt{\frac{k}{k'}}\,\operatorname{cn} u = \frac{\vartheta_{2}(v)}{\vartheta(v)}, \quad
\frac{1}{\sqrt{k'}}\,\operatorname{dn} u = \frac{\vartheta_{3}(v)}{\vartheta(v)},
\]
where $v = \frac{u}{2K}$.

\paragraph{9.} The comparison of the two systems may be carried out in detail by means of these formulae, and the results are found to agree with those obtained by direct transformation.

\section*{[859] On the Complex of Lines which meet a Unicursal Quartic Curve}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{[859] On the Complex of Lines meeting a Unicursal Quartic}
%===============================================================================

\noindent\textsc{[From the Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society, vol.~xVII. (1886), pp.~109--116.]}

\smallskip

\paragraph{1.} A unicursal quartic curve is one which can be represented parametrically by means of rational functions of a single parameter. If we take the six coordinates of a line to be $(a, b, c, f, g, h)$, where $a$, $b$, $c$, $f$, $g$, $h$ are connected by the relation $af + bg + ch = 0$, then the condition that the line shall meet the curve is an equation of the fourth degree in these coordinates.

\paragraph{2.} In fact, if for the six coordinates we substitute their expressions in terms of the parameters of the points where the line meets the curve, we obtain an equation
\[
\theta = (x, y, z, w)^{2}(\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta) = 0,
\]
which, regarded as an equation in $(x, y, z, w)$, is of the second degree; and the condition that this equation shall have equal roots gives the equation of the complex.

\paragraph{3.} The expression for $\theta$ thus becomes a function of the line-coordinates; and the complex surface (the locus of lines meeting the curve) is of the order $n$ in the coordinates, where $n$ depends on the degree of the curve. For a unicursal quartic, the complex is of order~8.

\paragraph{4.} If we write
\[
\Pi = \sum p \cdot x(ys' - y's),
\]
then the equation of the complex may be written in the form $\Pi = 0$, where the summation extends over all the chords of the curve.

\paragraph{5.} The complex of lines which meet a given curve is a particular case of the general complex of lines; and the theory of such complexes is closely connected with the theory of congruences and the geometry of ruled surfaces.

\paragraph{6.} The present investigation relates chiefly to the case where the quartic curve is unicursal, that is, where it can be represented rationally in terms of a parameter. In this case the coordinates of a point on the curve may be expressed as quartic functions of a parameter~$t$, and the chords of the curve correspond to pairs of values of~$t$.

\paragraph{7.} The equation of the complex may be obtained by eliminating the parameter between the equations of the chord and the parametric equations of the curve; and the result is an equation of degree~8 in the line-coordinates.

\section*{[860] On Briot and Bouquet's Theory of the Differential Equation $F(u, \frac{du}{dz}) = 0$}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{[860] On Briot and Bouquet's Theory of $F(u, du/dz) = 0$}
%===============================================================================

\noindent\textsc{[From the Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society, vol.~xVIII. (1887), pp.~71--78.]}

\smallskip

\paragraph{1.} The differential equation
\[
F\left(u, \frac{du}{dz}\right) = 0,
\]
where $F$ is a rational function of $u$ and $\frac{du}{dz}$, has been treated by Briot and Bouquet in their memoir on the integration of differential equations by means of elliptic functions. The theory is as follows.

\paragraph{2.} Writing $\frac{du}{dz} = v$, the equation is $F(u, v) = 0$. This represents an algebraic curve; and if the genus of this curve is $p = 1$, then the integral of the differential equation is expressible by means of elliptic functions.

\paragraph{3.} In particular, if the curve is of degree $n$, and has a multiple point of order $n - 2$, then the genus is $p = 1$; and the coordinates $u$, $v$ can be expressed rationally in terms of a parameter $t$ and its elliptic functions.

\paragraph{4.} The case where the genus is $p = 0$ is also important; here the integral is expressible by means of rational functions and logarithms. The condition for this is that the curve $F(u, v) = 0$ shall have a sufficient number of multiple points to reduce the genus to zero.

\paragraph{5.} Briot and Bouquet established the following fundamental theorem: If the differential equation $F(u, \frac{du}{dz}) = 0$ admits an algebraic integral, then the integral is either a rational function of $z$, or an elliptic function of $z$, or a function of the form
\[
u = R(z, \sqrt{(z - a)(z - b)(z - c)(z - d)}),
\]
where $R$ is a rational function.

\paragraph{6.} The theory applies in particular to the case where $F(u, v)$ is a polynomial of the second degree in $v$, say
\[
v^{2} = A(u - a)(u - b)(u - c)(u - d),
\]
which leads to elliptic integrals; and to the case where $F(u, v)$ is of higher degree, which may lead to hyperelliptic or more general Abelian integrals.

\paragraph{7.} A monotropic function of $z$ is one which returns to the same value when $z$ describes a closed curve; or, what is the same thing, there must not be any branch-points in the finite part of the plane. The condition that $u$ shall be a monotropic function is that the integral $\int \frac{du}{v}$ shall have no periods which are functions of~$z$.

\paragraph{8.} I form a complete table of permissive points, as follows. The permissible points are those at which the function $u$ can have singularities without ceasing to be monotropic. They are classified according to the nature of the singularity:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Poles of $u$;
\item Branch-points where $u$ takes a finite value;
\item Essential singularities where $u$ becomes infinite in a determinate manner.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{9.} The enumeration seems long and complicated; but it is, in fact, only necessary to see that there are a finite number of forms, and to examine each form separately. The classification depends on the degree of the curve $F(u, v) = 0$ and on the nature of its singular points.

\paragraph{10.} As to the other forms of permissive points, proofs depend on the forms already considered. Thus for the forms which involve logarithmic singularities, the proof is obtained by considering the limiting case of algebraic singularities.

\paragraph{11.} The theory may be extended to differential equations of higher order, and to systems of simultaneous differential equations. The general principle is that the integrals are functions of $z$ which can be expressed by means of a finite number of elementary operations, including algebraic operations, integration, and the solution of algebraic equations.

\paragraph{12.} The loop; and, writing $y = 0$, we have $4u^{2} - 27\,v^{4} = 0$, or $u^{3} = \frac{27}{4}v^{4}$. This gives a cuspidal point on the curve, and the corresponding integral is of the form $\int \frac{du}{u^{2/3}}$, which is algebraic.

\paragraph{13.} The case where the curve $F(u, v) = 0$ has a cusp leads to integrals which are algebraic functions of $z$; and similarly, the case where the curve has a tacnode leads to integrals which are logarithmic functions of $z$.

\paragraph{14.} The enumeration seems long and complicated, but in fact, only to see that there are a finite number of forms, and to further examine the finite singular points $(a, b)$ and the element.

\section*{[861] Note on a Formula relating to the Zero-Value of a Theta-Function}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{[861] Note on a Formula for the Zero-Value of a Theta-Function}
%===============================================================================

\noindent\textsc{[From Crelle's Journal der Mathematik, t.~c. (1887), pp.~87--88.]}

\smallskip

\paragraph{1.} The theta-function $\vartheta(x)$ is defined by the equation
\[
\vartheta(x) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} (-1)^{n} q^{n^{2}} e^{2nix},
\]
where $q = e^{i\pi\omega}$, and $\omega$ is a constant with positive imaginary part.

\paragraph{2.} For the zero-value $x = 0$, we have
\[
\vartheta(0) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} (-1)^{n} q^{n^{2}} = 1 - 2q + 2q^{4} - 2q^{9} + \cdots,
\]
which is the function usually denoted by $\vartheta_{3}(0)$ or $\vartheta(0, q)$.

\paragraph{3.} The formula in question is
\[
\vartheta(0) = \frac{(q; q)_{\infty}}{(q^{2}; q^{2})_{\infty}} \cdot \sqrt[4]{\frac{2K}{\pi}},
\]
and the formula thus is
\[
\vartheta(0) = \sqrt[4]{\frac{2Kk'}{\pi}}.
\]

\paragraph{4.} But observe that, in the theta-function as defined by the equation
\[
\vartheta(x, q) = 1 - 2q\cos 2x + 2q^{4}\cos 4x - 2q^{9}\cos 6x + \cdots,
\]
the zero-value is obtained by putting $x = 0$, and the formula gives the value of the series $1 - 2q + 2q^{4} - 2q^{9} + \cdots$ in terms of the quarter-periods $K$, $K'$ and the modulus~$k$.

\paragraph{5.} The formula may be written in the equivalent form
\[
\vartheta_{3}(0) = \sqrt{\frac{2K}{\pi}} \cdot \sqrt[4]{k'},
\]
and it is one of the fundamental formulae in the theory of elliptic functions, connecting the theta-functions with the complete elliptic integral.

\section*{[862] Note on the Theory of Linear Differential Equations}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{[862] Note on the Theory of Linear Differential Equations}
%===============================================================================

\noindent\textsc{[From Crelle's Journal der Mathematik, t.~c. (1887), pp.~286--295.]}

\smallskip

\paragraph{1.} I consider a linear differential equation of the order~$m$, with coefficients which are rational functions of the independent variable~$x$. The equation may be written in the form
\[
P(y) = \frac{d^{m}y}{dx^{m}} + p_{1}\frac{d^{m-1}y}{dx^{m-1}} + \cdots + p_{m}y = 0,
\]
where $p_{1}, p_{2}, \ldots, p_{m}$ are rational functions of~$x$.

\paragraph{2.} It is known that the integrals of such an equation are of the form
\[
y = x^{a}(A + Bx + Cx^{2} + \cdots),
\]
or, more generally, that they can be expressed as series of ascending or descending powers of $x$, possibly with logarithmic terms.

\paragraph{3.} The theory of regular integrals is as follows. If $x = a$ is a singular point of the equation, then the integrals in the neighbourhood of $x = a$ are of the form
\[
y = (x - a)^{\lambda}\phi(x),
\]
where $\phi(x)$ is a regular function of $(x - a)$, that is, a function which can be expanded in a series of positive integer powers of $(x - a)$. The exponent $\lambda$ is determined by the ``indicial equation,'' which is obtained by substituting $y = (x - a)^{\lambda}$ in the equation and equating to zero the coefficient of the lowest power of $(x - a)$.

\paragraph{4.} The number of regular integrals belonging to a singular point $x = a$ is at most equal to the degree of the indicial equation; and it may be less than this degree, if the roots of the indicial equation are equal or differ by integers. In the latter case, logarithmic terms may appear in the integrals.

\paragraph{5.} Considering as above the coefficients $p_{1}, p_{2}, \ldots, p_{m}$ as rational and integral functions of $t$, it is easy to determine the degrees of these coefficients in such wise that the exponent $\alpha$ of the leading term $At^{\alpha}$ shall have a given value. Suppose first, this given value is $\alpha$, a positive integer greater than~$1$: the degrees may be $\delta$, $\delta + \alpha$, $\delta + 2\alpha$, \ldots, $\delta + m\alpha$, ($\delta = 0$ or any positive integer at pleasure); and not only so, but if we write
\[
z = At^{\alpha},
\]
then writing $z = At^{\alpha}$, clearly the highest power in the equation is $t^{\delta + m\alpha}$, and equating the coefficient hereof to zero, we have
\[
L[A] + L_{1}[A]^{m-1} + \cdots + L_{m-1}[A] + L_{m} = 0,
\]
an equation of the degree $m$ for the determination of the coefficient of the leading term $At^{\alpha}$. Assuming that the roots are all unequal, we have series each of the form
\[
z = At^{\alpha} + Bt^{\alpha-1} + \cdots + K + \frac{C}{t} + \frac{D}{t^{2}} + \cdots.
\]

\paragraph{6.} Suppose $\alpha = 1$, that is, let the leading term be $At$; here the degrees may be $\delta$, $\delta + 1$, $\delta + 2$, \ldots, $\delta + m$, ($\delta = 0$ or any positive integer as before), but we have a different form for the $A$-equation. In fact, here ($z = At$) in the sets of terms $(z^{m} + \cdots + z^{(m-1)})$, \ldots, $(z^{3} + 3zz' + z'')$, $(z^{2} + z')$, the terms in each brackets are of the same degree, viz.\ the degrees are $m$, \ldots, $3$, $2$; and not only so, but we have
\[
z^{2} + z' = [A]_{2}t^{2}, \quad z^{3} + 3zz' + z'' = [A]_{3}t^{3}, \quad \ldots.
\]
Hence if
\[
p = Lt^{\delta} + \ldots, \quad p_{1} = L_{1}t^{\delta+1} + \ldots, \quad \ldots, \quad p_{m} = L_{m}t^{\delta+m} + \ldots,
\]
the $A$-equation is
\[
L_{m-1}[A] + L_{m} = 0,
\]
an equation of the degree $m$ as before; assuming that the roots are unequal, we have here series each of the form
\[
z = At + B + \frac{C}{t} + \frac{D}{t^{2}} + \cdots,
\]
being, as will presently appear, the case of regular integrals for the $y$-equation.

\paragraph{7.} In either of the last-mentioned cases, the formula for the $A$-equation holds good for any of the two or more of the coefficients $p_{1}$, \ldots, $p_{m}$ may have equal degrees, provided that the degree is the highest of all. If neither $L$ nor $L_{m}$ be $=0$, the equation is still an equation of the order~$m$, with effective roots; but if any of the coefficients $L$ at the beginning of the equation vanish we thus introduce roots $= \infty$: and if any of the coefficients $L$ at the end of the equation vanish, we thus introduce roots $= 0$: the number of effective roots is $= m$ minus the number of roots $= \infty$ or $=0$; the case requires further consideration. If the equation has equal roots, then, from what precedes, it is easy to infer that we may have distinct series containing as in the general case only integer powers of $t$, or we may have series beginning with $At^{\alpha}$ or $At$, but containing also fractional powers of~$t$.

\paragraph{8.} It is perhaps worth remarking that, in the case where the series is
\[
y = (x - a)^{\lambda}\{A + B(x - a) + C(x - a)^{2} + \cdots\},
\]
the case is, in fact, that of a non-singular point $x = a$: and I do not here further consider it.

\paragraph{9.} The general theory of regular integrals is connected with the theory of the indicial equation and its roots. The form of the integrals depends on the nature of the roots: if the roots are all distinct, the integrals are all of the simple power-series form; if some roots are equal, logarithmic factors appear; if roots differ by integers, the integrals may involve logarithms or may be of a more complicated form.

\paragraph{10.} Considering the case where the series is
\[
y = e^{\frac{A}{x-a}}(x - a)^{\mu}\{A + B(x - a) + \cdots\},
\]
this is what I call a subregular integral. By what precedes, it appears that, for the values
\[
p = Lx^{-\delta} + \ldots, \quad p_{1} = L_{1}x^{-\delta-1} + \ldots, \quad \ldots, \quad p_{m} = L_{m}x^{-\delta-m} + \ldots,
\]
then the equation for the leading coefficient (previously represented by~$A$) is
\[
L[A] + L_{1}[A]^{-1} + \cdots + L_{m-1}[A]^{-m+1} + L_{m}[A]^{-m} = 0,
\]
but I do not see any direct or simple way of obtaining the corresponding equation for the exponent of this subregular integral.

\paragraph{11.} Similarly, if the series be
\[
y = e^{\frac{A}{(x-a)^{\alpha}}} \cdot (x-a)^{\mu}\{A + B(x-a) + \cdots\},
\]
($\alpha$ being a positive integer $= 2$ at least), then writing as above $t = \frac{1}{x-a}$, this is
\[
\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{\alpha A}{t^{\alpha+1}} - \frac{\mu}{t} + B + Ct + \cdots,
\]
whence, if for shortness
\[
w = -\frac{A}{t^{\alpha}} - \mu\log t + Bt + \frac{1}{2}Ct^{2} + \cdots,
\]
we have
\[
\log y = w + A\log x + Bx + \tfrac{1}{2}Cx^{2} + \cdots,
\]
or passing to the value of $y$, but expanding the exponential of $Bx + \tfrac{1}{2}Cx^{2} + \cdots$, this is
\[
y = e^{w} x^{A} (1 + Bx + Cx^{2} + \cdots),
\]
a subregular integral. By what precedes, it appears that, if
\[
p_{1} = Lx^{-\delta} + \ldots, \quad p_{2} = L_{1}x^{-\delta-1} + \ldots, \quad \ldots, \quad p_{m} = L_{m}x^{-\delta-m} + \ldots,
\]
then the equation for the leading coefficient is as above.

\paragraph{12.} To illustrate the case of a series with fractional powers, and also in order to work out a simple example with some completeness, I consider the differential equation of the second order $\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}} + P\frac{dy}{dx} + Qy = 0$, giving the $z$-equation $z^{2} + z + p_{2} = 0$; $p_{2}$ is a rational and integral function of $t$ ($t = \frac{1}{x-a}$), and we have two cases according as the order of the function is even or odd.

\paragraph{13.} Suppose first that the order is even, and for convenience taking it to be $= 4$, and assuming the value of $p_{2}$ accordingly, I consider the equation
\[
\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}} = (\alpha + \beta t + \gamma t^{2} + \delta t^{3} + \epsilon t^{4})y.
\]
Here the form is at once seen to be
\[
y = e^{\int z\,dt}, \quad z = At^{2} + Bt + C + \frac{D}{t} + \frac{E}{t^{2}} + \cdots,
\]
and observing that we have
\[
\frac{dz}{dt} = 2At + B - \frac{D}{t^{2}} - \frac{2E}{t^{3}} + \cdots,
\]
we find
\[
A^{2} = \alpha, \quad 2AB = \beta, \quad 2AC + B^{2} - B = \gamma, \quad 2AD + 2BC = \delta,
\]
\[
2AE + 2BD + C^{2} + C = \epsilon, \quad 2AF + 2BE + 2CD + D = 0,
\]
equations which give
\[
A = \sqrt{\alpha}, \quad B = \frac{\beta}{2\sqrt{\alpha}}, \quad C = \frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\alpha}} - \frac{\beta^{2}}{8\alpha\sqrt{\alpha}} + \frac{1}{4\sqrt{\alpha}},
\]
and so on for the remaining coefficients.

\paragraph{14.} It is to be observed that, although the formal series thus obtained may in general be divergent, yet they serve to determine the asymptotic behaviour of the integrals for large values of~$t$, that is, for values of $x$ in the neighbourhood of the singular point $x = a$.

\paragraph{15.} Reverting to the case where the series is
\[
y = (x - a)^{\lambda}\{A + B(x - a) + C(x - a)^{2} + \cdots\},
\]
and substituting for $t$ its value $\frac{1}{x-a}$, or for greater convenience $s = x - a$, this is
\[
\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\lambda}{s} + B + 2Cs + 3Ds^{2} + \cdots,
\]
whence
\[
\log y = \lambda\log s + Bs + \tfrac{1}{2}Cs^{2} + \cdots,
\]
or passing to the value of $y$, but expanding the exponential of $Bs + \tfrac{1}{2}Cs^{2} + \cdots$, this is
\[
y = s^{\lambda}(1 + Bs + Cs^{2} + \cdots),
\]
viz.\ we have here a regular integral. By what precedes, it appears that, for the values
\[
p = Lx^{-\delta} + \ldots, \quad p_{1} = L_{1}x^{-\delta-1} + \ldots, \quad \ldots, \quad p_{m} = L_{m}x^{-\delta-m} + \ldots,
\]
the exponent $\lambda$ is determined by the equation
\[
L[\lambda] + L_{1}[\lambda]^{m-1} + \cdots + L_{m-1}[\lambda] + L_{m} = 0,
\]
which is the equation for that purpose considered by Fuchs, and is what I would call the indicial equation.

\paragraph{16.} The logarithmic forms which belong to the case of equal roots may be deduced from the case of unequal roots, by supposing two or more of the roots to approach each other continuously and become ultimately equal.

\paragraph{17.} Similarly, if the series be
\[
y = e^{\frac{A}{(x-a)^{\alpha}}} \cdot (x-a)^{\mu}\{A + B(x-a) + \cdots\},
\]
($\alpha$ being a positive integer $= 2$ at least), then writing as above $t = \frac{1}{x-a}$, this is
\[
\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{\alpha A}{t^{\alpha+1}} - \frac{\mu}{t} + B + Ct + \cdots,
\]
whence, if for shortness
\[
w = -\frac{A}{t^{\alpha}} - \mu\log t + Bt + \tfrac{1}{2}Ct^{2} + \cdots,
\]
we have
\[
\log y = w + A\log x + Bx + \tfrac{1}{2}Cx^{2} + \cdots,
\]
or passing to the value of $y$, but expanding as before the exponential of $Bx + \tfrac{1}{2}Cx^{2} + \cdots$, this is
\[
y = e^{w}x^{A}(1 + Bx + Cx^{2} + \cdots),
\]
a subregular integral. By what precedes, it appears that, if
\[
p_{1} = Lx^{-\delta} + \ldots, \quad p_{2} = L_{1}x^{-\delta-1} + \ldots, \quad \ldots, \quad p_{m} = L_{m}x^{-\delta-m} + \ldots,
\]
then the equation for the leading coefficient (previously represented by~$A$) is
\[
L[A] + L_{1}[A]^{-1} + \cdots + L_{m-1}[A]^{-m+1} + L_{m}[A]^{-m} = 0,
\]
but I do not see any direct or simple way of obtaining the corresponding equation for the exponent of this subregular integral.

\paragraph{18.} To illustrate the case of a series with fractional powers, and also in order to work out a simple example with some completeness, I consider the differential equation of the second order $\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}} + P\frac{dy}{dx} + Qy = 0$, giving the $z$-equation $z^{2} + z + p_{2} = 0$; $p_{2}$ is a rational and integral function of $t$ ($t = \frac{1}{x-a}$), and we have two cases according as the order of the function is even or odd.

\paragraph{19.} Suppose first that the order is even, and for convenience taking it to be $= 4$, and assuming the value of $p_{2}$ accordingly, I consider the equation
\[
\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}} = (\alpha + \beta t + \gamma t^{2} + \delta t^{3} + \epsilon t^{4})y.
\]
Here the form is at once seen to be
\[
y = e^{\int z\,dt}, \quad z = At^{2} + Bt + C + \frac{D}{t} + \frac{E}{t^{2}} + \cdots,
\]
and observing that we have
\[
\frac{dz}{dt} = 2At + B - \frac{D}{t^{2}} - \frac{2E}{t^{3}} + \cdots,
\]
we find
\[
A^{2} = \alpha, \quad 2AB = \beta, \quad 2AC + B^{2} - B = \gamma, \quad 2AD + 2BC = \delta,
\]
\[
2AE + 2BD + C^{2} + C = \epsilon, \quad 2AF + 2BE + 2CD + D = 0,
\]
equations which give
\[
A = \sqrt{\alpha}, \quad B = \frac{\beta}{2\sqrt{\alpha}}, \quad C = \frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\alpha}} - \frac{\beta^{2}}{8\alpha\sqrt{\alpha}} + \frac{1}{4\sqrt{\alpha}},
\]
