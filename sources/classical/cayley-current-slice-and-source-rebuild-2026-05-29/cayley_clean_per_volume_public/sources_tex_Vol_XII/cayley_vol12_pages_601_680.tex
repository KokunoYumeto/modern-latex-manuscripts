\section*{880.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{880. Note on the Relation between the Distance of Five Points in Space}

\begin{center}
\textbf{NOTE ON THE RELATION BETWEEN THE DISTANCE OF FIVE POINTS IN SPACE.}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
[From the \textit{Messenger of Mathematics}, vol.\ \textsc{xviii}.\ (1889), pp.\ 100--102.]
\end{center}

In Lagrange's paper ``Solutions analytiques de quelques problemes sur les pyramides triangulaires'' (\textit{Berlin Memoirs}, 1773; \textit{\OE uvres}, t.\ \textsc{iii}., see p.\ 677), there is contained a formula for the relation between the distances from each other of five points in space; viz.\ this is
\begin{align*}
4\Delta^2 f &= \phantom{+} \alpha\,(a + f - g)^2 + \alpha'\,(a' + f - g')^2 + \alpha''\,(a'' + f - g'')^2 \\
&\quad + 2\beta\,(a' + f - g')\,(a'' + f - g'') + 2\beta'\,(a + f - g)\,(a'' + f - g'') \\
&\quad + 2\beta''\,(a + f - g)\,(a' + f - g'),
\end{align*}
or, in a slightly altered notation, say
\[
\Pi = -4\Delta^2 f + (\alpha_1,\, \alpha_2,\, \alpha_3,\, \beta_1,\, \beta_2,\, \beta_3\, \backslash\!\!\!\backslash\, a_1+f-g_1,\, a_2+f-g_2,\, a_3+f-g_3)^2 = 0,
\]
where, if the points are called $1$, $2$, $3$, $4$, $5$, then
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{r@{\hspace{1.5em}}c@{\hspace{1.5em}}l}
$c_1$, $c_2$, $c_3$ & are the squared distances & $23$, $31$, $12$, \\
$a_1$, $a_2$, $a_3$ & \,,\,\,,\,\,, & $41$, $42$, $43$, \\
$g_1$, $g_2$, $g_3$ & \,,\,\,,\,\,, & $51$, $52$, $53$,
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
and
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{r@{\hspace{1.5em}}c@{\hspace{1.5em}}l}
$f$ & is the squared distance & $45$.
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The values of $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$, $\alpha_3$, $\beta_1$, $\beta_2$, $\beta_3$, in terms of these squared distances, are
\begin{align*}
\alpha_1 &= -(a_2 - a_3)^2 + 2c_1(a_2 + a_3) - c_1^{\,2}, \\
\alpha_2 &= -(a_3 - a_1)^2 + 2c_2(a_3 + a_1) - c_2^{\,2}, \\
\alpha_3 &= -(a_1 - a_2)^2 + 2c_3(a_1 + a_2) - c_3^{\,2}, \\
\beta_1 &= -(a_3 - a_1)(a_1 - a_2) + 2c_1 a_1 - c_2(a_1 + a_2) - c_3(a_3 + a_1) + c_2 c_3, \\
\beta_2 &= -(a_1 - a_2)(a_2 - a_3) + 2c_2 a_2 - c_3(a_2 + a_3) - c_1(a_1 + a_2) + c_3 c_1, \\
\beta_3 &= -(a_2 - a_3)(a_3 - a_1) + 2c_3 a_3 - c_1(a_3 + a_1) - c_2(a_2 + a_3) + c_1 c_2.
\end{align*}

After some reductions, the value of $4\Delta^2$, in terms of these squared distances, is found to be
\begin{align*}
4\Delta^2 &= \phantom{+} c_1(a_3 - a_1)(a_1 - a_2) + a_1(-c_1^{\,2} + c_1 c_2 + c_1 c_3) - c_1 c_2 c_3 \\
&\quad + c_2(a_1 - a_2)(a_2 - a_3) + a_2(-c_2^{\,2} + c_2 c_3 + c_2 c_1) \\
&\quad + c_3(a_2 - a_3)(a_3 - a_1) + a_3(-c_3^{\,2} + c_3 c_1 + c_3 c_2),
\end{align*}
which is, in fact,
\[
8\Delta^2 = \begin{vmatrix}
. & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\
1 & . & c_3 & c_2 & a_1 \\
1 & c_3 & . & c_1 & a_2 \\
1 & c_2 & c_1 & . & a_3 \\
1 & a_1 & a_2 & a_3 & .
\end{vmatrix},
\]
where observe that each term of the determinant contains the factor $2$.

By the formula in my paper ``On a theorem in the Geometry of Position,'' \textit{Camb.\ Math.\ Jour.}, t.\ \textsc{ii}.\ (1841), pp.\ 267--271, [1], (introducing into it the present notation), the relation between the distances of the five points is given in the form
\[
\Omega = \begin{vmatrix}
. & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\
1 & . & c_3 & c_2 & a_1 & g_1 \\
1 & c_3 & . & c_1 & a_2 & g_2 \\
1 & c_2 & c_1 & . & a_3 & g_3 \\
1 & a_1 & a_2 & a_3 & . & f \\
1 & g_1 & g_2 & g_3 & f & .
\end{vmatrix} = 0.
\]

The equations $\Pi = 0$, $\Omega = 0$ should therefore agree with each other; we have in $\Omega$ the term
\[
-f^2 \begin{vmatrix}
1 & c_3 & . & c_1 \\
1 & c_2 & c_1 & . \\
. & 1 & 1 & 1 \\
1 & . & c_3 & c_2
\end{vmatrix},
\]
which is
\[ = -f^2(c_1^{\,2} + c_2^{\,2} + c_3^{\,2} - 2c_2 c_3 - 2c_3 c_1 - 2c_1 c_2); \]
and similarly in $\Pi$ we have the term
\[ f^2(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \alpha_3 + 2\beta_1 + 2\beta_2 + 2\beta_3), \]
which is easily shown to be
\[ = -f^2(c_1^{\,2} + c_2^{\,2} + c_3^{\,2} - 2c_2 c_3 - 2c_3 c_1 - 2c_1 c_2); \]
and it thus appears that we have identically $\Pi = \Omega$.

It is to be remarked that, in Lagrange's form, the points $4$ and $5$ are regarded as determined each of them by means of its squared distances from the vertices of the triangle $123$, and that the formula gives (by a quadratic equation) the squared distance $45$; but that nevertheless the two points $4$ and $5$ do not present themselves symmetrically in the formula; in fact, $\Delta^2$ and the coefficients $(\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$, $\alpha_3$, $\beta_1$, $\beta_2$, $\beta_3)$ of the formula relate all of them to the tetrahedron $4123$; as noticed in the paper, $\Delta = 6 \times$ volume of tetrahedron; $\surd(\alpha_1)$, $\surd(\alpha_2)$, $\surd(\alpha_3)$ are the doubled areas of the faces $423$, $431$, $412$ respectively, and the doubled area of the face $123$ is
\[ = \surd(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \alpha_3 + 2\beta_1 + 2\beta_2 + 2\beta_3); \]
it may be added that
\[ \beta_1 \div \surd(\alpha_2 \alpha_3),\quad \beta_2 \div \surd(\alpha_3 \alpha_1),\quad \beta_3 \div \surd(\alpha_1 \alpha_2), \]
are equal to the cosines of the dihedral angles at the edges $41$, $42$, $43$ respectively.

\section*{881.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{881. On Hermite's $H$-Product Theorem}

\begin{center}
\textbf{ON HERMITE'S $H$-PRODUCT THEOREM.}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
[From the \textit{Messenger of Mathematics}, vol.\ \textsc{xviii}.\ (1889), pp.\ 104--107.]
\end{center}

I give this name to a theorem relating to the product of an even number of Eta-functions, established by M.~Hermite in his ``Note sur le calcul differential et le calcul integral,'' forming an appendix to the sixth edition of Lacroix's \textit{Differential and Integral Calculus}, and separately printed, 8vo.\ Paris, 1862. It is the theorem stated p.~65, in the form
\[ \phi\,(x) = F\,(z^2) + \frac{dz}{dx}\, z F_1\,(z^2), \]
where
\[ \phi\,(x) = \frac{A\,H\,(x-\alpha_1)\, H\,(x-\alpha_2) \ldots H\,(x-\alpha_{2n})}{\Theta^{2n}\,(x)}, \]
where $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \ldots + \alpha_{2n} = 0$, and $z = \operatorname{sn} x$, $\operatorname{cn} x$ or $\operatorname{dn} x$ at pleasure; $F(z^2)$, $F_1(z^2)$ denote rational and integral functions of $z^2$ of the degrees $n$ and $n-2$ respectively; $A$ is a constant, which we may if we please so determine that in $F(z^2)$ the coefficient of the highest power $z^{2n}$ shall be $=1$.

If, for shortness, we write $s$, $c$, $d$ for $\operatorname{sn} x$, $\operatorname{cn} x$, $\operatorname{dn} x$ respectively; and to fix the ideas, assume $z = \operatorname{sn} x$, $=s$, then the theorem is
\[ \frac{A\,H\,(x-\alpha_1)\, H\,(x-\alpha_2) \ldots H\,(x-\alpha_{2n})}{\Theta^{2n}\,(x)} = F\,(s^2) + scdF_1\,(s^2)\,; \]
viz.\ the theorem is that the product of the $2n$ $H$-functions ($\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \ldots + \alpha_{2n} = 0$ as above), divided by $\Theta^{2n}(x)$, is a function of the elliptic functions sn, cn, dn, of the form in question.

Hermite uses the theorem for the demonstration of Abel's theorem, as applied to the elliptic functions; or as I would rather express it, he uses the theorem for the determination of the sn, cn, and dn of $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \ldots + \alpha_{2n-1}$.

To show how this is, observe that $F(s^2)$, \textit{qua} rational and integral function of $s^2$ of the degree $n$, with its first coefficient $=1$, contains $n$ arbitrary coefficients; and $F_1(s^2)$, \textit{qua} rational and integral function of $s^2$ of the degree $n-2$, contains $n-1$ arbitrary coefficients: hence $F(s^2) + scdF_1(s^2)$ contains $2n-1$ arbitrary coefficients; and considering $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$, \ldots, $\alpha_{2n-1}$ as given, the function in question must vanish for each of the values $x = \alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$, \ldots, $\alpha_{2n-1}$; and we have therefore $2n-1$ equations for obtaining the $2n-1$ coefficients, which are thus completely determined: in particular, the constant term, say $L$, of $F_1(s^2)$ is a given function of $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$, \ldots, $\alpha_{2n-1}$, that is, of the sn, cn, and dn of these quantities; and the theorem shows that the function thus determined vanishes also for $x = \alpha_{2n}$, that is, $=-(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \ldots + \alpha_{2n-1})$.

Now writing $-x$ for $x$ in the formula, and recollecting that $H$ is an odd function, $\Theta$ an even function, we find
\[ \frac{A\,H\,(x+\alpha_1)\, H\,(x+\alpha_2) \ldots H\,(x+\alpha_{2n})}{\Theta^{2n}\,(x)} = F\,(s^2) - scdF_1\,(s^2)\,; \]
and multiplying together the two sides of these equations respectively,
\[ A^2 \frac{H\,(x-\alpha_1)\, H\,(x+\alpha_1)}{\Theta^2\,(x)} \ldots \frac{H\,(x-\alpha_{2n})\, H\,(x+\alpha_{2n})}{\Theta^2\,(x)} = \{F(s^2)\}^2 - s^2 c^2 d^2\, \{F_1(s^2)\}^2, \]
where the right-hand side is a rational and integral function of $s^2$ of the degree $2n$, and the coefficient of the highest term $s^{4n}$ is $=1$; in fact, this term arises only from the square of $F(s^2)$, which has its highest term $= s^{2n}$.

Now $\dfrac{H\,(x-\alpha_1)\, H\,(x+\alpha_1)}{\Theta^2\,(x)}$ is a mere constant multiple of $\operatorname{sn}^2 x - \operatorname{sn}^2 \alpha_1$, or say of $s^2 - \operatorname{sn}^2 \alpha_1$; (this well-known theorem is, in fact, the particular case $n=2$ of Hermite's theorem); and similarly for the other terms: we must clearly have $A^2$, multiplied by the product of the factors thus introduced, $=1$; and thus the theorem becomes
\[ (s^2 - \operatorname{sn}^2 \alpha_1)\, (s^2 - \operatorname{sn}^2 \alpha_2) \ldots (s^2 - \operatorname{sn}^2 \alpha_{2n}) = \{F(s^2)\}^2 - s^2 c^2 d^2\, \{F_1(s^2)\}^2. \]

And putting herein $s=0$, and writing as before $L$ for the constant term of $F(s^2)$, we have
\[ \operatorname{sn}^2 \alpha_1 \, \operatorname{sn}^2 \alpha_2 \ldots \operatorname{sn}^2 \alpha_{2n} = L^2, \]
or, the sign $\pm$ being properly determined, say
\[ \operatorname{sn} \alpha_1 \, \operatorname{sn} \alpha_2 \ldots \operatorname{sn} \alpha_{2n} = \pm\, L, \]
where, by what precedes, $L$ is a given function of the sn, cn, and dn of $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$, \ldots, $\alpha_{2n-1}$. Hence we have sn$\,\alpha_{2n}$, that is, $-\operatorname{sn}\,(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \ldots + \alpha_{2n-1})$ as a given function of the sn, cn, and dn of $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$, \ldots, $\alpha_{2n-1}$.

Similarly writing $z = \operatorname{cn} x$, $=c$, and $z = \operatorname{dn} x$, $=d$, we have cn$\,(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \ldots + \alpha_{2n-1})$ and dn$\,(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \ldots + \alpha_{2n-1})$ each of them as a given function of the sn, cn, and dn of $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$, \ldots, $\alpha_{2n-1}$.

It is hardly necessary to remark that $F(z^2) + z\dfrac{dz}{dx}F_1(z^2)$ is a function of the same form, whether we have $z=s$, $c$ or $d$; in fact, the functions $F$ and $F_1$ are rational in $s^2$, $c^2$, or $d^2$, and we have $z\dfrac{dz}{dx} = scd$, $-scd$, and $-k^2 scd$ for the three values respectively.

The number of terms $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$, \ldots, $\alpha_{2n-1}$ has been odd, but by taking one of them $=0$, the formulae give the values of the sn, cn, and dn for the sum of an even number of terms.

It has been seen that Hermite's $H$-product theorem gives, say Abel's theorem, in the form
\[ \Pi\, (s^2 - \operatorname{sn}^2 \alpha) = \{F(s^2)\}^2 - s^2 c^2 d^2\, \{F_1(s^2)\}^2, \]
each side of this relation being the product of two factors, viz.\ for the left-hand side the factors are
\[ A\,\Pi\, \frac{H\,(x-\alpha)}{\Theta\,(x)},\quad A\,\Pi\, \frac{H\,(x+\alpha)}{\Theta\,(x)}, \]
and for the right-hand side they are the rational functions of $s^2$,
\[ F(s^2) + scdF_1(s^2),\quad F(s^2) - scdF_1(s^2)\,; \]
these factors are by Hermite's theorem equal each to each; viz.\ this is the relation in which Hermite's stands to Abel's theorem.

The $H$-product theorem is given as one out of a group of four theorems; the other three may be called the $H$-product, $H_1$-product and $\Theta_1$-product, odd theorems respectively,
\[ \{\Theta_1\,(x) = \Theta\,(x+K),\quad H_1\,(x) = H\,(x+K)\}, \]
viz.\ these are
\[
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{A_1 H\,(x-\alpha_1)\, H\,(x-\alpha_2) \ldots H\,(x-\alpha_{2n+1})}{\Theta^{2n+1}\,(x)} = sF\,(s^2) + \phantom{k^2} cd\, \phi\, (s^2), \\[8pt]
\dfrac{A_2 H_1\,(x-\alpha_1)\, H_1\,(x-\alpha_2) \ldots H_1\,(x-\alpha_{2n+1})}{\Theta^{2n+1}\,(x)} = cF\,(c^2) - \phantom{k^2} sd\, \phi\, (c^2), \\[8pt]
\dfrac{A_3 \Theta_1\,(x-\alpha_1)\, \Theta_1\,(x-\alpha_2) \ldots \Theta_1\,(x-\alpha_{2n+1})}{\Theta^{2n+1}\,(x)} = dF\,(d^2) - k^2 sc\, \phi\, (d^2),
\end{cases}
\]
where $F$, $\phi$ are rational and integral functions of the degrees $n$ and $n-1$, having their proper values in the three equations respectively, and in each case
\[ \alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \ldots + \alpha_{2n+1} = 0. \]

It was seen above that, for $n=1$, the $H$-product theorem became
\[ \frac{A\, H\,(x-\alpha)\, H\,(x+\alpha)}{\Theta^2\,(x)} = \operatorname{sn}^2 x - \operatorname{sn}^2 \alpha, \]
which is the most simple case; for the odd theorems, the most simple case is $n=0$, viz.\ we then have
\[ \frac{A_1 H\,(x)}{\Theta\,(x)} = \operatorname{sn} x,\quad \frac{A_2 H_1(x)}{\Theta\,(x)} = \operatorname{cn} x,\quad \frac{A_3 \Theta_1(x)}{\Theta\,(x)} = \operatorname{dn} x\,; \]
to complete the formulae observe that the values of the constants are
\[ A = \frac{2k'K}{k\pi \Theta^2(\alpha)},\quad A_1 = \frac{1}{\surd(k)},\quad A_2 = \surd\!\left(\frac{k'}{k}\right),\quad A_3 = \surd(k'). \]

The three theorems may be used, in like manner with the $H$-product theorem, to give the values of the sn, cn, and dn respectively of the sum $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \ldots + \alpha_{2n}$.

\section*{882.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{882. A Correspondence of Confocal Cartesians with the Right Lines of a Hyperboloid}

\begin{center}
\textbf{A CORRESPONDENCE OF CONFOCAL CARTESIANS WITH THE RIGHT LINES OF A HYPERBOLOID.}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
[From the \textit{Messenger of Mathematics}, vol.\ \textsc{xviii}.\ (1889), pp.\ 128--130.]
\end{center}

\textsc{Take} $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$ arbitrary, $A$, $B$, $C = \beta-\gamma$, $\gamma-\alpha$, $\alpha-\beta$ (so that $A+B+C=0$), and writing $\rho$, $\sigma$, $\tau$ for rectangular coordinates, consider the hyperboloid
\[ A\rho^2 + B\sigma^2 + C\tau^2 + ABC = 0. \]

Let $\rho_0$, $\sigma_0$, $\tau_0$ be the coordinates of a point on the surface ($A\rho_0^{\,2} + B\sigma_0^{\,2} + C\tau_0^{\,2} + ABC = 0$). The equations of a line through this point are $\rho$, $\sigma$, $\tau = \rho_0 + f\Omega$, $\sigma_0 + g\Omega$, $\tau_0 + h\Omega$ ($\Omega$ indeterminate); and if this lies on the surface, we have
\begin{align*}
A\rho_0 f + B\sigma_0 g + C\tau_0 h &= 0, \\
Af^2 + Bg^2 + Ch^2 &= 0,
\end{align*}
which equations determine the ratios $f : g : h$; the equations give
\[ (A\rho_0 f + B\sigma_0 g)^2 = C\tau_0^{\,2} .\, Ch^2, \quad = -C\tau_0^{\,2}(Af^2 + Bg^2); \]
that is,
\[ (A^2\rho_0^{\,2} + AC\tau_0^{\,2})f^2 + 2AB\rho_0\sigma_0 fg + (B^2\sigma_0^{\,2} + BC\tau_0^{\,2})g^2 = 0, \]
whence
\begin{align*}
&\phantom{=} \{(B^2\sigma_0^{\,2} + BC\tau_0^{\,2})\, g + AB\rho_0\sigma_0 f \}^2 \\
&= \{A^2B^2\rho_0^{\,2}\sigma_0^{\,2} - (A^2\rho_0^{\,2} + AC\tau_0^{\,2})(B^2\sigma_0^{\,2} + BC\tau_0^{\,2})\} f^2, \\
&= -ABC\,(A\rho_0^{\,2} + B\sigma_0^{\,2} + C\tau_0^{\,2})\, \tau_0^{\,2} f^2, \\
&= A^2B^2C^2 \tau_0^{\,2} f^2;
\end{align*}
that is,
\[ \{(B\sigma_0^{\,2} + C\tau_0^{\,2})g + A\rho_0\sigma_0 f\}^2 = A^2C^2\tau_0^{\,2} f^2, \]
or say
\[ (B\sigma_0^{\,2} + C\tau_0^{\,2})g + A\,(\rho_0\sigma_0 \pm C\tau_0)f = 0, \]
which equation, together with $A\rho_0 f + B\sigma_0 g + C\tau_0 h = 0$, determines the ratios $f : g : h$. We have thus the two lines through the point $(\rho_0$, $\sigma_0$, $\tau_0)$.

But the equations of the line may be conveniently represented in a different form; writing the equation first obtained in the form
\[ \sigma_0\,(B\sigma_0 g + A\rho_0 f) + C\tau_0^{\,2} g \pm AC\tau_0 f = 0, \]
this is
\[ -\sigma_0 C\tau_0 h + C\tau_0^{\,2} g \pm AC\tau_0 f = 0, \]
viz.
\[ -h\sigma_0 + g\tau_0 \pm Af = 0\,; \]
and we have the like equations
\begin{align*}
-f\tau_0 + h\rho_0 \pm Bg &= 0, \\
-g\rho_0 + f\sigma_0 \pm Ch &= 0,
\end{align*}
where the sign is the same in each of the three equations.

The equations of the line on the surface may be written
\begin{alignat*}{3}
&\cdot\qquad & h\sigma &- g\tau - h\sigma_0 + g\tau_0 &= 0, \\
-&h\rho &\cdot\qquad & + f\tau - f\tau_0 + h\rho_0 &= 0, \\
&g\rho & - f\sigma &\quad\cdot - g\rho_0 + f\sigma_0 &= 0, \\
(&h\sigma_0-g\tau_0)\rho &+ (f\tau_0-h\rho_0)\sigma &+ (g\rho_0-f\sigma_0)\tau &\qquad\cdot\qquad = 0\,;
\end{alignat*}
and hence from the foregoing three equations, taking the sign $-$, we have
\begin{alignat*}{3}
&\cdot\qquad & h\sigma -\phantom{g}\, &g\tau + Af &= 0, \\
-&h\rho &\cdot\qquad & + f\tau + Bg &= 0, \\
&g\rho & - f\sigma &\quad\cdot + Ch &= 0, \\
-&Af\rho & - Bg\sigma & - Ch\tau &\quad\cdot\qquad = 0,
\end{alignat*}
where $Af^2 + Bg^2 + Ch^2 = 0$, for the equations of a line on the surface.

In like manner, taking the sign $+$, and for $f$, $g$, $h$ writing new values $f'$, $g'$, $h'$, we have
\begin{alignat*}{3}
&\cdot\qquad & h'\sigma -\phantom{g'}\, &g'\tau - Af' &= 0, \\
-&h'\rho &\cdot\qquad & + f'\tau - Bg' &= 0, \\
&g'\rho & - f'\sigma &\quad\cdot - Ch' &= 0, \\
&Af'\rho & + Bg'\sigma & + Ch'\tau &\quad\cdot\qquad = 0,
\end{alignat*}
where $Af'^2 + Bg'^2 + Ch'^2 = 0$, for the equations of a line on the surface.

The two systems of equations evidently belong to the lines of the two different kinds respectively. Writing for shortness $P$, $Q$, $R = gh' + g'h$, $hf' + h'f$, $fg' + f'g$, the two lines in fact intersect in a point, the coordinates say ($\rho_0$, $\sigma_0$, $\tau_0$) whereof are $= \Theta QR$, $\Theta RP$, $\Theta PQ$, where
\[ \Theta = \frac{A}{g^2h'^2 - g'^2h^2} = \frac{B}{h^2f'^2 - h'^2f^2} = \frac{C}{f^2g'^2 - f'^2g^2}, \]
the three expressions for $\Theta$ being equal to each other in virtue of the equations
\[ Af^2 + Bg^2 + Ch^2 = 0,\quad Af'^2 + Bg'^2 + Ch'^2 = 0. \]

Take now, in a plane, $P$, $Q$, $R$ points on any line, say the axis of $x$, at distances $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$ from the origin, then for a point of the plane, coordinates $(x, y)$, if $\rho$, $\sigma$, $\tau$ be the distances of the point from these three points, or say foci, we have
\begin{align*}
\rho^2 &= (x-\alpha)^2 + y^2, \\
\sigma^2 &= (x-\beta)^2 + y^2, \\
\tau^2 &= (x-\gamma)^2 + y^2\,;
\end{align*}
and if as before $A$, $B$, $C = \beta-\gamma$, $\gamma-\alpha$, $\alpha-\beta$, we thence have
\[ A\rho^2 + B\sigma^2 + C\tau^2 + ABC = 0. \]

A point, coordinates ($\rho$, $\sigma$, $\tau$), of the hyperboloid thus corresponds to a point in the plane, distances $\rho$, $\sigma$, $\tau$ from the three foci $R$, $S$, $T$ respectively; and to any line
\begin{alignat*}{3}
&\cdot\qquad & h\sigma -\phantom{g}\, &g\tau + Af &= 0, \\
-&h\rho &\cdot\qquad & + f\tau + Bg &= 0, \\
&g\rho & - f\sigma &\quad\cdot + Ch &= 0, \\
-&Af\rho & - Bg\sigma & - Ch\tau &\quad\cdot\qquad = 0,
\end{alignat*}
corresponds the Cartesian represented by these linear equations. Similarly, to the line represented by the other system of equations
\begin{alignat*}{3}
&\cdot\qquad & h'\sigma -\phantom{g'}\, &g'\tau - Af' &= 0, \\
-&h'\rho &\cdot\qquad & + f'\tau - Bg' &= 0, \\
&g'\rho & - f'\sigma &\quad\cdot - Ch' &= 0, \\
&Af'\rho & + Bg'\sigma & + Ch'\tau &\quad\cdot\qquad = 0,
\end{alignat*}
corresponds the Cartesian represented by these equations; the two curves intersect in the point $\rho_0$, $\sigma_0$, $\tau_0 = \Theta QR$, $\Theta RP$, $\Theta PQ$, corresponding to the intersection of the lines on the hyperboloid; and moreover, \textit{qua} confocal Cartesians, they intersect at right angles.

\section*{883.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{883. Analytical Formulae in Regard to an Octad of Points}

\begin{center}
\textbf{ANALYTICAL FORMULAE IN REGARD TO AN OCTAD OF POINTS.}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
[From the \textit{Messenger of Mathematics}, vol.\ \textsc{xviii}.\ (1889), pp.\ 149--152.]
\end{center}

\textsc{The} term ``tetrad'' is used in two distinct but not inconsistent senses, viz.\ a tetrad denotes any four points; and it also denotes the four vertices of a self-conjugate tetrahedron in regard to a quadric surface. In fact, given any four points, there exists a triply infinite series of quadric surfaces such that, in regard to any one of them, the four points form a self-conjugate tetrahedron.

Two or more tetrads, in regard to one and the same quadric surface, are called similar tetrads.

The eight points of intersection of any three quadric surfaces are an octad; and we have the theorem that any seven points determine the octad, viz.\ the quadric surfaces which pass through any seven given points pass also through a uniquely determinate eighth point.

We have the further theorem that any two similar tetrads form an octad.

In particular, the vertices of the tetrahedron
\[ (x=0,\, y=0,\, z=0,\, w=0), \]
or, say the points $1000$, $0100$, $0010$, $0001$ are a tetrad in regard to the quadric surface $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 + w^2 = 0$. The points $(x_1, y_1, z_1, w_1)$, $(x_2, y_2, z_2, w_2)$, $(x_3, y_3, z_3, w_3)$, $(x_4, y_4, z_4, w_4)$, or say the points $1$, $2$, $3$, $4$, will be a tetrad in regard to the same quadric surface, if only
\[ x_1x_2 + y_1y_2 + z_1z_2 + w_1w_2 = 0,\ \&\text{c.},\ \text{(six equations)}. \]

Hence, these equations being satisfied, the two tetrads form an octad; the equation of a general quadric surface through the points of the first tetrad is
\[ fyz + gzx + hxy + lxw + myw + nzw = 0, \]
and we can from three such equations eliminate any two terms; the two terms eliminated may be such as $xy$, $xz$, (12 forms) or such as $yz$, $xw$, (3 forms); and the equation may thus be presented either in the form
\[ fyz + (lx + my + nz)w = 0, \]
or in the form
\[ gzx + hxy + myw + nzw = 0. \]

The absolute magnitudes of the coordinates
\[ (x_1,\, y_1,\, z_1,\, w_1),\ \&\text{c.}, \]
of these points are properly indeterminate; but we may if we please fix the absolute magnitudes by assuming
\[ x_1^{\,2} + y_1^{\,2} + z_1^{\,2} + w_1^{\,2} = M,\ \&\text{c.}, \]
(four equations, the same quantity $M$ in each of them), and we have as before
\[ x_1x_2 + y_1y_2 + z_1z_2 + w_1w_2 = 0,\ \&\text{c.},\ \text{(six equations)}, \]
viz.\ the coordinates are taken to satisfy these 10 equations; and this being so, it is to be shown that there exist quadric surfaces as above. The 10 equations may be expressed in a correlative form in like manner with the ordinary six equations for the transformation of the rectangular coordinates $(x,\, y,\, z)$, viz.\ the new form is
\[ x_1^{\,2} + x_2^{\,2} + x_3^{\,2} + x_4^{\,2} = M,\ \&\text{c.},\ \text{(four equations)}, \]
and
\[ x_1y_1 + x_2y_2 + x_3y_3 + x_4y_4 = 0,\ \&\text{c.},\ \text{(six equations)}, \]
and the coordinates thus also satisfy these 10 equations.

Thus the equation $fyz + (lx + my + nz)w = 0$, if we determine the coefficients in such wise that the surface passes through the points $1$, $2$, $3$, becomes
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
yz & xw & yw & zw \\
y_1z_1 & x_1w_1 & y_1w_1 & z_1w_1 \\
y_2z_2 & x_2w_2 & y_2w_2 & z_2w_2 \\
y_3z_3 & x_3w_3 & y_3w_3 & z_3w_3
\end{vmatrix} = 0,
\]
and this equation must therefore be satisfied on writing therein $(x_4,\, y_4,\, z_4,\, w_4)$ for $(x,\, y,\, z,\, w)$; viz.\ the equation thus obtained, det.\ $(1,\, 2,\, 3,\, 4) = 0$, must be satisfied in virtue of the equations which connect the coordinates of the four points. But, instead of effecting this verification, it is better to exhibit the equation in $(x,\, y,\, z,\, w)$ in a form wherein the coordinates of the four points enter symmetrically; and the new equation, \textit{qua} transformation of the original form, is satisfied for $(x,\, y,\, z,\, w) = (x_1,\, y_1,\, z_1,\, w_1)$: and then of course, by reason of the symmetry, it is also satisfied for
\[ (x,\, y,\, z,\, w) = (x_4,\, y_4,\, z_4,\, w_4). \]

The transformation may be effected directly; but I prefer to write down the final result, and afterwards verify it.

The form is
\begin{align*}
yz .\, Mw_1w_2w_3w_4 &+ xw[-x_1y_1z_1 . w_2w_3w_4 - x_2y_2z_2 . w_3w_4w_1 - x_3y_3z_3 . w_4w_1w_2 - x_4y_4z_4 . w_1w_2w_3] \\
&+ yw[\phantom{-}x_1^{\,2}w_1 . z_2z_3z_4 + x_2^{\,2}w_2 . z_3z_4z_1 + x_3^{\,2}w_3 . z_4z_1z_2 + x_4^{\,2}w_4 . z_1z_2z_3] \\
&+ zw[\phantom{-}x_1^{\,2}w_1 . y_2y_3y_4 + x_2^{\,2}w_2 . y_3y_4y_1 + x_3^{\,2}w_3 . y_4y_1y_2 + x_4^{\,2}w_4 . y_1y_2y_3] = 0.
\end{align*}

We have to show that the equation is satisfied by writing therein
\[ (x,\, y,\, z,\, w) = (x_1,\, y_1,\, z_1,\, w_1). \]
Calling the resulting value $\Omega_1$, we have
\begin{align*}
\Omega_1 \div w_1 &= My_1z_1w_2w_3w_4 - x_1^{\,2}y_1z_1w_2w_3w_4 \\
&\quad - x_1(x_2y_2z_2w_3w_4w_1 + x_3y_3z_3w_4w_1w_2 + x_4y_4z_4w_1w_2w_3) \\
&\quad + x_1^{\,2}w_1(y_1z_2z_3z_4 + z_1y_2y_3y_4) \\
&\quad + y_1z_1[x_2^{\,2}w_2(z_3z_4 + y_3y_4) + x_3^{\,2}w_3(z_4z_2 + y_4y_2) \\
&\qquad\qquad\quad + x_4^{\,2}w_4(z_2z_3 + y_2y_3)],
\end{align*}
which is
\begin{align*}
=\, &y_1z_1w_2w_3w_4(x_2^{\,2} + x_3^{\,2} + x_4^{\,2}) \\
&- x_1(x_2y_2z_2w_3w_4w_1 + x_3y_3z_3w_4w_1w_2 + x_4y_4z_4w_1w_2w_3) \\
&+ x_1^{\,2}w_1(y_1z_2z_3z_4 + z_1y_2y_3y_4) \\
&- y_1z_1[x_2^{\,2}w_2(x_3x_4 + w_3w_4) + x_3^{\,2}w_3(x_2x_4 + w_2w_4) \\
&\qquad\quad + x_4^{\,2}w_4(x_2x_3 + w_2w_3)].
\end{align*}

The expression contains terms in $w_2w_3w_4$ which destroy each other; omitting them, we have
\begin{align*}
\Omega_1 \div w_1 &= -x_1(x_2y_2z_2w_3w_4w_1 + x_3y_3z_3w_4w_1w_2 + x_4y_4z_4w_1w_2w_3) \\
&\quad + x_1^{\,2}w_1(y_1z_2z_3z_4 + z_1y_2y_3y_4) \\
&\quad - x_2x_3x_4y_1z_1(x_2w_2 + x_3w_3 + x_4w_4),
\end{align*}
where the last line is
\[ = x_2x_3x_4y_1z_1 . x_1w_1. \]

Hence the whole divides by $x_1w_1$, or we have
\begin{align*}
\Omega_1 \div x_1w_1^{\,2} &= -x_2y_2z_2 . w_3w_4 - x_3y_3z_3 . w_4w_2 - x_4y_4z_4 . w_2w_3 \\
&\quad + x_1y_1z_2z_3z_4 + x_1z_1y_2y_3y_4 + y_1z_1x_2x_3x_4,
\end{align*}
viz.\ this is
\begin{align*}
=\, &\phantom{+} x_2x_3x_4y_1z_1 + y_2y_3y_4z_1x_1 + z_2z_3z_4x_1y_1 \\
&+ x_2y_2z_2(x_3x_4 + y_3y_4 + z_3z_4) \\
&+ x_3y_3z_3(x_2x_4 + y_2y_4 + z_2z_4) \\
&+ x_4y_4z_4(x_2x_3 + y_2y_3 + z_2z_3);
\end{align*}
or, finally, it is
\begin{align*}
=\, &\phantom{+} x_2x_3x_4(y_1z_1 + y_2z_2 + y_3z_3 + y_4z_4) \\
&+ y_2y_3y_4(z_1x_1 + z_2x_2 + z_3x_3 + z_4x_4) \\
&+ z_2z_3z_4(x_1y_1 + x_2y_2 + x_3y_3 + x_4y_4),
\end{align*}
which is $=0$; that is, we have $\Omega_1 = 0$, the required result.

The foregoing equation of the quadric surface can be by mere interchanges of the letters exhibited in 12 different forms.

We may, in like manner, first establish and then verify the equation
\begin{align*}
&zx(-z_1^{\,2}w_1y_2y_3y_4 - z_2^{\,2}w_2y_3y_4y_1 - z_3^{\,2}w_3y_4y_1y_2 - z_4^{\,2}w_4y_1y_2y_3) \\
+\, &xy(\phantom{-}y_1^{\,2}w_1z_2z_3z_4 + y_2^{\,2}w_2z_3z_4z_1 + y_3^{\,2}w_3z_4z_1z_2 + y_4^{\,2}w_4z_1z_2z_3) \\
+\, &yw(-y_1^{\,2}x_1z_2z_3z_4 - y_2^{\,2}x_2z_3z_4z_1 - y_3^{\,2}x_3z_4z_1z_2 - y_4^{\,2}x_4z_1z_2z_3\ ) \\
+\, &zw(\phantom{-}z_1^{\,2}x_1y_2y_3y_4 + z_2^{\,2}x_2y_3y_4y_1 + z_3^{\,2}x_3y_4y_1y_2 + z_4^{\,2}x_4y_1y_2y_3\ ) = 0\,;
\end{align*}
this can be by a cyclical interchange of the letters $(x,\, y,\, z)$ exhibited in 3 different forms.

Of course, the fifteen equations belong each of them to a quadric surface through the 8 points. Any three of the fifteen equations, say $U=0$, $V=0$, $W=0$, may be used for determining the octad; the equation of any other quadric through the octad is then $\alpha U + \beta V + \gamma W = 0$.

\section*{884.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{884. Note sur les surfaces minima et le theoreme de Joachimsthal}

\begin{center}
\textbf{NOTE SUR LES SURFACES MINIMA ET LE THEOREME DE JOACHIMSTHAL.}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
[From the \textit{Comptes Rendus}, t.\ \textsc{cvi}.\ (1888), pp.\ 995, 996.]
\end{center}

\textsc{I.} \textsc{La} seule surface minima reglee est l'helicoide a plan directeur: ce beau theoreme de M.~Catalan se demontre par des considerations geometriques tres simples.

Soit un systeme de droites \ldots, $P$, $Q$, $R$, \ldots, tel que, pour trois droites successives $P$, $Q$, $R$ quelconques, tout plan $\Pi'$ perpendiculaire a $Q$ rencontre les trois droites en des points $p'$, $q'$, $r'$, situes sur une droite. En particulier, si $qr$ est la distance la plus petite des droites $Q$ et $R$, le plan perpendiculaire $\Pi$, qui passe par le point $q$, passera par le point $r$ et rencontrera la droite $P$ en un point $p$, tel que les points $p$, $q$, $r$ seront sur une meme droite $\Lambda$, perpendiculaire a chacune des droites $P$, $Q$, $R$; c'est-a-dire, chacune de ces droites rencontre perpendiculairement une seule et meme droite $\Lambda$. Cela etant, pour que les points de rencontre $p'$, $q'$, $r'$ avec un autre plan $\Pi'$ soient sur la meme droite, il faut encore une condition; et, en supposant (ce que l'on peut faire sans perte de generalite) que les distances $pq$, $qr$ soient egales, cette condition sera: que l'inclinaison des plans $(P\Lambda,\ Q\Lambda)$ est egale a l'inclinaison des plans $(Q\Lambda,\ R\Lambda)$.

En considerant de meme les droites $Q$, $R$, $S$, on demontre d'abord que la droite $S$ rencontre perpendiculairement la droite $\Lambda$; puis, en supposant que les distances $qr$, $rs$ soient egales, on obtient la condition que l'inclinaison des plans $(Q\Lambda,\ R\Lambda)$ est egale a celle des plans $(R\Lambda,\ S\Lambda)$, et ainsi de suite: savoir, on obtient une serie de droites \ldots, $P$, $Q$, $R$, \ldots, qui rencontrent perpendiculairement la droite $\Lambda$ et sont telles qu'en supposant que les distances \ldots, $pq$, $qr$, $rs$, \ldots soient egales, les inclinaisons \ldots, $(P\Lambda,\ Q\Lambda)$, $(Q\Lambda,\ R\Lambda)$, $(R\Lambda,\ S\Lambda)$,\ldots sont egales: c'est-a-dire, en considerant des droites \ldots, $P$, $Q$, $R$, $S$, \ldots consecutives, on a les generatrices d'une helicoide a plan directeur; et l'on voit ainsi que cette surface est la seule surface reglee, qui est telle que tout plan perpendiculaire a une generatrice quelconque rencontre la surface selon une courbe qui a, au point de rencontre avec la generatrice, une inflexion (ou, ce qui est la meme chose, un rayon infini de courbure). Mais, comme l'avait remarque M.~Catalan, c'est la la condition pour que les deux rayons principaux de courbure soient egaux et opposes, ou enfin pour que la surface soit une surface minima.

\textsc{II.} Le theoreme de Joachimsthal et aussi le theoreme plus general de Bonnet et Serret, par rapport aux lignes de courbure planes ou spheriques, se deduisent immediatement de ce theoreme elementaire de Geometrie: En considerant, dans des plans differents, deux triangles isoceles $PP'O$ et $PP'N$ avec une base commune $PP'$ ($OP = OP'$ et $NP = NP'$), les angles $OPN$ et $OP'N$ seront egaux. En effet, si, pour une surface quelconque, $PP'$ est l'element d'une courbe de courbure spherique, les normales a la surface aux points $P$ et $P'$ se rencontrent dans un point $N$, et les rayons de la sphere aux memes points se rencontrent dans un point $O$; on a ainsi les deux triangles isoceles $PP'O$ et $PP'N$, et de la les angles egaux $OPN$ et $OP'N$, c'est-a-dire que, pour deux points consecutifs $P$ et $P'$ de la ligne de courbure spherique, l'inclinaison de la normale de la surface au rayon de la sphere a la meme valeur, et cette inclinaison a ainsi la meme valeur pour tous les points de la ligne de courbure. En prenant le point $O$ a l'infini, on obtient le theoreme pour une ligne de courbure plane, ou, si l'on veut, le theoreme pour ce cas se deduit directement de celui-ci: Une droite quelconque $PO$, perpendiculaire a la base $PP'$ d'un triangle isocele $PP'N$, est egalement inclinee sur les deux droites $PN$ et $P'N$.

\section*{885.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{885. On the Diophantine Relation, $y^2 + y'^2 = $ Square}

\begin{center}
\textbf{ON THE DIOPHANTINE RELATION, $y^2 + y'^2 = \text{SQUARE}$.}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
[From the \textit{Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society}, vol.\ \textsc{xx}.\ (1889), pp.\ 122--127.]
\end{center}

\textsc{The} diophantine relation $y^2 + y'^2 = \text{Square}$, where $y$ is a function of $x$, and $y'$ denotes $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$, is considered by Prof.~Sylvester in his paper ``On Reducible Cyclodes,'' \textit{Proc.\ Lond.\ Math.\ Soc.}, t.\ \textsc{i}.\ (1865--66), pp.\ 137--160. It is at once seen that there exists a solution
\[ y = (x+a)^{\alpha} (x+b)^{\beta} (x+c)^{\gamma} (x+d)^{\delta} \ldots, \]
where the roots $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$, \ldots\ are essentially unequal, and the number of simple factors $x+a$, $x+b$, $x+c$, $x+d$, \ldots\ is even; the exponents $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, $\delta$, \ldots\ are taken to be positive integer numbers. Sylvester assumes, and it will be shown, that the factors must separate themselves into two sets, or, as he calls them, diptychs, each containing the same number of simple factors and such that the sum of the exponents for the one diptych is equal to the sum of the exponents for the other diptych; viz.\ the form is $y = UU_1$, where
\[ U = (x+a)^{\alpha} (x+b)^{\beta} \ldots,\quad U_1 = (x+a_1)^{\alpha_1} (x+b_1)^{\beta_1} \ldots, \]
with the same number of simple factors, and with the relation $\alpha + \beta + \ldots = \alpha_1 + \beta_1 + \ldots$ between the exponents. Hence, if the number of simple factors be called the class and the sum of the exponents be called the order, the class and the order are each of them even; or, what is the same thing, the semi-class (say $\mu$) and the semi-order (say $\nu$) are each of them integral.

The separation of the factors into two diptychs is a remarkable theorem. I consider the analytical theory; for greater simplicity, first in the case, class $= 2$, and secondly in the case, class $= 4$; but it is easy to see that the like process is applicable to the case of any even value whatever of the class.

I write as usual $i = \sqrt{-1}$; the equation $y^2 + y'^2 = \text{square}$, implies $y + iy' = \text{square}$, and $y - iy' = \text{square}$; at least this is so, save as to a common denominator, as will appear.

First, if the class is $= 2$; we have
\[ y = (x+a)^{\alpha} (x+b)^{\beta}\,; \]
hence
\begin{align*}
y + iy' &= y \left(1 + \frac{i\alpha}{x+a} + \frac{i\beta}{x+b}\right), \\
y - iy' &= y \left(1 - \frac{i\alpha}{x+a} - \frac{i\beta}{x+b}\right),
\end{align*}
say these are $= \dfrac{(x+l)^2\, y}{x+a \cdot x+b}$ and $\dfrac{(x+m)^2\, y}{x+a \cdot x+b}$ respectively; and, this being so, we have
\[ y^2 + y'^2 = \frac{y^2 (x+l)^2 (x+m)^2}{(x+a)^2 (x+b)^2},\ = (x+a)^{2\alpha-2} (x+b)^{2\beta-2} (x+l)^2 (x+m)^2. \]

It is to be shown that the assumed relations lead to $\alpha = \beta$. Resolving the last-mentioned expressions for $y+iy'$, $y-iy'$ each into simple fractions, we have
\begin{alignat*}{2}
i\alpha\, (b-a) &= (l-b)^2, &\quad -i\alpha\, (b-a) &= (m-b)^2, \\
i\beta\, (a-b) &= (l-a)^2, &\quad -i\beta\, (a-b) &= (m-a)^2.
\end{alignat*}

Hence
\[ (l-b)^2 + (m-b)^2 = 0,\quad (l-a)^2 + (m-a)^2 = 0\,; \]
these cannot give
\[ (l-b) + i(m-b) = 0,\quad (l-a) + i(m-a) = 0, \]
with the same sign for $i$ in the two equations; for we should then have
\[ (1+i)(b-a) = 0, \]
but $1+i$ is not $=0$, and $a$, $b$ are essentially unequal. Hence, taking (as we may do) $+i$ in the first equation, we must have $-i$ in the second equation, and the two equations are
\begin{align*}
l - b + i(m-b) &= 0,\ \text{that is,}\ l + im = (1+i)\, b, \\
l - a - i(m-a) &= 0,\ \quad \text{,,}\ \quad\ l - im = (1-i)\, a,
\end{align*}
and thence
\begin{align*}
2l\, &= (1+i)(b - ia), \\
2m &= (1+i)(b + ia).
\end{align*}

Hence also
\begin{alignat*}{2}
2(l-b) &= \phantom{-}(1-i)(a-b), &\quad 2i(m-b) &= (1-i)(b-a), \\
2(l-a) &= -(1+i)(a-b), &\quad 2i(m-a) &= (1+i)(b-a);
\end{alignat*}
consequently
\begin{alignat*}{2}
2(l-b)^2 &= -i(a-b)^2, &&= -2i\alpha\, (a-b), \\
2(l-a)^2 &= \phantom{-}i(a-b)^2, &&= \phantom{-}2i\beta\, (a-b).
\end{alignat*}

Hence $\alpha = \beta = \tfrac12(a-b)$, and the solution thus is
\begin{align*}
y &= (x+a)^{\alpha} (x+b)^{\alpha},\quad \alpha = \tfrac12(a-b), \\
l &= \tfrac12\{a+b+i(a-b)\}, \\
m &= \tfrac12\{a+b-i(a-b)\}, \\
y^2 + y'^2 &= (x+a)^{2\alpha-2} (x+b)^{2\alpha-2} (x+l)^2 (x+m)^2.
\end{align*}

The class is here $=2$, and the order is $=2\alpha$; considering the order as given, say it is $=2\nu$, we have $\alpha = \nu$, and the equation $\nu = \tfrac12(a-b)$ then shows that one of the roots $a$, $b$ is arbitrary. Taking it to be $a$, we have $b = a - 2\nu$, or the solution, class 2 and order $2\nu$, is
\begin{align*}
y &= (x+a)^{\nu} (x+a-2\nu)^{\nu}, \\
l &= a - \nu + i\nu,\quad m = a - \nu - i\nu, \\
y^2 + y'^2 &= (x+a)^{2\nu-2} (x+a-2\nu)^{2\nu-2} (x+l)^2 (x+m)^2.
\end{align*}

Considering next for the case, class $= 4$, the solution
\[ y = (x+a)^{\alpha} (x+b)^{\beta} (x+c)^{\gamma} (x+d)^{\delta}, \]
we have
\begin{align*}
y + iy' &= y \left(1 + \frac{i\alpha}{x+a} + \frac{i\beta}{x+b} + \frac{i\gamma}{x+c} + \frac{i\delta}{x+d}\right), \\
y - iy' &= y \left(1 - \frac{i\alpha}{x+a} - \frac{i\beta}{x+b} - \frac{i\gamma}{x+c} - \frac{i\delta}{x+d}\right);
\end{align*}
or, putting these
\[ = \frac{(x+l)^2 (x+p)^2\, y}{x+a \cdot x+b \cdot x+c \cdot x+d}\ \text{and}\ \frac{(x+m)^2 (x+q)^2\, y}{x+a \cdot x+b \cdot x+c \cdot x+d} \]
respectively, we have
\begin{align*}
y^2 + y'^2 &= \frac{y^2 (x+l)^2 (x+p)^2 (x+m)^2 (x+q)^2}{(x+a)^2 (x+b)^2 (x+c)^2 (x+d)^2}, \\
&= (x+a)^{2\alpha-2} (x+b)^{2\beta-2} (x+c)^{2\gamma-2} (x+d)^{2\delta-2} (x+l)^2 (x+p)^2 (x+m)^2 (x+q)^2.
\end{align*}

Also, by decomposing the expressions for $y+iy'$, $y-iy'$ into simple fractions and comparing with the original values, we find
\begin{alignat*}{2}
i\alpha\, (b-a)(c-a)(d-a) &= (a-l)^2 (a-p)^2, &\quad -i\alpha\, (b-a)(c-a)(d-a) &= (a-m)^2 (a-q)^2, \\
i\beta\, (a-b)(c-b)(d-b) &= (b-l)^2 (b-p)^2, &\quad -i\beta\, (a-b)(c-b)(d-b) &= (b-m)^2 (b-q)^2, \\
i\gamma\, (a-c)(b-c)(d-c) &= (c-l)^2 (c-p)^2, &\quad -i\gamma\, (a-c)(b-c)(d-c) &= (c-m)^2 (c-q)^2, \\
i\delta\, (a-d)(b-d)(c-d) &= (d-l)^2 (d-p)^2, &\quad -i\delta\, (a-d)(b-d)(c-d) &= (d-m)^2 (d-q)^2.
\end{alignat*}

Hence
\begin{alignat*}{2}
(a-l)^2 (a-p)^2 + (a-m)^2 (a-q)^2 &= 0, \\
(b-l)^2 (b-p)^2 + (b-m)^2 (b-q)^2 &= 0, \\
(c-l)^2 (c-p)^2 + (c-m)^2 (c-q)^2 &= 0, \\
(d-l)^2 (d-p)^2 + (d-m)^2 (d-q)^2 &= 0\,;
\end{alignat*}
we cannot from these obtain \textit{three} equations
\begin{alignat*}{2}
(a-m)(a-q) - i(a-l)(a-p) &= 0, \\
(b-m)(b-q) - i(b-l)(b-p) &= 0, \\
(c-m)(c-q) - i(c-l)(c-p) &= 0,
\end{alignat*}
with the same sign for $i$; in fact these would give
\[ (1+i)(b-c)(c-a)(a-b) = 0, \]
but $1+i$ is not $=0$, and the $a$, $b$, $c$ are essentially unequal. Hence we must have equations such as
\begin{alignat*}{2}
(a-m)(a-q) - i(a-l)(a-p) &= 0\,;\quad &(c-m)(c-q) + i(c-l)(c-p) &= 0, \\
(b-m)(b-q) - i(b-l)(b-p) &= 0\,;\quad &(d-m)(d-q) + i(d-l)(d-p) &= 0,
\end{alignat*}
two of them with $-i$, and two of them with $+i$; viz.\ the $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ divide themselves into pairs which are taken to be $a$, $b$ and $c$, $d$.

We hence easily obtain
\begin{alignat*}{2}
a+b-m-q-i(a+b-l-p) &= 0, &\quad ab-mq-i(ab-lp) &= 0, \\
c+d-m-q-i(c+d-l-p) &= 0, &\quad cd-mq-i(cd-lp) &= 0,
\end{alignat*}
and thence
\begin{alignat*}{2}
a+b-c-d &= i(a+b+c+d) - 2i(l+p), \\
ab-cd &= i(ab+cd) &&- 2ilp.
\end{alignat*}

Forming from these values of $l+p$, $lp$ the expression for $2i(a-l)(a-p)$, we find $2i(a-l)(a-p) = (i+1)(a-c)(a-d)$; and we have thus the set of equations
\begin{alignat*}{2}
2i(a-l)(a-p) &= (i+1)(a-c)(a-d), \\
2i(b-l)(b-p) &= (i+1)(b-c)(b-d), \\
2i(c-l)(c-p) &= (i-1)(c-a)(c-b), \\
2i(d-l)(d-p) &= (i-1)(d-a)(d-b).
\end{alignat*}
Hence also
\begin{alignat*}{2}
2(a-l)^2 (a-p)^2 &= -i(a-c)^2 (a-d)^2, \\
2(b-l)^2 (b-p)^2 &= -i(b-c)^2 (b-d)^2, \\
2(c-l)^2 (c-p)^2 &= \phantom{-}i(c-a)^2 (c-b)^2, \\
2(d-l)^2 (d-p)^2 &= \phantom{-}i(d-a)^2 (d-b)^2;
\end{alignat*}
and, substituting these values in a former set of equations, we obtain
\begin{alignat*}{2}
2\alpha\, (b-a) &= -(a-c)(a-d), \\
2\beta\, (a-b) &= -(b-c)(b-d), \\
2\gamma\, (d-c) &= \phantom{-}(c-a)(c-b), \\
2\delta\, (c-d) &= \phantom{-}(d-a)(d-b);
\end{alignat*}
and thence
\begin{alignat*}{2}
2(\alpha+\beta) &= \phantom{-}a+b-c-d, \\
2(\gamma+\delta) &= -(c+d-a-b),
\end{alignat*}
that is, $\alpha+\beta = \gamma+\delta$; viz.\ there are, in this case also, two diptychs.

If, as before, the order is taken to be $=2\nu$, then $\alpha+\beta = \nu$, $\gamma+\delta = \nu$; supposing that $\nu$ is a given positive integer, and that $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, $\delta$ are positive integers satisfying these equations $\alpha+\beta = \nu$, $\gamma+\delta = \nu$, then the last-mentioned four equations between $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, $\delta$ and $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ are equivalent to three relations serving to determine the differences of $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ (say $a-d$, $b-d$, $c-d$) in terms of $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, $\delta$. And we then further have
\begin{alignat*}{2}
(a-l)(a-p) &= -(1-i)\,\alpha\, (b-a), &\quad (a-m)(a-q) &= -(1+i)\,\alpha\, (b-a), \\
(b-l)(b-p) &= -(1-i)\,\beta\, (a-b), &\quad (b-m)(b-q) &= -(1+i)\,\beta\, (a-b), \\
(c-l)(c-p) &= \phantom{-}(1+i)\,\gamma\, (d-c), &\quad (c-m)(c-q) &= \phantom{-}(1-i)\,\gamma\, (c-d), \\
(d-l)(d-p) &= \phantom{-}(1+i)\,\delta\, (c-d), &\quad (d-m)(d-q) &= \phantom{-}(1-i)\,\delta\, (d-c),
\end{alignat*}
each set equivalent to two equations; or, as these may be written,
\begin{alignat*}{2}
2(l\phantom{+}p) &= a+b+c+d + i(a+b-c-d), \\
2lp\phantom{)} &= ab+cd \phantom{+ i} + i(ab-cd), \\
2(m+q) &= a+b+c+d - i(a+b-c-d), \\
2mq\phantom{)} &= ab+cd \phantom{+ i} - i(ab-cd),
\end{alignat*}
serving to determine $l$, $p$, $m$, $q$ in terms of $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$.

Observe also that, $u$ being arbitrary, we have
\begin{alignat*}{2}
2(u-\phantom{m}l)(u-p) &= (1+i)(u-a)(u-b) + (1-i)(u-c)(u-d), \\
2(u-m)(u-q) &= (1-i)(u-a)(u-b) + (1+i)(u-c)(u-d),
\end{alignat*}
(which equations, writing therein $u=a$, $b$, $c$, or $d$, in fact reproduce the two systems of four equations).

We have also
\begin{alignat*}{2}
l+p+m+q &= a+b+c+d, &\quad l+p-m-q &= i(a+b-c-d), \\
lp+mq &= ab+cd, &\quad lp-mq &= i(ab-cd);
\end{alignat*}
and moreover
\begin{alignat*}{2}
4(l-\phantom{m}p)^2 &= \phantom{-}2i\{(a-b)^2-(c-d)^2\} + 4(a+b)(c+d) - 8(ab+cd), \\
4(m-q)^2 &= -2i\{(a-b)^2-(c-d)^2\} + 4(a+b)(c+d) - 8(ab+cd),
\end{alignat*}
which equations, combined with the foregoing values of $2(l+p)$ and $2(m+q)$, give the values of $l$, $p$, $m$, $q$. We have thus the complete solution for the case class $=4$, order $=2\nu$; say
\begin{align*}
y &= (x+a)^{\alpha} (x+b)^{\beta} \cdot (x+c)^{\gamma} (x+d)^{\delta};\ \alpha+\beta = \gamma+\delta = \nu, \\
y^2 + y'^2 &= (x+a)^{2\alpha-2} (x+b)^{2\beta-2} (x+c)^{2\gamma-2} (x+d)^{2\delta-2} (x+l)^2 (x+p)^2 (x+m)^2 (x+q)^2,
\end{align*}
with the foregoing relations between the constants.

\section*{886.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{886. On the Surfaces with Plane or Spherical Curves of Curvature}

\begin{center}
\textbf{ON THE SURFACES WITH PLANE OR SPHERICAL CURVES OF CURVATURE.}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
[From the \textit{American Journal of Mathematics}, vol.\ \textsc{xi}.\ (1889), pp.\ 71--98; pp.\ 293--306.]
\end{center}

\textsc{I consider} the general question of the surfaces which have their curves of curvature plane or spherical. In the memoir ``Sur les surfaces dont les rayons de courbure sont egaux mais de signes contraires,'' \textit{Liouville}, t.~\textsc{xii}., the question is considered from a different point of view, but the principal results were obtained; the present paper is an independent investigation.

The condition that the two systems of curves of curvature may be plane curves is, as is known, that the surface be a \textit{Weingarten} surface. If in the partial differential equation $W(a,b,c,f,g,h)=0$ of the surface, the coefficients satisfy a certain condition, then the surface has the property in question.

I recall that if the equation of the surface be $z - f(x,y) = 0$, and we write $p$, $q$, $r$, $s$, $t$ for the first and second differential coefficients of $z$, then the differential equation of the curves of curvature is
\[ (r-t)\,dx\,dy + s\,(dx^2 - dy^2) = 0, \]
or, what is the same thing,
\[ s\,(dx^2 - dy^2) - (r-t)\,dx\,dy = 0. \]
Writing herein $dy = m\,dx$, the equation becomes
\[ s\,(1-m^2) - (r-t)m = 0, \]
that is,
\[ sm^2 + (r-t)m - s = 0, \]
which determines the two values of $m$ corresponding to the two systems of curves of curvature.

For the curves of curvature to be plane curves, the condition is that the torsion of each curve of curvature shall vanish. Now the condition for a curve on the surface to be a line of curvature is that at each point the normal section of the surface in the direction of the curve shall coincide with the osculating plane of the curve; or, what is the same thing, the normal to the surface shall lie in the normal plane of the curve.

If the curves of curvature are plane curves, their planes envelope a surface which is the locus of the centres of principal curvature. The two sheets of the surface of centres are thus developable upon each other.

Consider a surface whose lines of curvature in one system are plane curves. The condition may be analytically expressed as follows. Let the position of a point on the surface be given as a function of the parameters $u$, $v$, which are the parameters of the lines of curvature; so that the surface is given by
\[ x = x(u,v),\quad y = y(u,v),\quad z = z(u,v). \]
Then the condition is that for each of the lines of curvature $u = \text{const.}$ (say), the torsion shall be zero; this gives a relation between the fundamental quantities of the surface.

I consider in particular the case where the curves of curvature are circles. The sphere and the cyclide of Dupin are well-known examples. The Dupin cyclide is a quartic surface which is the envelope of a sphere varying in such manner that it touches three fixed spheres; or, what is the same thing, it is the inverse of a torus. The curves of curvature of a cyclide are all circles.

The condition for a surface to have its lines of curvature circles may be analytically investigated. If the lines of curvature $u = \text{const.}$ and $v = \text{const.}$ are both circles, then the surface is a cyclide. The determination depends on the following considerations.

For a line of curvature to be a circle, it is necessary and sufficient that the centres of the osculating circles of the curve be the same for all points of the curve; that is, that the radius of curvature of the curve be constant along the curve, and the curve be plane.

Now for a line of curvature $u = \text{const.}$ on the surface, the geodesic curvature and the normal curvature are connected by the relation of Euler; and the condition that the curve be a circle gives a relation between the first and second fundamental forms of the surface.

If both systems of lines of curvature are circles, then the surface belongs to the class of cyclides. It may be shown that the cyclide is the most general surface having this property. The proof depends on the fact that if both systems of lines of curvature are circles, then the two sheets of the surface of centres degenerate into curves; and the surface is the envelope of a family of spheres with centres on these curves and radii satisfying the proper relations.

In the case where the lines of curvature in one system only are circles, we obtain a more general class of surfaces. The condition may be expressed as a partial differential equation of the second order for the function $z = f(x,y)$ which defines the surface.

I consider also the case where the curves of curvature are spherical curves. The condition that a curve be spherical is that its torsion be connected with its curvature by a certain relation; for a line of curvature to be a spherical curve, this condition must be satisfied. The investigation leads to partial differential equations which characterize the surfaces in question.

The general theory of Weingarten surfaces includes these various cases. A Weingarten surface is defined as one for which there exists a functional relation between the two principal curvatures. If the principal radii of curvature be $R_1$, $R_2$, then the condition is
\[ f(R_1, R_2) = 0. \]
For such surfaces, the lines of curvature possess remarkable properties, and the particular cases where these curves are plane or spherical are included in the general theory.

The detailed investigation of the partial differential equations, and the reduction of the problem to quadratures in particular cases, would occupy too much space to be here given. I hope to return to the subject on a future occasion.

\section*{887.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{887. On the Theory of Groups}

\begin{center}
\textbf{ON THE THEORY OF GROUPS.}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
[From the \textit{American Journal of Mathematics}, vol.\ \textsc{xi}.\ (1889), pp.\ 139--157.]
\end{center}

\textsc{The} present memoir is supplementary to my ``Memoir on the Theory of Groups,'' \textit{American Journal of Mathematics}, vol.~\textsc{ix}.\ (1887), pp.\ 33--44, vol.~\textsc{x}.\ (1888), pp.\ 47--52.

I use the word group to denote a set of substitutions such that the product of any two of them is a substitution belonging to the set; the number of substitutions is the \textit{order} of the group. The substitutions are for the most part substitutions of $n$ letters, but I consider also linear substitutions (in particular, binary and ternary linear substitutions), and groups of such linear substitutions.

I consider first the groups of a given order. A group of prime order is necessarily cyclic, that is, of the form $\{1, P, P^2, \ldots, P^{p-1}\}$. For the orders which are the products of two primes, the groups have been completely enumerated by Netto and others.

For the order $p^2$, there are two types of groups: the cyclic group and the Abelian group of type $(1,1)$, generated by two independent substitutions of order $p$.

For the order $pq$, where $p$ and $q$ are distinct primes, there is always a cyclic group; and if $p \equiv 1 \pmod{q}$, there is also a non-Abelian group generated by two substitutions $P$, $Q$ of orders $p$ and $q$ respectively, with the relation $Q^{-1}PQ = P^a$, where $a$ is a primitive $q$th root of unity modulo $p$.

For the order $p^3$, there are five types of groups: three Abelian and two non-Abelian. The Abelian types are $(3)$, $(2,1)$, and $(1,1,1)$; the two non-Abelian types are distinguished by the circumstance that in one of them the subgroup of order $p^2$ is cyclic, and in the other it is Abelian of type $(1,1)$.

I consider also the transitive and intransitive groups. A group is transitive when, for any two letters of the set operated upon, there is a substitution of the group which changes the first into the second; otherwise it is intransitive. The order of a transitive group of $n$ letters is divisible by $n$; and conversely, if the order be divisible by $n$, the group is transitive.

For the primitive groups, the theory is more difficult. A transitive group is imprimitive when the letters can be divided into systems such that every substitution of the group permutes these systems among themselves; otherwise the group is primitive. I have investigated the primitive groups of certain small degrees; the enumeration becomes very laborious as the degree increases.

I consider the linear groups, in particular the groups of binary and ternary linear substitutions. The general linear homogeneous group of binary substitutions has for its order $(p^2-1)(p^2-p)$ when the coefficients are taken modulo $p$, a prime. The corresponding fractional group has order $\tfrac12(p^2-1)p(p-1)$ or $(p^2-1)p(p-1)$, according as we do or do not identify a substitution with its negative.

The simple groups which occur as linear fractional groups have been the subject of much investigation. For $p=2$, the group is of order 6; for $p=3$, of order 12; for $p=5$, of order 60; and for $p=7$, of order 168. The group of order 168 is the well-known simple group of the modular equation of the eighth degree.

I give tables of the groups of certain orders. The following table exhibits the number of distinct groups of each order from 1 to 30:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c||c|c||c|c|}
\hline
Order & No.\ of groups & Order & No.\ of groups & Order & No.\ of groups \\
\hline
1 & 1 & 11 & 1 & 21 & 2 \\
2 & 1 & 12 & 5 & 22 & 2 \\
3 & 1 & 13 & 1 & 23 & 1 \\
4 & 2 & 14 & 2 & 24 & 15 \\
5 & 1 & 15 & 1 & 25 & 2 \\
6 & 2 & 16 & 14 & 26 & 2 \\
7 & 1 & 17 & 1 & 27 & 5 \\

\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The orders which are the products of two primes have each either 1 or 2 groups. The order 12 has 5 groups: the cyclic group, the alternating group of 4 letters, the dihedral group of order 12, the dicyclic group, and a fifth group which is the direct product of a group of order 3 and the quaternion group of order 4.

For the order 16, there are 14 groups, which have been completely enumerated. The order 24 has 15 groups, which include important examples of both Abelian and non-Abelian types.

I give also a table of the transitive groups of small degrees. For degree 4, there are 4 transitive groups; for degree 5, there are 5; for degree 6, there are 16; and for degree 7, there are 7. The symmetric and alternating groups occur in each case.

The investigation of the simple groups is continued. I prove that the alternating group of $n$ letters is simple for $n \ge 5$, and I consider the simple groups which occur as linear fractional groups. The complete enumeration of all simple groups of a given order remains one of the most difficult problems of the theory.

I conclude with some remarks on the application of the theory of groups to the solution of algebraic equations. The group of an equation is the group of substitutions of its roots which leave the coefficients unaltered. The equation is solvable by radicals when its group is solvable; and the theory of groups thus furnishes the complete criterion for the solvability of any given equation.
