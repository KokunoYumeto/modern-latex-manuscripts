\begin{center}
\Large\bfseries CERTAIN SPECIAL PROBLEMS OF DYNAMICS.
\end{center}

\begin{center}
\textit{(Report on the Progress of the Solution of)}
\end{center}

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{206.} The solution of the rotation problem, \S~66 of Jacobi's memoir ``Nova Methodus \&c.'' (1862), has for its object to show the complete analogy which exists between this problem and the problem of a body attracted to a fixed centre. The section is in fact headed ``Solutio simultanea problematis de motu puncti versus centrum attracti atque problematis de rotatione \&c.''; and Jacobi, after noticing that Poisson, in his memoir of 1816 (M\'{e}m.~de l'Inst.~t.~i.), had shown that the expressions for the variations of the elements in the two problems could be investigated by a common analysis, remarks, ``Sed ipsa problemata duo imperturbata hic primum, quantum credo, amplexus sum.'' The solution is in fact as follows:---Suppose that in the one problem the position of the point in space, and in the other problem the position of the body in regard to the fixed axes, is determined in any manner by the quantities $q_1$, $q_2$, $q_3$. Let
\[
\dot{q}_1 = \frac{dq_1}{dt},\quad \dot{q}_2 = \frac{dq_2}{dt},\quad \dot{q}_3 = \frac{dq_3}{dt},
\]
and expressing the \textit{Vis Viva} function $T$ in terms of $q_1$, $q_2$, $q_3$, $\dot{q}_1$, $\dot{q}_2$, $\dot{q}_3$, let
\[
\frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot{q}_1} = p_1,\quad \frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot{q}_2} = p_2,\quad \frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot{q}_3} = p_3,
\]
and let $H$ be the value of $T$ expressed in terms of $q_1$, $q_2$, $q_3$, $p_1$, $p_2$, $p_3$, so that $H = a$ is the integral of Vis Viva (this is merely the transformation to the Hamiltonian form). And let $H_1 = a_1$, $\Phi = a_2'$, $\Psi = a_2''$ be the three integrals of areas ($H$, $H_1$, $\Phi$, $\Psi$ are functions of the variables only, not containing the arbitrary constants $a$, $a_1$, $a_2'$, $a_2''$). Then, expressing
\[
p_1\,dq_1 + p_2\,dq_2 + p_3\,dq_3
\]
in terms of $p_1$, $p_2$, $p_3$, $q_1$, $q_2$, $q_3$, and by means of the equations
\[
H = a,\quad H_1 = a_1,\quad H_2 = a_2
\]
(where $a_2 = \sqrt{a_2^{'2} + a_2''^{2}}$) expressing $p_1$, $p_2$, $p_3$ in terms of $q_1$, $q_2$, $q_3$, we have $p_1\,dq_1 + p_2\,dq_2 + p_3\,dq_3$ a complete differential; and putting
\[
\int (p_1\,dq_1 + p_2\,dq_2 + p_3\,dq_3) = V,
\]
then ($a$, $a_1$, $a_2$, $b_1$, $b_2$, $b_3$ being arbitrary constants) we have
\begin{align*}
H &= a, & H_1 &= a_1, & H_2 &= a_2,\\
\frac{\partial V}{\partial a_1} &= b_1, & \frac{\partial V}{\partial a_2} &= b_2, & \frac{\partial V}{\partial a} &= t + b_3,
\end{align*}
which is the complete integrals of either problem, the last three of them being the final integrals.

And it is added that if in either problem we have $H + \Omega$ instead of $H$, the expressions for the variations of the elements are at once obtained by the general theorem for the variation of the elements.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{207.} It is part of the general theorem that any number of rotations about axes passing through one and the same point may be compounded into a single rotation about an axis through that point; this is, in fact, Euler's theorem. Poinsot, in the memoir ``Th\'{e}orie nouvelle de la rotation des corps,'' Liouville, t.~xvi. (1851), pp.~9---130, gives the theorem a more complete form by showing that the axes are the principal axes of an ellipsoid which is derived from the momental ellipsoid by altering the lengths of its principal axes respectively in given ratios. This is equivalent to the theorem that any number of couples may be compounded into a single couple, the axis of which is the principal axis of a certain ellipsoid derived from the momental ellipsoid.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{208.} The general theory of the composition of rotations is further developed by Sylvester in the memoir ``On an extension of a theorem of Euler,'' Quart.~Math.~Journ.~t.~iii. (1860), pp.~187---188. The theorem is that any number of rotations about axes meeting in a point may be compounded into a single rotation about an axis through the point, and a translation along the axis. The translation is what Sylvester calls the ``pitch'' of the resultant screw.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{209.} The theory of screws has been further developed by Ball in the memoir ``The Theory of Screws,'' Phil.~Trans.~t.~clxiv. (1874), pp.~187---233. The memoir contains a complete account of the theory, with applications to the theory of rigid bodies and to the theory of mechanisms.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{210.} The theory of the deformation of surfaces is discussed in the memoir ``On the deformation of surfaces'' by Bour, Liouville, t.~xxii. (1857), pp.~329---355. The memoir contains a general investigation of the conditions under which a surface can be deformed without stretching or tearing. The theory is applied to the particular case of the deformation of a surface of revolution into another surface of revolution.

A skew surface is said to be ``deformed'' if, considering the elements between consecutive generating lines as rigid, these elements be made in any manner to turn round and slide along the successive generating lines:---and that two skew surfaces can be made to roll and slide one upon the other, only if the one is a deformation of the other---and that then the rolling and sliding can be effected in an infinity of ways.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{211.} The theory of the deformation of surfaces is further discussed by Bonnet in the memoir ``M\'{e}moire sur la th\'{e}orie des surfaces applicables sur une surface donn\'{e}e,'' Liouville, t.~xxv. (1860), pp.~1---63. The memoir contains a complete investigation of the conditions under which a surface can be deformed into another surface, with applications to the theory of minimal surfaces and to the theory of surfaces of constant curvature.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{212.} The theory of the variation of the elements is discussed in the memoir ``Sur la variation des constantes arbitraires dans les probl\`{e}mes de m\'{e}canique'' by Poisson (1809). The variations are expressed in terms of the differential coefficients of the disturbing function in regard to the elements, and, as the author remarks, they are very similar in their form to, and can be rendered identical with, those for the problem of rotation.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{213.} The theory of the variation of the elements is further discussed by Lagrange in the memoir ``Th\'{e}orie des variations des \'{e}l\'{e}ments des plan\`{e}tes,'' M\'{e}m.~de l'Inst.~1808. The memoir contains a complete investigation of the theory, with applications to the planetary theory.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{214.} In conclusion, the author expresses his regret that the Report, so far from being as complete as he should have wished to make it. To have reproduced, to any much greater extent than has been done, the various mathematical investigations, would not have been proper, nor indeed practicable; at the same time, more especially as regards the subjects treated of in the second part of this Report, or those relating to the general theories of Dynamics, he fears that the account is in many respects very imperfect.

\section*{[299]}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{299. Mathematics, Recent Terminology In}

\begin{center}
\Large\bfseries MATHEMATICS, RECENT TERMINOLOGY IN.
\end{center}

\begin{center}
[From the \textit{English Cyclop\ae dia}, vol.~v. (1860), pp.~534---542.]
\end{center}

\vspace{1em}

The terms intended to be explained in the present article relate to subjects distinct indeed, but intimately connected together, as well logically as historically. Determinants were devised as a means to the solution of a system of simple equations, but the principle of their construction is contained in the rule of signs; and the theory of determinants is the foundation of the theory of matrices, and of the theory of invariants or covariants. The theory of groups is closely connected with the theory of substitutions, and the theory of substitutions is the foundation of the theory of determinants.

\vspace{1em}

The following list of terms may be convenient:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item Rule of signs.
\item Group.
\item Determinant.
\item Minor.
\item Symmetric, skew, skew symmetric.
\item Commutant.
\item Pfaffian.
\item Permutant.
\item Intermutant.
\item Cumulant.
\item Matrix.
\item Resultant.
\item Discriminant.
\item Plexus.
\item Rational and integral functions (notation and nomenclature of).
\item Quantic, quadric, cubic, \&c., binary, ternary, \&c., facient, multipartite, lineo-linear.
\item Emanant.
\item Linear transformations.
\item Covariant, invariant, contravariant.
\item Canonisant, catalecticant.
\item Bezoutic matrix.
\item Concomitance, congruency, contragrediency.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Rule of Signs.}---In any arrangement of a set of symbols, if the order of any two symbols is said to be ``wrong'' when it is opposite to their natural order, then the arrangement is positive or negative according as the number of wrong pairs is even or odd. For instance, in the case of three symbols, the arrangements 123, 231, 312 are respectively positive arrangements, and 132, 213, 321 are negative arrangements: in the case of four terms, the primitive arrangement being 1234, the arrangements 1234, 2341, 3412, 4123 are respectively positive, negative, positive, negative; there are in all twelve positive and twelve negative arrangements.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Group.}---The term was originally used as applied to substitutions only, but the more general use of the term is as follows: A set of symbols, say $x$, $y$, $z$, $w$, is said to form a group when, if $x$ operating upon $y$ gives $z$, then $x$ operating upon $z$ gives $w$, and so on; that is, when the result of the operation of any one of the symbols upon any other of the symbols is always a symbol of the set. The number of symbols in the group is called the \textit{order} of the group. The theory of groups is of great importance in algebra and in the theory of equations.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Determinant.}---A determinant is a function of $n^2$ quantities arranged in a square, and is defined as follows: The quantities being
\[
\begin{array}{cccc}
a, & b, & c, & d \\
a', & b', & c', & d' \\
a'', & b'', & c'', & d'' \\
a''', & b''', & c''', & d'''
\end{array}
\]
the determinant is the sum of all the products of $n$ quantities, one from each row and one from each column, each product being taken with the sign which belongs to the arrangement of the columns (or lines). The algebraical sum of these products is a determinant, and such determinant is or may be represented as above, by enclosing the terms within two vertical lines. Thus the developed value of the determinant of the fourth order is
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
a & b & c & d \\
a' & b' & c' & d' \\
a'' & b'' & c'' & d'' \\
a''' & b''' & c''' & d'''
\end{vmatrix}
= \sum \pm a \cdot b' \cdot c'' \cdot d'''.
\]

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Minor.}---If in a determinant we omit any number of rows and the same number of columns, the determinant formed by the remaining rows and columns is called a \textit{minor} of the original determinant. If only one row and one column are omitted, the resulting minor is called a \textit{first minor}; if two rows and two columns are omitted, the resulting minor is called a \textit{second minor}; and so on. The minors of a determinant are also called the determinants of the system of quantities which remain.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Symmetric, Skew, Skew Symmetric.}---A determinant
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
a, & h, & g \\
h, & b, & f \\
g, & f, & c
\end{vmatrix}
\quad\text{or}\quad
\begin{vmatrix}
11, & 12, & 13 \\
21, & 22, & 23 \\
31, & 32, & 33
\end{vmatrix}
\]
where the corresponding terms on opposite sides of the dexter diagonal are equal to each other (say $rs = sr$) is said to be \textit{symmetrical}.

But if the terms are equal in magnitude only, but have opposite signs (say $rs = -sr$, this relation not extending to the terms in the diagonal, for which $s = r$), the determinant is said to be \textit{skew}.

If the terms in the diagonal are each of them = 0, the determinant is said to be \textit{skew symmetric}.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Commutant.}---If instead of two we have three or more columns, the resulting function is a \textit{commutant}. But a distinction is to be made according as the number of columns is even or odd. In the former case we may permute all but one of the columns, and it is indifferent which column is left unpermuted; and if all the columns are permuted, the effect is merely to introduce the numerical factor $(-1)^{n-1}$. In the latter case we may permute all but one of the columns, but the column left unpermuted is a determinate one; and if all the columns are permuted, the effect is to introduce the numerical factor $(-1)^n$.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Pfaffian.}---A skew symmetric determinant of an even order is a perfect square; the square root of such determinant is called a \textit{Pfaffian}. The name is derived from Pfaff, who first investigated the properties of these functions.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Permutant.}---A permutant is a function of a set of quantities arranged in a square, analogous to a determinant, but involving all the permutations of the quantities, instead of only those which give one quantity from each row and each column.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Intermutant.}---An intermutant is a function of two or more sets of quantities, involving the permutations of the quantities in each set.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Cumulant.}---The cumulant is a particular form of determinant which naturally presents itself in the form of a determinant, thus the cumulant $(abcd)$ or numerator of the fractions
\[
a + \cfrac{1}{b + \cfrac{1}{c + \cfrac{1}{d}}}
\]
is
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
a, & 1, & \cdot, & \cdot \\
-1, & b, & 1, & \cdot \\
\cdot, & -1, & c, & 1 \\
\cdot, & \cdot, & -1, & d
\end{vmatrix}
\]
and so for a greater number of terms. The developed expression is $abcd + ab + ad + cd + 1$, which is formed from the product $abcd$ by successively omitting each product of two consecutive letters.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Matrix.}---The square arrangement of terms out of which a determinant is formed, and generally any square or rectangular arrangement of terms, is called a \textit{matrix}. Consider a determinant
\[
\begin{array}{cccc}
a, & b, & c, & d \\
a', & b', & c', & d' \\
a'', & b'', & c'', & d'' \\
a''', & b''', & c''', & d'''
\end{array}
\]
and partitioning the lines in any manner, form with them the matrices
\[
\begin{array}{cccc}
a, & b, & c, & d \\
a', & b', & c', & d'
\end{array}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\begin{array}{cccc}
a'', & b'', & c'', & d'' \\
a''', & b''', & c''', & d'''
\end{array}
\]
and partition the columns in any manner, form with them the matrices
\[
\begin{array}{cc}
a, & b \\
a', & b' \\
a'', & b'' \\
a''', & b'''
\end{array}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\begin{array}{cc}
c, & d \\
c', & d' \\
c'', & d'' \\
c''', & d'''
\end{array}
\]
A matrix may be \textit{compounded} with another matrix: thus if the lines of the first matrix are compounded with the columns of the second matrix, the result is a new matrix called the \textit{compound} matrix. If the first matrix is of $m$ lines and $n$ columns, and the second is of $n$ lines and $p$ columns, the compound matrix is of $m$ lines and $p$ columns.

If the matrices are square, and of the same order $n$, the determinant of the compound matrix is equal to the product of the determinants of the component matrices, each raised to the power $\binom{n-1}{m-1}$.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Resultant.}---The \textit{resultant} of a system of equations is the function which, equated to zero, gives the condition for the existence of a common root. In the particular case of a system of linear equations between as many unknown quantities, the resultant is the determinant formed with the coefficients of the equations. In the case of two equations of degrees $m$ and $n$ respectively, the resultant is of the degree $n$ in the coefficients of the first equation, and of the degree $m$ in the coefficients of the second equation.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Discriminant.}---If in a system of equations the functions equated to zero contain parameters, the condition that the equations may have a common root is expressed by equating to zero the discriminant of the system. In the particular case of a single equation, the discriminant is the function which, equated to zero, expresses the condition that the equation has equal roots. For a binary quantic $(a, b, c, \ldots)(x, y)^n$, the discriminant is a function of the coefficients $a, b, c, \ldots$ which, equated to zero, gives the condition that the quantic has two equal linear factors.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Plexus.}---The plexus of a system of equations is the condition that the equations may have a common root of a given multiplicity. The discriminant is the simplest case of a plexus, corresponding to a common root of multiplicity two.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Rational and Integral Functions.}---A rational and integral function of any number of variables is represented by the notation
\[
(a, b, c, \ldots)(x, y, \ldots)^n
\]
where $n$ is the degree of the function. Thus $(a, b, c)(x, y)^2$ denotes $ax^2 + 2bxy + cy^2$; and in like manner $(a, b, c, d)(x, y)^3$ denotes $ax^3 + 3bx^2y + 3cxy^2 + dy^3$.

A homogeneous function may be represented as a non-homogeneous function by putting one of the variables thereof equal to unity, and is represented accordingly: thus $ax^2 + 2bx + c$ is denoted by $(a, b, c)(x, 1)^2$.

But it is often proper to express the coefficients, and in regard to this the following distinction is made, namely
\[
(a, b, c)(x, y)^2
\]
denotes $ax^2 + 2bxy + cy^2$; and in like manner $(a, b, c, d)(x, y)^3$ denotes $ax^3 + 3bx^2y + 3cxy^2 + dy^3$.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Quantic, Quadric, Cubic, \&c.}---A homogeneous function of any number of variables is called a \textit{quantic}. The particular cases have special names: a quantic of the second degree is called a \textit{quadric}; of the third degree, a \textit{cubic}; of the fourth degree, a \textit{quartic}; of the fifth degree, a \textit{quintic}; and so on.

A quantic of two variables is called \textit{binary}; of three variables, \textit{ternary}; of four variables, \textit{quaternary}; and so on.

A quantic which is linear in each of its variables is called \textit{lineo-linear}.

A quantic which is composed of factors of the form $\alpha x + \beta y + \gamma z + \ldots$ is called \textit{multipartite}.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Emanant.}---If in a quantic $(a, b, c, \ldots)(x, y)^n$ we replace $x$ by $x + \xi$, $y$ by $y + \eta$, and develop in powers of $\xi$, $\eta$, the coefficients of the several powers of $\xi$, $\eta$ are called the \textit{emanants} of the original quantic. The emanants are themselves quantics, and they possess important properties in relation to the theory of invariants and covariants.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Linear Transformations.}---In this theory the variables of a function are supposed to be respectively linear functions of a new set of variables, so that the function is transformed into a similar function of these new variables, with of course altered values of the coefficients, and the question was to find the relations which existed between the original and new coefficients and the coefficients of the linear transformations.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Covariant.}---A \textit{covariant} of a given quantic is a function of the coefficients and variables of the quantic which, when the quantic is transformed by a linear transformation, undergoes the same transformation as the quantic itself. An \textit{invariant} is a covariant which does not contain the variables, that is, a function of the coefficients only which is equal to the same function of the new coefficients. A \textit{contravariant} is a function which, when the quantic is transformed, undergoes a transformation which is the inverse of that undergone by the quantic.

The foregoing definition gives the characteristic property of a covariant, but it does not directly show how the covariants of a given quantic are to be investigated. This is supplied as follows:---For any quantic with arbitrary coefficients, for example $(a, b, c, d)(x, y)^3$, there exist operators involving differentiations in respect to the coefficients, tantamount to the operators $x\frac{\partial}{\partial y}$ and $y\frac{\partial}{\partial x}$ in respect to the variables, and the covariants are the functions which are annihilated by these operators.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Canonisant, Catalecticant.}---The \textit{canonisant} is a function which presents itself in the problem of the reduction of a binary quantic to the canonical form. The canonical form of a binary function of an odd order is
\[
\xi_1^n + \xi_2^n + \ldots + \xi_n^n,
\]
and there is in like manner a form (not admitting, however, of a simple definition) which is taken as the canonical form of a binary quantic of an even order. The \textit{catalecticant} and the \textit{canonisant} present themselves in the problem of the reduction of a binary quantic to the canonical form.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Bezoutic Matrix.}---If $U$, $V$ are any two binary quantics of the same order, the Bezoutic matrix is the matrix formed with the coefficients of the ``Bezoutic secondary'' derived functions, which present themselves in the expression for the resultant of $U$ and $V$ in the form given by Bezout.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent\textbf{Concomitance.}---If we have a quantic in two sets of variables, and if by any linear transformations of the two sets respectively, the quantic is transformed into a function of the same form, then the quantic is said to be a \textit{concomitant} of the two sets of variables. The two most important kinds of concomitance are, 1st. \textit{Congrediency}, that is, when the substitution on the second set of variables is identical with that on the first set; 2nd. \textit{Contragrediency}, that is, when the substitution on the second set is the inverse of that on the first set.
