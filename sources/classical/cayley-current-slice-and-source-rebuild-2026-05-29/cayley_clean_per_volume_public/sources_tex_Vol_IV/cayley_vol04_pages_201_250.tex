\paragraph{27.}
It  is  clear  that  we  have  in  general
\begin{multline*}
\Delta^{-1} D^r H = \frac{1}{[n-1]^{\underline{1}}} \Bigg[
    [n-2]^0 [n-2]^r \, \{1, 2, (r+1)\} \\
  + [n-2]^1 [n-2]^{r-1} \, \{1, 3, (r)\} \\
  + \frac{[r]^{\underline{2}}}{[2]^{\underline{2}}} [n-2]^2 [n-2]^{r-2} \, \{1, 4, (r-1)\} \\
  + \cdots \\
  + [n-2]^r [n-2]^0 \, \{1, r+2, (1)\} \\
  + R_1'    [n-2]^1 [n-2]^{r-1} \, \{2, 3, (r-1)\} \\
  + R_2''   [n-2]^2 [n-2]^{r-2} \, \{2, 4, (r-2)\} \\
  + \cdots \\
  + R_{r-1}^{(r-1)} [n-2]^{r-1} [n-2]^1 \, \{2, r+1, (1)\} \\
  + R_1''    [n-2]^2 [n-2]^{r-2} \, \{3, 4, (r-3)\} \\
  + \cdots \\
  + R_{r-2}^{(r-2)} [n-2]^{r-2} [n-2]^2 \, \{3, r, (1)\} \\
  + \cdots \\
  + \begin{cases}
    \frac{1}{[\frac{1}{2}r]^{\underline{\frac{1}{2}r}}} [n-2]^{\frac{1}{2}r} [n-2]^{\frac{1}{2}r} \, \{r+1, r+2, (1)\}, & r \text{ even}; \text{ or} \\
    \frac{1}{[\frac{1}{2}(r-1)]^{\underline{\frac{1}{2}(r-1)}}} [n-2]^{\frac{1}{2}(r+1)} [n-2]^{\frac{1}{2}(r-1)} \, \{(r+1), (r+3), (2)\}, & r \text{ odd}
    \end{cases}
\Bigg],
\end{multline*}
and  the  general  term  is
\[
\frac{[n-1]^{\underline{1}} R_s^{\underline{S}} [n-2]^{S+1} [n-2]^{r-S-s}}{[S+1]^{\underline{S}}}
    \{S+1, S+2+s, (r-2S+1-s)\},
\]
where  $s$  extends  from  $s = 0$  to  $s = r - 2S$,  and  $S$  from  $S = 0$  to  $S = \frac{1}{2}r$  or  $\frac{1}{2}(r-1)$,  according
as  $r$  is  even  or  odd.     The  expression  for  the  coefficient  $R_1$  is
\[
R_1 = \frac{[r]^{\underline{s}}}{[s]^{\underline{s}} [r-s]^{\underline{s}}},
\]
and  that  of  the  other  coefficients  $R_s$ ($s = $ or $< 1$)  is  not  required  for  the  present  purpose.

\paragraph{29.}
I  write  now
\[
\mathrm{III} = \frac{1}{[n-1]^{\underline{1}}} \Delta^{-1} D^{n-2} H,
\]
and  I  consider  the  expression
\[
\frac{1}{[n-1]^{\underline{1}}} \Delta^{-1} D^{n-1} H \times \mathrm{III},
\]
the  general  term  of  which  is
\[
\frac{R_s^{\underline{S}} \{S+1, S+2+s, (r-2S+1-s)\}}
     {[n-1]^{\underline{1}} [n-2]^S [n-2]^{r-S}}
  \times [n-1]^{\underline{1}} \cdot \frac{[q]^{\underline{\alpha}} [q-\alpha]^{\underline{\beta}}}
     {[n-2]^\alpha [n-3]^\alpha [n-3]^\beta \cdots},
\]
or,  as  this  may  be  written,  putting  $q = n - S - 1$,
\begin{multline*}
R_s^{\underline{S}} \{S+1, S+2+s, (r-2S+1-s)\} \\
\times \frac{[n-2]^S [n-2]^{r-S}}
     {[n-1]^{\underline{1}} [n-2]^\alpha [n-3]^\alpha [n-3]^\beta \cdots} + \text{\&c.}
\end{multline*}

\paragraph{30.}
I  assume  $r > n - 2$;  we  have  then  $\alpha + \beta = r - S > n - S - 2$,  and  therefore
$\alpha + \beta < q$.     The  general  term  of  the  series  in  $\{\}$  is
\[
\pm \frac{[q]^{\underline{\alpha}} [q-\alpha]^{\underline{\beta}}}
     {[n-2-\alpha]^\beta [n-2-\alpha]^\beta \cdots},
\]
where  the  terms  for  which  $n - 2 - \alpha$  is  negative  are  to  be  excluded,  or  what  is  the
same  thing,  the  series  is  not  to  be  continued  beyond  $\alpha = n - 2$.  But  observing  that
$[q]^\alpha$  vanishes  for  $\alpha > q$,  that  is,  $\alpha > n - S - 1$,  it  is  in  fact  the  same  thing  whether  the
series  is  continued  indefinitely  or  only  to  the  term  for  which  $\alpha = n - S - 1$,  and  we
may  consistently  with  the  condition  $\alpha > n - 2$,  continue  the  series  as  far  as  $\alpha = n - S - 1$,
except  in  the  case  $S = 0$,  when  by  doing  so  we  include  the  term

\newpage

corresponding  to  $\alpha = n - 1$,  which  in  virtue  of  the  condition  ought  to  be  excluded.  The  expression  for
the  term  in  question  is  $(-)^{n-1} [-1]^r [-1]^\beta$;  hence  if  the  sum  of  the  series  continued
to  the  proper  point  is  $\mathcal{S}$,  the  sum  continued  indefinitely  (in  the  particular  case  $S = 0$)
is  $\mathcal{S} + (-)^{n-1} [-1]^r [-1]^\beta$,  but  in  every  other  case  the  sum  continued  indefinitely  is
simply  $\mathcal{S}$.  And  by  a  well-known  theorem  in  finite  differences,  the  sum  continued
indefinitely  is  in  fact  zero.  That  is,  except  in  the  case  $S = 0$,  we  have  $\mathcal{S} = 0$,  but  in  the
excepted  case
\[
\mathcal{S} + (-)^{n-1} [-1]^r [-1]^\beta = 0;
\]
or  observing  that  $\alpha + \beta (= r - S)$  is  in  this  case  $= r$,  and  transforming  the  factorials,
we  have
\[
\mathcal{S} = -(-)^{n-1} (-)^{r} [n]^r [r-n]^{\underline{\beta}};
\]
or  substituting  for  $\alpha$  and  $\beta$  their  values,
\[
\mathcal{S} = (-)^{n-r-1} [n]^r [r-n]^{r-n}.
\]

\paragraph{31.}
Hence  the  general  term  of
\[
\frac{1}{[n-1]^{\underline{1}}} \Delta^{-1} D^{n-1} H \times \mathrm{III}
\]
vanishes  except  for  $S = 0$,  but  when  $S = 0$,  its  value  is
\[
R_s \{1, 2+s, (r+1-s)\} \times (-)^{n-r-1} [n]^r [r-n]^{r-n};
\]
or  observing  that  $R_s$  is  equal  to  $[r]^{\underline{s}} \div [s]^{\underline{s}} [r-s]^{\underline{s}}$,  the  value  is  simply
\[
(-)^{n-r-1} [n]^r \{1, 2+s, (r+1-s)\},
\]
that  is,  we  have
\[
\frac{1}{[n-1]^{\underline{1}}} \Delta^{-1} D^{n-1} H \times \mathrm{III}
= (-)^{n-r-1} [n]^r \sum_s \{1, 2+s, (r+1-s)\},
\]
the  summation   in  respect  to   $s$  extending  from   $s = 0$  to  $s = r$.      In  particular,   giving  to
$r$  the  values  $n-2$  and  $n-1$,  and  attending  to  the  expressions  for  $\mathrm{III}$  and  $\mathrm{IV}$,  we  find
\begin{align*}
\Delta^n (D^{n-2} H - \&c.) \cdot \mathrm{III} &= -[n-2]^{\underline{n-2}} \, \mathrm{III}, \\
\Delta^n (D^{n-1} H - \&c.) \cdot \mathrm{III} &= -[n-3]^{\underline{n-3}} \, \mathrm{IV}.
\end{align*}

\paragraph{32.}
The  equation  $\mathrm{III} = 0$  belongs  to  the  curve  which  by  its  intersections  with  the
tangent,  gives  the  tangentials  of  a  point  of  the  curve  $U = 0$.  Hence  the  equation  of
the  curve  in  question  is
\[
D^{n-2} H - \&c. = 0,
\]
which   is    Mr   Salmon's    theorem,   leading    to    the    solution    of    the    problem    of   double
tangents.

\paragraph{33.}
The  expressions  for  $\mathrm{I}$  and  $\mathrm{II}$  are  obtained  from  those  of  $\mathrm{IV}$  and  $\mathrm{III}$  by
interchanging  $(X, Y, Z)$  and  $(x, y, z)$,  and  reversing  the  sign.  Hence  if,  as  before,
$\mathrm{I}_1$, $\mathrm{II}_1$,  \&c.\@  denote  the  values  which  $\mathrm{I}$, $\mathrm{II}$,  \&c.\@  assume  by  this  interchange,  we  have
\begin{align*}
\Delta^n (\mathrm{I}_1 - \&c.) &= [n-2]^{\underline{n-2}} \, \mathrm{II}, \\
\Delta^n (\mathrm{II}_1 - \&c.) &= [n-3]^{\underline{n-3}} \, \mathrm{I},
\end{align*}
and  the  identical  equation
\[
a_0 \, \mathrm{I} + a_1 \, \mathrm{II} + a_{n-1} \, \mathrm{III} + a_n \, \mathrm{IV} = 0
\]

\newpage

becomes  therefore
\[
(D^{n-2} H - \&c.) \, \mathrm{T} = 0,
\]
which  is  the  general    identical   equation  referred  to   in   the  introduction  to  the  present
memoir.

\paragraph{34.}
It  is  to  be  noticed  that  for  $n = 3$,  the  equation  is
\[
(9 \Delta H - 2 D \mathrm{I}_1) U + (\mathrm{I}_1^2 - 2 \Delta^2 \mathrm{II}_1) \, DU - \Delta (DH - 3 D\mathrm{II}_1) \mathrm{T} - (\Delta H - 3 \mathrm{II}_1) \mathrm{T} = 0.
\]
But  we  have  $\mathrm{II}_1 = \mathrm{H}$,  and  in  like  manner  $\mathrm{I}_1 = \Delta$,  and  the  equation  thus  becomes
\[
\Delta \, DU + \mathrm{I}_1^2 \, DU - \Delta^2 D\mathrm{T} + \mathrm{H} \, \mathrm{T} = 0.
\]
And  so  also  for  $n = 4$,  the  equation  is
\begin{multline*}
(D^2 H - 3 D\mathrm{II}_1) \, U + (\mathrm{I}_1^2 - 3 \Delta^2 \mathrm{II}_1) \, DU \\
- \Delta (D^2 H - 3 D\mathrm{II}_1) \, \mathrm{T} - (DH - 3 \Delta H) \, \mathrm{T} = 0.
\end{multline*}
But  we  have  in  general  $D\mathrm{II}_1 = \frac{n-1}{2} DH$,  and  therefore  in  the  present  case  $D\mathrm{II}_1 = \frac{3}{2} DH$,
and   consequently   $D^2 \mathrm{II}_1 = \frac{3}{2} D^2 H$,  and  the  equation  thus  becomes
\[
D^2 H \cdot U + (\mathrm{I}_1^2 - 3 \Delta^2 \mathrm{II}_1) \, DU - \Delta (D^2 H - 3 \Delta^2 D^2 H) \, \mathrm{T} + DH \cdot \mathrm{T} = 0,
\]
which  agrees  with  the  results  previously  obtained  for  the  two  particular  cases.

\section*{261. On the Conic of Five-Pointic Contact at any Point of a Plane Curve.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{261. On the Conic of Five-Pointic Contact at any Point of a Plane Curve}
\label{sec:paper261}

\noindent
[From the \textit{Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London}, vol.~CXLIX.~(for  the
year  1859),  pp.~371--400.     Received  March  1,---Read  March  24,  1859.]

\medskip

\noindent\textsc{I.~Investigation of the Equation of the Conic of Five-pointic Contact.}

\medskip

The  tangent  is  a  line  passing  through  two  consecutive  points  of  a  plane  curve,
and  we  may  in  like  manner  consider  the  conic  which  passes  through  five  consecutive
points  of  a  plane  curve;  and  as  there  are  certain  singular  points,  viz.  the  points  of
inflexion  where  three  consecutive  points  of  the  curve  lie  in  a  line,  so  there  are
singular  points  where  six  consecutive  points  of  the  curve  lie  in  a  conic.  In  the
particular  case  where  the  given  curve  is  a  cubic,  the  last-mentioned  species  of  singular
points  have  been  considered  by  Pl\"ucker  and  Steiner,  and  in  the  same  particular  case,
the  theory  of  the  conic  of  five-pointic  contact  has  recently  been  established  by  Mr~Salmon.
But  the  general  case,  where  the  curve  is  of  any  order  whatever,  has  not  so  far  as
I  am  aware  been  hitherto  considered;---the  establishment  of  this  theory  is  the  object
of  the  present  memoir.

\paragraph{1.}
I  take  $(X, Y, Z)$  as  current  coordinates,  and  I  represent  the  equation  of  the
given  curve  by
\[
\mathrm{U} = (\\bullet\!\!\mid\, x, Y, Z)^n = 0.
\]
Let  $(x, y, z)$  be  the  coordinates  of  a  given  point  on  the  curve,  and  let  $U = (\\bullet\!\!\mid\, x, y, z)^n$
be  what  $\mathrm{U}$  becomes  when  $(x, y, z)$  are  written  in  the  place  of  $(X, Y, Z)$;  we  have
therefore  $U = 0$  as  a  condition  satisfied  by  the  coordinates  of  the  point  in  question.

\paragraph{2.}
Write  for  shortness
\begin{align*}
DU &= (X\partial_x + Y\partial_y + Z\partial_z) \, U, \\
D^2 U &= (X\partial_x + Y\partial_y + Z\partial_z)^2 \, U,
\end{align*}
and  let  $\nabla = aX + bY + cZ = 0$  be  the  equation  of  an  arbitrary  line;  then  if  in  the  equation
of  the  given  curve  we  write  $X + \lambda a$,  $Y + \lambda b$,  $Z + \lambda c$  in  the  place  of  $X$,  $Y$,  $Z$
respectively,  and  write  $\lambda = -\nabla / (a\partial_x + b\partial_y + c\partial_z)$,  the  result  is
\[
\mathrm{U} - \frac{\nabla}{a\partial_x + b\partial_y + c\partial_z} \, DU
  + \frac{\nabla^2}{(a\partial_x + b\partial_y + c\partial_z)^2} \, D^2 U - \&c. = 0,
\]
which  is  the  equation  of  the  given  curve  referred  to  the  line  $\nabla = 0$  as  a  line  of  coordinates.

\paragraph{3.}
The  equation  of  a  conic  passing  through  the  point  $(x, y, z)$  is
\[
(A, B, C, F, G, H \,\!\!\mid\, X, Y, Z)^2 = 0,
\]
where  the  coefficients  are  such  that
\[
(A, B, C, F, G, H \,\!\!\mid\, x, y, z)^2 = 0.
\]
The  condition  that  the  conic  shall  have  five-pointic  contact  with  the  given  curve  at  the
point  $(x, y, z)$  gives  rise  to  a  system  of  equations  which  determine  the  ratios  of  the
coefficients  $A : B : C : F : G : H$.


\paragraph{4.}
Consider  the  coordinates  of  a  point  of  the  curve  as  functions  of  a  single
variable  parameter;  then  for  the  present  purpose  the  coordinates  of  a  point  consecutive
to  $(x, y, z)$  may  be  taken  to  be
\begin{align*}
&x + dx + \tfrac{1}{2}d^2x + \tfrac{1}{6}d^3x + \tfrac{1}{24}d^4x, \\
&y + dy + \tfrac{1}{2}d^2y + \tfrac{1}{6}d^3y + \tfrac{1}{24}d^4y, \\
&z + dz + \tfrac{1}{2}d^2z + \tfrac{1}{6}d^3z + \tfrac{1}{24}d^4z,
\end{align*}
values  which,  substituted  for  $X$,  $Y$,  $Z$,  must  satisfy  the  equations
\[
\mathrm{U} = 0, \quad D^2 U - \mathrm{II} \cdot DU = 0.
\]

I  write  for  shortness
\begin{align*}
d_1 &= dx\, \partial_x + dy\, \partial_y + dz\, \partial_z, \\
d_2 &= d^2x\, \partial_x + d^2y\, \partial_y + d^2z\, \partial_z, \\
d_3 &= d^3x\, \partial_x + d^3y\, \partial_y + d^3z\, \partial_z, \\
d_4 &= d^4x\, \partial_x + d^4y\, \partial_y + d^4z\, \partial_z,
\end{align*}
then  the  consecutive  value  of  $U$  is
\[
e^{d_1 + \frac{1}{2}d_2 + \frac{1}{6}d_3 + \frac{1}{24}d_4} \, U,
\]
(Read  $\exp.\ z$,  exponential  of  $z$,  $= e^z$),  which  is
\begin{multline*}
\left(1 + d_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}d_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{6}d_1^3 + \tfrac{1}{24}d_1^4\right) U \\
+ \tfrac{1}{2}d_2 \left(1 + d_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}d_1^2\right) U \\
+ \tfrac{1}{6}d_3 (1 + d_1) U \\
+ \tfrac{1}{24}d_4 U,
\end{multline*}
or,  what  is  the  same  thing,
\begin{multline*}
U + d_1 U + \tfrac{1}{2}(d_1^2 + d_2) U + \tfrac{1}{6}(d_1^3 + 3d_1 d_2 + d_3) U \\
+ \tfrac{1}{24}(d_1^4 + 6d_1^2 d_2 + 4d_1 d_3 + 3d_2^2 + d_4) U,
\end{multline*}
the  several  terms  of  which  must  respectively  vanish,  and  we  have  therefore
\begin{align*}
U &= 0, \\
d_1 U &= 0, \\
d_1^2 U &= -(d_1^2 + 2d_2) U, \\
d_1^3 U &= -(d_1^3 + 3d_1 d_2 + d_3) U.
\end{align*}

\paragraph{5.}
Next,  preparing  to  substitute  in  the  equation
\[
D^2 U - \mathrm{II} \cdot DU = 0,
\]
the  consecutive  value  of  $DU$  is
\[
(x + dx + \tfrac{1}{2}d^2x + \tfrac{1}{6}d^3x + \tfrac{1}{24}d^4x) \, \partial_x U + \&c.,
\]
where
\[
(x + dx + \tfrac{1}{2}d^2x + \tfrac{1}{6}d^3x + \tfrac{1}{24}d^4x) \, \partial_x U + \&c.
\]
Reducing  by  the  above  results,  the  consecutive  value  of  $DU$  is
\begin{multline*}
= d_1 U + \tfrac{1}{2}(d_1^2 + d_2) U + \tfrac{1}{6}(d_1^3 + 3d_1 d_2 + d_3) U \\
+ \tfrac{1}{24}(d_1^4 + 6d_1^2 d_2 + 4d_1 d_3 + 3d_2^2 + d_4) U.
\end{multline*}

\newpage

Hence  also  writing
\begin{align*}
P &= ax + by + cz, \\
d_1 P &= a\, dx + b\, dy + c\, dz, \\
d_2 P &= a\, d^2x + b\, d^2y + c\, d^2z,
\end{align*}
the   consecutive   value   of  $-\mathrm{II} \cdot DU$  is   $-(P + d_1 P + \tfrac{1}{2}d_2 P)$  multiplied   into   the  consecutive
value  of  $DU$,  and  the  product  is
\begin{multline*}
= P \cdot d_1 U \\
+ P \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}(d_1^2 + d_2) U + d_1 P \cdot d_1 U \\
+ P \cdot \tfrac{1}{6}(d_1^3 + 3d_1 d_2 + d_3) U + d_1 P \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}(d_1^2 + d_2) U + \tfrac{1}{2}d_2 P \cdot d_1 U.
\end{multline*}

\paragraph{7.}
The  consecutive  value  of  $D^2 U$  is
\begin{multline*}
(x + dx + \tfrac{1}{2}d^2x + \tfrac{1}{6}d^3x + \tfrac{1}{24}d^4x)^2 \, \partial_x^2 U + \&c., \\
= d_1^2 U + d_1(d_1^2 + d_2) U + \&c.
\end{multline*}


\paragraph{8.}
Substituting   in  the  equation  $D^2 U - \mathrm{II} \cdot DU = 0$,  we   obtain   as   the   conditions  of
a  five-pointic  contact
\begin{align*}
&-[(m-1)a_1^2 + 3(m-2)a_1 a_2] \, U + P \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}(d_1^2 + 3d_1 d_2) \, U + d_1 P \cdot d_1^2 U = 0, \\
&-[(m-1)(a_1^3 + 6a_1^2 a_2) + (m-2)(4a_1 a_3 + 3a_2^2)] \, U \\
&\quad + P \cdot \tfrac{1}{6}(d_1^3 + 6d_1^2 d_2 + 4d_1 d_3 + 3d_2^2) \, U + d_1 P \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}(d_1^2 + 3d_1 d_2) \, U + \tfrac{1}{2}d_2 P \cdot d_1^2 U = 0;
\end{align*}
or  reducing
\[
P = 2(m-2) \frac{d_1^2 U}{d_1^2 U},
\]
\begin{align*}
d_1 P &= \frac{[(m-1)a_1^3 + 6a_1^2 a_2] \, U}{d_1^2 U} - \frac{P \cdot [a_1^3 + 3a_1^2 a_2] \, U}{d_1^2 U}, \\
d_2 P &= \frac{[(m-1)(a_1^3 + 6a_1^2 a_2) + (m-2)(4a_1 a_3 + 3a_2^2)] \, U}{d_1^2 U} \\
&\quad - \frac{d_1 P \cdot [a_1^3 + 3a_1^2 a_2] \, U}{d_1^2 U} - \frac{P \cdot [a_1^3 + 6a_1^2 a_2 + 4a_1 a_3 + 3a_2^2] \, U}{d_1^2 U},
\end{align*}
which  are  the  conditions  of  a  five-pointic  contact:  it  is  to  be  remarked  that  if  only
the  first  and  second  conditions  are  satisfied,  we  have  a  four-pointic  contact,  and  if  only
the  first  condition  is  satisfied,  a  three-pointic  contact.

\paragraph{9.}
We   have  to   reduce   the   last-mentioned    equations ;    suppose   that   $A$,   $B$,  $C$  are
the  first  derived  functions  of  $U$,  then  the  equation  $d_1 U = 0$  may  be  written
\[
A\, dx + B\, dy + C\, dz = 0,
\]
and  this  will  be  satisfied  identically  if
\begin{align*}
dx &= B\nu - C\mu, \\
dy &= C\lambda - A\nu, \\
dz &= A\mu - B\lambda,
\end{align*}
where  $\lambda$,  $\mu$,  $\nu$  are  arbitrary  multipliers,  which  may  be   taken   to   be   constants.     We  have
therefore  $d_1 = \mathfrak{D}$,  where
\[
\mathfrak{D} = (B\nu - C\mu) \, \partial_x + (C\lambda - A\nu) \, \partial_y + (A\mu - B\lambda) \, \partial_z.
\]

\paragraph{10.}
The  resulting  expressions   for  $d_1^2 U$,  $d_1^3 U$,  $d_1^4 U$  may  be  exhibited  in  the  reduced
forms  given  by  Hesse,  viz.  if  $\varpi = \lambda x + \mu y + \nu z$,  we  have
\[
d_1^2 U = P_2 U - Q_2 \varpi^{m-2}, \quad d_1^3 U = P_3 U - Q_3 \varpi^{m-3}, \quad d_1^4 U = P_4 U - Q_4 \varpi^{m-4},
\]
where   the   values   of  $P_2$,  $P_3$,  $P_4$;   $Q_2$,  $Q_3$,  $Q_4$   are   as  follows,   viz.   if  $(a, b, c, f, g, h)$  are
the  second  derived  functions  of  $U$,  and  if
\[
H = \begin{vmatrix} a & h & g \\ h & b & f \\ g & f & c \end{vmatrix}
\]
be  the  Hessian ;   if,  moreover,
\[
\Phi = -\begin{vmatrix} 0 & \lambda & \mu & \nu \\ \lambda & a & h & g \\ \mu & h & b & f \\ \nu & g & f & c \end{vmatrix}
\]
be  the  bordered  Hessian  (we  may  also  write  $\Phi = (\mathfrak{A}, \mathfrak{B}, \mathfrak{C}, \mathfrak{F}, \mathfrak{G}, \mathfrak{H} \,\!\!\mid\, \lambda, \mu, \nu)^2$,  where
$(\mathfrak{A}, \mathfrak{B}, \mathfrak{C}, \mathfrak{F}, \mathfrak{G}, \mathfrak{H})$  are  the  inverse  coefficients  of  $(a, b, c, f, g, h)$,  viz.  $\mathfrak{A} = bc - f^2$,  \&c.);  
and  finally,  if  for  shortness  we  write
\[
\Omega = \partial_\lambda \Phi \cdot \partial_\mu \Phi + \partial_\mu \Phi \cdot \partial_\nu \Phi + \partial_\nu \Phi \cdot \partial_\lambda \Phi,
\]
then  we  have
\begin{align*}
P_2 &= \frac{m-1}{m} \mathfrak{D} \Phi, \\
P_3 &= \frac{(m-1)^2}{m^2} \mathfrak{D}^2 \Phi - \frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{m^2} \Omega, \\
P_4 &= \frac{(m-1)^3}{m^3} \mathfrak{D}^3 \Phi - \frac{3(m-1)^2(m-2)}{m^3} \mathfrak{D} \Omega + \frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{m^3} \Psi,
\end{align*}
where  $\Psi$  is  a  certain  function  of  the  third  derivatives  of  $U$.

\newpage

In  the  present  case  $\varpi = 0$,  and  we  have
\[
d_1^2 U = P_2 U, \quad d_1^3 U = P_3 U, \quad d_1^4 U = P_4 U.
\]

\paragraph{11.}
Hence,  substituting   for   $Q_2$  and  $Q_3$  their  values,   the  first  and    second   of   the
equations  for  the  five-pointic  contact  give
\[
P = 2(m-2) \frac{P_2 U}{d_1^2 U},
\]
and  observing  that  $\mathrm{II}$  is  a  linear  function  of  $(X, Y, Z)$,  and  consequently  that  $P$,  $d_1 P$
denote  simply  the  values  which  $\mathrm{II}$  assumes  when  $(x, y, z)$,  $(dx, dy, dz)$  are  respectively
substituted  for  $(X, Y, Z)$,  we  see  at  once  that  these  two  conditions  will  be  satisfied  if
we  put
\[
\mathrm{II} = \Lambda \cdot \Phi,
\]
where  $\Lambda$  is  an  arbitrary  constant,  or,  what  is  the  same  thing,  an  arbitrary  function  of
$(x, y, z)$.     We  have  thus  the  general  equation  of  a  conic  of  four-pointic  contact.

\paragraph{12.}
The  above  value  of  $\mathrm{II}$  gives
\[
P = \Lambda \cdot \Phi(x, y, z), \quad d_1 P = \Lambda \cdot \mathfrak{D} \Phi, \quad d_2 P = \Lambda \cdot d_2 \Phi,
\]
and  the  third  equation  of  the  system  of  conditions  for  a  five-pointic  contact  is  therefore
\[
-[(m-1)(a_1^3 + 6a_1^2 a_2) + (m-2)(4a_1 a_3 + 3a_2^2)] \, U + \Lambda \cdot [\text{expr.}] = 0,
\]
which  leads  to  the  value  of  $\Lambda$.


\paragraph{15.}
We  have  $R_2 = H$,  and  it  will  be  shown  that
\begin{align*}
(m-1)^2 R_3 &= -3(m-2)^2 H \cdot \Phi + 4(m-2) \Omega, \\
(m-1)^2 R_4 &= -12(m-2)^2 \Omega \cdot \Phi + 4(m-2) \Psi,
\end{align*}
where  for  shortness
\[
\Omega = (\mathfrak{A}, \mathfrak{B}, \mathfrak{C}, \mathfrak{F}, \mathfrak{G}, \mathfrak{H} \,\!\!\mid\, \partial_\lambda, \partial_\mu, \partial_\nu)^2 H,
\]
and  hence,  writing
\[
\Lambda = \frac{9H^2 \cdot A}{(m-1)^2},
\]
we  have
\begin{align*}
9H^2 A &= 3R_3 \cdot (m-1)^2 = R_4 - 4(m-1)^2 R_2^2, \\
9H^2 A &= -3\Omega \cdot \Phi + 4\Psi;
\end{align*}
or  replacing  $H$,  $\Phi$  by  their  values,
\[
9H^2 A = 3\Omega \cdot \Phi - 4(\mathfrak{A}, \mathfrak{B}, \mathfrak{C}, \mathfrak{F}, \mathfrak{G}, \mathfrak{H} \,\!\!\mid\, \partial_\lambda H, \partial_\mu H, \partial_\nu H)^2,
\]
and  $\Lambda$  having  this  value,  the  equation  of  the  five-pointic  conic  is
\[
D^2 U - \frac{1}{2(m-2)} \left(DH + \Lambda \cdot DU\right) DU = 0,
\]
where  it  will  be  recollected  that  the  current  coordinates  are  $(X, Y, Z)$,  and  that  $D$
denotes  $X\partial_x + Y\partial_y + Z\partial_z$.

\paragraph{16.}
I  remark,  in  passing,  that  the  problem  of  finding  the  circle  of  curvature  at
a  given  point  of  a  plane  curve,  is  in  fact  that  of  determining  the  conic  having  with
the  curve  at  the  given  point  a  three-pointic  contact,  and  besides  passing  through  two
given  points.     The  equation  of  a  conic  having  an  ordinary  contact,  is
\[
D^2 U - \mathrm{II} \cdot DU = 0,
\]
where
\[
\mathrm{II} = aX + bY + cZ,
\]
and  the  condition  of  a  three-pointic  contact  is
\[
ax + by + cz = 2(m-2).
\]
Let  the  coordinates  of  the  two  given  points  be
\[
(x_1, y_1, z_1), \quad (x_2, y_2, z_2),
\]
and  let  $(D^2 U)_1$,  \&c.\@  be  the  corresponding  values  of  $D^2 U$,  \&c.,  then  we  have
\begin{align*}
aX + bY + cZ &= \mathrm{II}, \\
ax + by + cz &= 2(m-2), \\
ax_1 + by_1 + cz_1 &= \frac{(D^2 U)_1}{(DU)_1}, \\
ax_2 + by_2 + cz_2 &= \frac{(D^2 U)_2}{(DU)_2},
\end{align*}
and  if  from  the  four  equations  we  eliminate  $a$,  $b$,  $c$,  we  find
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
XDU & YDU & ZDU & D^2 U \\
x & y & z & 2(m-2) \\
x_1 & y_1 & z_1 & (D^2 U)_1/(DU)_1 \\
x_2 & y_2 & z_2 & (D^2 U)_2/(DU)_2 \\
\end{vmatrix} = 0
\]
for  the   equation   of   the  conic  in   question;   $(x, y, z)$   being   the   coordinates   of  the   point
of  contact,  and  $(X, Y, Z)$  current  coordinates.

\noindent\textsc{II.~Demonstration of Identities assumed in the preceding Section.}

\medskip

\noindent\textit{Proof of the expressions for $(d_1^3 + 3d_1 d_2)U$ and $(d_1^4 + 6d_1^2 d_2 + 4d_1 d_3 + 3d_2^2)U$:}

\paragraph{17.}
It  will  be  remembered  that  $d_1$,  $d_2$  stand  originally  for
\[
dx\, \partial_x + dy\, \partial_y + dz\, \partial_z,
\]
\[
d^2x\, \partial_x + d^2y\, \partial_y + d^2z\, \partial_z,
\]
and  that  $A$,  $B$,  $C$  being  the  first  derived  functions  of  $U$,  $dx$,  $dy$,  $dz$  are  changed  into
\[
B\nu - C\mu, \quad C\lambda - A\nu, \quad A\mu - B\lambda,
\]
and  that  the  resulting  value  of  $d_1$,  viz.
\[
(B\nu - C\mu) \partial_x + (C\lambda - A\nu) \partial_y + (A\mu - B\lambda) \partial_z
\]
is  also  represented  by  $\mathfrak{D}$,  so  that  $d_1 = \mathfrak{D}$.     The  corresponding  values  of  $d^2x$,  $d^2y$,  $d^2z$  are
\begin{align*}
d^2x &= \nu\, dB - \mu\, dC, \\
d^2y &= \lambda\, dC - \nu\, dA, \\
d^2z &= \mu\, dA - \lambda\, dB,
\end{align*}
where  we  have
\[
dA = a\, dx + h\, dy + g\, dz, \quad \&c.,
\]
in  which  $dx$,  $dy$,  $dz$  are  to  be  replaced  by  the  values
\[
B\nu - C\mu, \quad C\lambda - A\nu, \quad A\mu - B\lambda;
\]
and  $d_2$  really  denotes  what
\[
d^2x\, \partial_x + d^2y\, \partial_y + d^2z\, \partial_z
\]
becomes  when  the  above  values  are  substituted  for  $d^2x$,  $d^2y$,  $d^2z$.  But  in  the  expressions
$d_1^2 U$,  $d_1 d_2 U$  \&c.,  the  symbols  $\partial_x$,  $\partial_y$,  $\partial_z$  contained  in  $d_1$  and  $d_2$  operate  only  on  $U$,  and
not  on  the  variable  quantities  $A$,  $B$,  $C$,  \&c.\@  contained  in  $d_1$  and  $d_2$.


\paragraph{18.}
If  now  we  treat  $d_2$  as  an  operand,  that  is,  perform  the  differentiations  on  the
variable  quantities  $A$,  $B$,  $C$  which  enter  into  $d_2$,  we  obtain
\[
d_2(d_1 U) = d_2(d_1 U) + (d_2 d_1)U,
\]
or,  what  is  the  same  thing,  operating  on  $d_1 U$  with  $d_2$,  the  result  is
\[
d_1 d_2 U + (d_2 d_1)U,
\]
and  in  like  manner
\[
d_1^2 U = d_1^2 U + 2(d_2 d_1)U + (d_3 d_1)U.
\]
It  is,  in  fact,  upon  these  principles  that  Hesse's  values  of  $d_1^2 U$,  $d_1^3 U$,  \&c.\@  were  obtained,
and  we  may  by  means  of  them  obtain  the  other  expressions  assumed  in  the  preceding
section.

\paragraph{19.}
In  fact,  starting  from  Hesse's  equation,
\[
d_1^2 U = P_2 U - Q_2 \varpi^{m-2},
\]
we   have
\[
\frac{1}{m-1}(d_1^3 + 3d_1 d_2)U = -\frac{1}{(m-1)^2}(U \mathfrak{D} \Phi + \Phi \mathfrak{D} U) - \frac{1}{(m-1)^2}(DH \cdot \Phi + H \cdot \mathfrak{D} \Phi).
\]
But  we  have  identically  $\mathfrak{D} U = 0$,  $\mathfrak{D}^2 U = 0$,  and  this  equation  becomes  therefore
\[
\frac{1}{m-1}(d_1^3 + 3d_1 d_2)U = -\frac{1}{(m-1)^2}(U \mathfrak{D} \Phi + \Phi \cdot 0) - \frac{1}{(m-1)^2}(DH \cdot \Phi + H \cdot \mathfrak{D} \Phi);
\]
this  is  precisely  Hesse's  value  of  $d_1^3 U$,  and  thus  we  have  $\mathfrak{D} \Delta U = 0$,  and  therefore
\[
\frac{1}{m-1}(d_1^3 + 3d_1 d_2)U = -\frac{1}{(m-1)^2} H \cdot \mathfrak{D} \Phi.
\]

\paragraph{20.}
In  like  manner,  starting  from  the  expression  of  $d_1^3 U$,  we  have
\[
\frac{1}{m-1}(d_1^4 + 6d_1^2 d_2 + 4d_1 d_3 + 3d_2^2)U = \text{[expression involving  } \mathfrak{D}^2 \Phi, \Omega, \Psi\text{]},
\]
or    since    $DU$    and    $D^2 U$    vanish    identically,    and    the    values    of    $D \cdot D\Phi$,    $D \cdot DH$    are
$d_1^3 + D^2 \Phi$,  $d_1 \cdot H + D^2 H$,  we  have
\[
\text{[complicated  expression]}
\]
and  if  from  the  double  of  this  equation  we  subtract  Hesse's  equation,
\[
\frac{1}{m-1} d_1^4 U = P_4 U - Q_4 \varpi^{m-4},
\]
we  find  the  required  relation,
\[
\frac{1}{m-1}(d_1^4 + 6d_1^2 d_2 + 4d_1 d_3 + 3d_2^2)U = -\frac{3}{(m-1)^2} \Omega \cdot \Phi + \frac{4}{(m-1)^2} \Psi.
\]

\newpage

\noindent\textit{Proof of the expression for $\mathfrak{D}^2 H$:}

\paragraph{21.}
We   have
\[
\mathfrak{D}^2 H = \partial_x H \cdot (\nu\, dB - \mu\, dC) + \partial_y H \cdot (\lambda\, dC - \nu\, dA) + \partial_z H \cdot (\mu\, dA - \lambda\, dB),
\]
where
\begin{align*}
dA &= a\, dx + h\, dy + g\, dz, \\
dB &= h\, dx + b\, dy + f\, dz, \\
dC &= g\, dx + f\, dy + c\, dz,
\end{align*}
in  which  $dx$,  $dy$,  $dz$  are  to  be  replaced  by  their  values:   we  have  therefore
\begin{multline*}
\mathfrak{D}^2 H = \partial_x H \cdot \{\nu[(bC-fB)\lambda + (fA-hC)\mu + (hB-bA)\nu] \\
- \mu[(fC-cB)\lambda + (cA-gC)\mu + (gB-fA)\nu]\} + \&c.,
\end{multline*}
where  the  coefficient  of  $\partial_x H$  is
\begin{multline*}
\mathfrak{A}\lambda^2 + (gC-cA)\mu^2 + (hB-bA)\nu^2 - (2fA-gB-hC)\mu\nu \\
+ (bC-fB)\nu\lambda + (cB-fC)\lambda\mu;
\end{multline*}
or,  since  we  have
\begin{align*}

\section*{262. On the Equation of Differences for an Equation of any Order, and in particular for the Equations of the Orders Two, Three, Four, and Five.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{262. On the Equation of Differences for an Equation of any Order}
\label{sec:paper262}

\noindent
[From   the   \textit{Philosophical  Transactions}  of  the  Royal  Society   of  London,  vol.~CL.~(for  the
year  1860),  pp.~93--112.     Received  March  2,---Read  March  29,  1860.]

\medskip

The  term,  equation  of  differences,  denotes  the  equation  for  the  squared  differences
of  the  roots  of  a  given  equation ;  the  equation  of  differences  afforded  a  means  of
determining  the  number  of  real  roots,  and  also  limits  for  the  real  roots,  of  a  given
numerical  equation,  and  was  upon  this  account  long  ago  sought  for  by  geometers.
In  the  Philosophical  Transactions  for  1763,  Waring  gives,  but  without  demonstration
or  indication  of  the  mode  of  obtaining  it,  the  equation  of  differences  for  an  equation
of  the  fifth  order  wanting  the  second  term :  the  result  was  probably  obtained  by  the
method  of  symmetric  functions.  This  method  is  employed  in  the  \textit{Meditationes  Alge-}
\textit{braicae}  (1782),  where  the  equation  of  differences  is  given  for  the  equations  of  the
third  and  fourth  orders  wanting  the  second  terms;  and  in  p.~85  the  before-mentioned
result  for  the  equation  of  the  fifth  order  wanting  the  second  term,  is  reproduced.
The  formul\ae\  for  obtaining  by  this  method  the  equation  of  differences,  are  fully
developed  by  Lagrange  in  the  \textit{Trait\'e  des  \'Equations  Num\'eriques}  (1808);  and  he  finds
by  means  of  them  the  equation  of  differences  for  the  equations  of  the  orders  two  and
three,  and  for  the  equation  of  the  fourth  order  wanting  the  second  term ;  and  in
Note~III.\  he  gives,  after  Waring,  the  result  for  the  equation  of  the  fifth  order  wanting
the  second  term.  It  occurred  to  me  that  the  equation  of  differences  could  be  most
easily  calculated  by  the  following  method.  The  coefficients  of  the  equation  of  differences,
qua    functions    of    the    differences    of    the    roots    of    the    given  equation,   are    leading
coefficients  of  covariants,  or  (to  use  a  shorter  expression)  they  are  ``Seminvariants,''
that  is,  each  of  them  is  a  function  of  the  coefficients  which  is  reduced  to  zero  by  one
of  the  two  operators  which  reduce  an  invariant  to  zero.  In  virtue  of  this  property
they  can  be  calculated,  when  their  values  are  known  for  the  particular  case  in  which
one  of  the  coefficients  of  the  given  equation  is  zero.

To  fix  the  ideas,  let  the  given  equation  be  $(a, b, \ldots \mid\, v,  1)^n = 0$;  then,  when  the
last  coefficient  or  constant  term  vanishes,  the  equation  breaks  up  into  $v = 0$  and  into
an  equation  of  the  degree  $n-1$,  which  I  call  the  \textit{reduced  equation};  the  equation  of
differences  will  break  up  into  two  equations,  one  of  which  is  the  equation  of  differences
for  the  reduced  equation,  the  other  is  the  equation  for  the  squares  of  the  roots  of  the
same  reduced  equation.


The  equations  successively  considered  are
\begin{align*}
(a, b, c \,\!\!\mid\, v, 1)^2 &= 0, \\
(a, b, c, d \,\!\!\mid\, v, 1)^3 &= 0, \\
(a, b, c, d, e \,\!\!\mid\, v, 1)^4 &= 0, \\
(a, b, c, d, e, f \,\!\!\mid\, v, 1)^5 &= 0.
\end{align*}
The  equation  of  differences  for  the  quadric,  and  that  for  the  squares  of  the  roots,
are  considered  to  be  known,  and  the  other  results  are  derived  from  them.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Quadric Equation.}

\noindent
Equation  of  differences  is,  $0 =$
\[
\begin{array}{r|rrr}
a^2 x & +1 & ac + 4 & b^2 - 1 \\
\hline
& a^2 x & +1 & ac + 2 \\
& & b^2 - 1 & c^2 + 1 \\
\end{array}
\]

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Cubic Equation.}

\noindent
Equation  of  differences  is,  $0 =$
\[
\begin{array}{r|rrrrr}
a^3 x & +1 & ac + 6 & a^2 c^2 + 9 & a^3 c^3 + 27 & \\
& & b^2 - 2 & ab^2 c - 6 & b^4 + 1 & \\
& & & ac^3 + 4 & a^2 bc d - 18 & \\
& & & & b^2 c^2 - 1 & \\
\end{array}
\]

\noindent
Equation  for  the  squares  of  the  roots  is,  $0 =$
\[
\begin{array}{r|rrrr}
a^2 x & +1 & ac + 2 & b^2 - 1 & \\
& & bd - 2 & c^2 + 1 & \\
& & & d^2 + 1 & \\
\end{array}
\]

\newpage
\noindent\textbf{Quartic Equation.}

\noindent
Equation  of  differences  is,  $0 =$
\[
\begin{array}{r|rrrrrr}
a^4 x & +1 & ac + 8 & a^2 c^2 + 8 & a^3 c e + 16 & a^4 e^2 - 112 & \\
& & b^2 - 3 & a^2 b d - 2 & a^3 c d^2 + 26 & a^3 b c d e + 56 & \\
& & & a^2 c e + 22 & a^2 b^2 c e - 6 & a^3 b d e^2 + 216 & \\
& & & b^4 + 3 & a^2 b^2 d + 8 & a^3 c d^3 - 192 & \\
& & & & a b^2 c^2 + 8 & a^3 d^4 - 27 & \\
& & & & a b^2 c d + 38 & a^2 b c^2 d e + 120 & \\
& & & & a c^4 + 4 & a^2 c d^2 e - 54 & \\
& & & & b^3 d - 6 & a b^3 c e + 32 & \\
& & & & b^2 c^2 + 2 & a b^2 c d^2 - 25 & \\
& & & & & a b^2 d^3 + 18 & \\
& & & & & a b c^3 d + 42 & \\
& & & & & a c^2 e + 16 & \\
& & & & & b^4 e + 6 & \\
& & & & & b^3 c d - 12 & \\
& & & & & b^2 c d^2 - 9 & \\
& & & & & b^2 c^3 e + 6 & \\
& & & & & b c^2 d^2 + 1 & \\
\end{array}
\]

\medskip
\noindent
Equation  for  the  squares  of  the  roots  is,  $0 =$
\[
\begin{array}{r|rrrrr}
a^2 x & +1 & ac + 2 & b^2 - 1 & & \\
& & ae + 2 & bd - 2 & c^2 + 1 & \\
& & & ce + 2 & d^2 - 1 & \\
& & & & e^2 + 1 & \\
\end{array}
\]

\medskip

The  multiplication  of  the  equation  of  differences  and  the  equation  of  the  squares
of  the  roots  of  the  quadric  equation,  gives  the  equation,  $0 =$
\[
\begin{array}{r|rrrr}
a^4 x & +1 & ac + 6 & a^2 c^2 + 9 & a^3 b^2 c - 6 \\
& & b^2 - 2 & a b^2 c - 6 & b^4 + 1 \\
& & & a c^3 + 4 & b^2 c^2 - 1 \\
\end{array}
\]
where  all  the  coefficients  except  the  last  are  reduced  to  zero  by  the  operator
\[
3a\partial_b + 2b\partial_c + c\partial_d,
\]
and  they  are  consequently  (without  any  alteration)  coefficients  of  the  equation  of  differences  of  the  cubic  equation.


\newpage
\noindent\textbf{Quintic Equation.}

The  multiplication  of  the  equation  of  differences,  and  the  equation  for  the  squares
of  the  roots  of  the  quartic  equation,  gives,  in  like  manner,  the  equation  of  differences
for  the  quintic  equation,  except  as  to  the  terms  involving  $f$;  and  these  are  obtained
as  above,  viz.  each  coefficient  is  of  the  form  $L_0 + L_1 f + L_2 f^2 + \&c.$,  where  $L_0$  is  known;
such  coefficient  is  reduced  to  zero  by  the  operator
\[
5a\partial_b + 4b\partial_c + 3c\partial_d + 2d\partial_e + e\partial_f,
\]
or  putting  for  shortness  $V = 5a\partial_b + 4b\partial_c + 3c\partial_d + 2d\partial_e$,  the  operator  $V + e\partial_f$.  We  have
therefore
\[
L_1 = -\tfrac{1}{e} VL_0, \quad L_2 = -\tfrac{1}{2e} VL_1, \quad L_3 = -\tfrac{1}{3e} VL_2, \quad \&c.
\]
which  give  $L_1$,  $L_2$,  \&c.\  by  means  of  $L_0$.

For  the  calculation  of  $VL$  (where  $L$  is  any  one  of  the  coefficients  $L_0$,  $L_1$,  \&c.),
it  is  proper  to  separate  the  terms  involving  the  different  powers  of  $e$,  and  write
$L = M_0 + M_1 e + M_2 e^2 + \&c.$,  $V = V' + 2d\partial_e$,  where  $V' = 5a\partial_b + 4b\partial_c + 3c\partial_d$.  We  have  then
\begin{align*}
VL &= V'M_0 + e(V'M_1 + 2d\partial_e M_0) \\
   &\quad + e^2(V'M_2 + 2d\partial_e M_1) + \&c.
\end{align*}

\medskip

[The  memoir  continues  with  detailed  calculations  of  each  coefficient  for  the  quintic
equation,  following  the  method  explained  above.  The  calculations  involve  extensive
tabular  arrangements  showing  the  terms  of  each  coefficient  $L_0$,  $L_1$,  $L_2$,  \&c.,  and
the  application  of  the  operator  $V$  to  obtain  the  successive  coefficients.  The  complete
equation  of  differences  for  the  quintic  equation  is  thus  obtained.]

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{General  Equation  of  Differences  by  Elimination.}

At  the  conclusion  of  the  memoir,  I  show  how  the  equation  of  differences  for  an
equation  of  the  order  $n$  can  be  obtained  by  the  elimination  of  a  single  quantity  from
two  equations  each  of  the  order  $n-1$;  and  by  applying  to  these  two  equations  the
simplification  which  I  have  made  in  Bezout's  abridged  method  of  elimination,  I  exhibit
the  equation  of  differences  for  the  given  equation  of  the  order  $n$,  in  a  compendious
form  by  means  of  a  determinant.
