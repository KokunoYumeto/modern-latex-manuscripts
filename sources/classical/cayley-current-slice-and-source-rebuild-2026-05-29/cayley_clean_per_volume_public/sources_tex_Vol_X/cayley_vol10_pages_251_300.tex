\section*{671. On a Sibi-Reciprocal Surface.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{671. On a Sibi-Reciprocal Surface}

[From the Berlin.\ Akad.\ Monatsber., (1878), pp.\ 309---313.]

The question of the generation of a sibi-reciprocal surface---that is, a surface the reciprocal of which is of the same order and has the same singularities as the original surface---was considered by me in the year 1868, see \textit{Proc.\ London Math.\ Soc.}\ t.\ ii.\ pp.\ 61---63, [part of 387], where it is remarked that if a surface be considered as the envelope of a quadric surface varying according to given conditions, then the reciprocal surface is given as the envelope of a quadric surface varying according to the reciprocal conditions; whence, if the conditions be sibi-reciprocal, it follows that the surface is a sibi-reciprocal surface. And I gave as instances the surface which is the envelope of a quadric surface touching each of 8 given lines; and also the surface called the ``tetrahedroid,'' which is a homographic transformation of Fresnel's Wave Surface and a particular case of the quartic surface with 16 nodes.

The interesting surface of the order 8, recently considered by Herr Kummer, Berl.\ Monatsber., Jan.\ 1878, pp.\ 25---36, is included under the theory. In fact, if we consider a line $L$, whereof the six coordinates $a$, $b$, $c$, $f$, $g$, $h$ satisfy each of the three linear relations
\begin{align*}
l_1 a + g_1 b + k_1 c + a_1 f + b_1 g + c_1 h &= 0,\\
l_2 a + g_2 b + k_2 c + a_2 f + b_2 g + c_2 h &= 0,\\
l_3 a + g_3 b + k_3 c + a_3 f + b_3 g + c_3 h &= 0,
\end{align*}
the locus of this line is a quadric surface the equation of which is
\begin{multline*}
T = (agh)\, x^2 + (bhf)\, y^2 + (cfg)\, z^2 + (abc)\, w^2 \\
+ [(abg) - (cah)]\, xw + [(bfg) + (chf)]\, yz \\
+ [(bch) - (abf)]\, yw + [(cgh) + (afg)]\, zx \\
+ [(caf) - (bcg)]\, zw + [(ahf) + (bgh)]\, xy = 0.
\end{multline*}

where $(agh)$ is used to denote the determinant $\begin{vmatrix} a_1 & g_1 & h_1 \\ a_2 & g_2 & h_2 \\ a_3 & g_3 & h_3 \end{vmatrix}$, and so for the other symbols.

Considering the reciprocal of the line $L$ in regard to the quadric surface $X^2 + Y^2 + Z^2 + W^2 = 0$, the six coordinates of the reciprocal line are $f$, $g$, $h$, $a$, $b$, $c$, and it is hence at once seen that the locus of the reciprocal line is the quadric surface obtained from the equation $T=0$ by interchanging therein the symbolical quantities $a$, $b$, $c$ and $f$, $g$, $h$: viz. writing also $(\xi, \eta, \zeta, \omega)$ in place of $(x, y, z, w)$, the new equation is
\begin{multline*}
T' = (fbc)\, \xi^2 + (gca)\, \eta^2 + (hab)\, \zeta^2 + (fgh)\, \omega^2 \\
+ [(fgh) - (abc)]\, \xi\omega + [(fab) + (hbc)]\, \eta\zeta \\
+ [(ghc) - (fga)]\, \eta\omega + [(gbc) + (fab)]\, \zeta\xi \\
+ [(fca) - (ghb)]\, \zeta\omega + [(hca) + (gbc)]\, \xi\eta = 0;
\end{multline*}
or, what is the same thing, this equation $T' = 0$ is the equation of the original quadric surface (the locus of $L$) expressed in terms of the plane-coordinates $\xi$, $\eta$, $\zeta$, $\omega$.

Now considering each of the quantities $a_1$, $b_1$, $c_1$, $f_1$, $g_1$, $h_1$, $a_2$, $b_2$, etc., $a_3$, $b_3$, etc., as a given linear function of a variable parameter $\lambda$, say
\[
a_1 = a_1' + a_1''\lambda, \quad b_1 = b_1' + b_1''\lambda, \quad \text{etc.},
\]
the equation $T=0$ takes the form
\[
A\lambda^3 + 3B\lambda^2 + 3C\lambda + D = 0,
\]
where $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ are given quadric functions of the coordinates $x$, $y$, $z$, $w$; and the envelope of the quadric surface $T=0$ is Herr Kummer's surface of the eighth order
\[
(AD - BC)^2 - 4(AC - B^2)(BD - C^2) = 0.
\]

In like manner the equation $T' = 0$ takes the form
\[
A'\lambda^3 + 3B'\lambda^2 + 3C'\lambda + D' = 0,
\]
where $A'$, $B'$, $C'$, $D'$ are given functions of the coordinates $\xi$, $\eta$, $\zeta$, $\omega$; and we have
\[
(A'D' - B'C')^2 - 4(A'C' - B'^2)(B'D' - C'^2) = 0,
\]
as the equation of the reciprocal surface; or (what is the same thing) as that of the original surface, regarding $\xi$, $\eta$, $\zeta$, $\omega$ as plane-coordinates.

In regard to the foregoing equation $T = 0$, it is to be noticed that, if $a_1$, $b_1$, $c_1$, $f_1$, $g_1$, $h_1$, $a_2$, $b_2$, etc., $a_3$, $b_3$, etc., instead of being arbitrary coefficients, were the coordinates of three given lines $L_1$, $L_2$, $L_3$ respectively; that is, if we had
\[
a_1 f_1 + b_1 g_1 + c_1 h_1 = 0, \quad a_2 f_2 + b_2 g_2 + c_2 h_2 = 0, \quad a_3 f_3 + b_3 g_3 + c_3 h_3 = 0,
\]
then the three linear relations satisfied by $(a, b, c, f, g, h)$ would express that the line $L$ was a line meeting each of the three given lines $L_1$, $L_2$, $L_3$: the locus is therefore the quadric surface which passes through these three lines; and I have in my paper ``On the six coordinates of a Line,'' \textit{Camb.\ Phil.\ Trans.}, t.\ xi.\ (1869), pp.\ 290---323, [435], found the equation to be the foregoing equation $T=0$.

But it is easy to see that the same equation subsists in the case where the three equations $a_1 f_1 + b_1 g_1 + c_1 h_1 = 0$, etc., are not satisfied. For the several coefficients being perfectly general, any one of the three linear relations may be replaced by a linear combination of these equations that is, in place of $a_1$, $b_1$, $c_1$, $f_1$, $g_1$, $h_1$, we may write $a'$, $b'$, $c'$, $f'$, $g'$, $h'$, where
\[
a' = \theta_1 a_1 + \theta_2 a_2 + \theta_3 a_3, \quad b' = \theta_1 b_1 + \theta_2 b_2 + \theta_3 b_3, \quad \text{etc.};
\]
and these factors $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$, $\theta_3$ may be conceived to be such that the condition in question $a' f' + b' g' + c' h' = 0$ is satisfied. Similarly the second set of coefficients may be replaced by $a'$, $b'$, $c'$, $f'$, $g'$, $h'$, where
\[
a' = \phi_1 a_1 + \phi_2 a_2 + \phi_3 a_3, \quad \text{etc.},
\]
and the condition $a' f' + b' g' + c' h' = 0$ is satisfied: and the third set by $a'$, $b'$, $c'$, $f'$, $g'$, $h'$, where
\[
a' = \psi_1 a_1 + \psi_2 a_2 + \psi_3 a_3, \quad \text{etc.},
\]
and the condition $a' f' + b' g' + c' h' = 0$ is satisfied.

We have therefore an equation
\[
T' = (a'g'h')\, x^2 + \text{etc.},
\]
which only differs from the equation $T=0$ by having therein the accented letters in place of the unaccented ones: and, substituting for the accented letters their values, the whole divides by the determinant $(\theta\phi\psi)$, and throwing this out we obtain the required equation $T = 0$.

But it is easier to obtain the equation $T=0$ directly. We have
\begin{align*}
hy - gz + aw &= 0, \\
-hx \phantom{{}-gz} + fz + bw &= 0, \\
gx - fy \phantom{{}+fz} + cw &= 0, \\
-ax - by - cz \phantom{{}+cw} &= 0;
\end{align*}
viz. in virtue of the equation $af + bg + ch = 0$ which connects the six coordinates, these four equations are equivalent to two independent equations which are the equations of the line $(a, b, c, f, g, h)$: or, what is the same thing, any three of these equations imply the fourth equation and also the relation $af + bg + ch = 0$.

We might, from the three linear relations and any three of the last-mentioned four equations, eliminate $a$, $b$, $c$, $f$, $g$, $h$ and so obtain the required equation $T = 0$; but it is better, introducing the arbitrary coefficients $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, $\delta$, to employ all the four equations. The result of the elimination is thus given in the form
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
\alpha, & w, & -z, & y \\
\beta, & w, & \phantom{-}z, & -x \\
\gamma, & -y, & x, & w \\
\delta, & x, & y, & z
\end{vmatrix}
\begin{matrix}
a_1, & g_1, & h_1, & a_1, & b_1, & c_1 \\
a_2, & g_2, & h_2, & a_2, & b_2, & c_2 \\
a_3, & g_3, & h_3, & a_3, & b_3, & c_3 \\
f_1, & g_1, & h_1, & a_1, & b_1, & c_1 \\
f_2, & g_2, & h_2, & a_2, & b_2, & c_2 \\
f_3, & g_3, & h_3, & a_3, & b_3, & c_3
\end{matrix} = 0,
\]
viz. the left-hand side here contains the factor $-(\alpha x + \beta y + \gamma z + \delta w)$; throwing this out, we obtain the required quadric equation $T=0$.

If for the calculation of $T$ we compare the terms containing $\delta$, we have
\[
Tw =
\begin{vmatrix}
w, & -z, & y \\
w, & \phantom{-}z, & -x \\
-y, & x, & w
\end{vmatrix}
\begin{vmatrix}
f_1, & g_1, & h_1 \\
f_2, & g_2, & h_2 \\
f_3, & g_3, & h_3 \\
a_1, & b_1, & c_1 \\
a_2, & b_2, & c_2 \\
a_3, & b_3, & c_3
\end{vmatrix},
\]
where observe that, writing $w = 0$, the right-hand side vanishes as containing the factor
\[
\begin{vmatrix} -z, & y \\ \phantom{-}z, & -x \\ \end{vmatrix}
\begin{vmatrix} -y, & x \end{vmatrix}.
\]
Hence the right-hand side divides by $w$; and one of its terms being evidently $w^2 (abc)$, $T$ contains as it should do the term $(abc)w^2$: the remaining terms can be found without any difficulty, and the foregoing expression for $T$ is thus verified.

\section*{672. On the Game of Mousetrap.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{672. On the Game of Mousetrap}

[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.\ xv.\ (1878), pp.\ 8---10.]

In the note ``A Problem in Permutations,'' \textit{Quarterly Mathematical Journal}, t.\ i.\ (1857), p.\ 79, [161], I have spoken of the problem of permutations presented by this game. A set of cards---ace, two, three, \&c., say up to thirteen---are arranged (in any order) in a circle with their faces upwards; you begin at any card, and count one, two, three, \&c., and if upon counting, suppose the number five, you arrive at the card five, the card is thrown out; and beginning again with the next card, you count one, two, three, \&c., throwing out (if the case happen) a new card as before, and so on until you have counted up to thirteen, without coming to a card which has to be thrown out.

The original question proposed was: for any given number of cards to find the arrangement (if any) which would throw out all the cards in a given order; but (instead of this) we may consider all the different arrangements of the cards, and inquire how many of these there are in which all or any given smaller number of the cards will be thrown out; and (in the several cases) in what orders the cards are thrown out.

Thus to take the simple case of four cards, the different arrangements, with the cards thrown out in each, are

1, & 3, & 4, & 2 &---& 2, 1, 3, 4 \\
1, & 4, & 2, & 3 &---& 3, 2, 1, 4 \\
1, & 4, & 3, & 2 &---& 2, 3, 1, 4 \\
2, & 1, & 3, & 4 &---& 3, 1, 2, 4 \\
2, & 1, & 4, & 3 &---& none \\
2, & 3, & 1, & 4 &---& 3, 4, 1, 2 \\
2, & 3, & 4, & 1 &---& 1, 2, 4, 3 \\
2, & 4, & 1, & 3 &---& 2, 3, 4, 1 \\
2, & 4, & 3, & 1 &---& 1, 3, 2, 4 \\

3, & 1, & 4, & 2 &---& none \\
3, & 2, & 1, & 4 &---& 4, 1, 2, 3 \\

4, & 1, & 3, & 2 &---& 3, 4, 2, 1 \\

The general question is of course to form a like table for the numbers 5, 6, \dots{}, or any greater number of cards.

\section*{673. Note on the Theory of Correspondence.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{673. Note on the Theory of Correspondence}

[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.\ xv.\ (1878), pp.\ 32, 33.]

If the point $P$ on a given curve $U$ of the order $m$, and the point $Q$ on a given curve $V$ of the order $m'$, have a $(1,1)$ correspondence, this implying that the two curves have the same deficiency; then if $PQ$ intersects the consecutive line $P'Q'$ in a point $R$, the locus of $R$ is a curve $W$ of the class $m + m'$, and the point $R$ on this curve has, in general (but not universally), a $(1,1)$ correspondence with the point $P$ on $U$ or with the point $Q$ on $V$.

For, considering the correspondence of the points $P$ and $R$, to a given position of $P$ there corresponds, it is clear, a single position of $R$; on the other hand, starting from $R$, the tangent at this point to the curve $W$ meets the curve $U$ in $m$ points and the curve $V$ in $m'$ points, but it is in general only one of the $m$ points and only one of the $m'$ points which are corresponding points on the curves $U$ and $V$; that is, it is only one of the $m$ points which is a point $P$; and the correspondence of $(P, R)$ is thus a $(1,1)$ correspondence.

But the curves $U$, $V$ may be such that the correspondence of $(P, R)$ is not a $(1,1)$ but a $(k,1)$ correspondence; viz., that to a given position of $P$ there corresponds a single position of $R$, but to a given position of $R$, $k$ positions of $P$.

To show that this is so, imagine through $P$ a line $\Pi$ having therewith a $(k,1)$ correspondence; $P$ being, as above, a point on the curve $U$, the line in question envelopes a curve $W$; and the correspondence is such that, for any given position of $P$ on the curve $U$, we have through it a single position of the line: but, for a given tangent of the curve $W$, we have upon it $k$ positions of the point $P$, viz. $k$ of the $m$ intersections of the line with the curve $U$ are points corresponding to the line; this, of course, implies that the curve $U$ is not any curve whatever of the order $m$, but a curve of a peculiar nature.

Imagine now that we have on the line $\Pi$ a point $Q$, having with $P$ a $(1,1)$ correspondence of a given nature: to fix the ideas, suppose $P$, $Q$ are harmonics in regard to a given conic: since on each of the lines $\Pi$ there are $k$ positions of $P$, there are also on the line $k$ positions of $Q$, and the locus of these $k$ points $Q$ is a curve $V$, say of the order $m'$.

The point $P$ on the curve $U$ and the point $Q$ on the curve $V$ have a $(1,1)$ correspondence. For, consider $P$ as given: there is a single position of the line $\Pi$ intersecting $V$ in $m'$ points, but obviously only one of these is the point $Q$. And consider $Q$ as given: then through $Q$ we have say $\mu$ tangents of the curve $W$; each of these tangents intersects the curve $U$ in $m$ points, $k$ of which are points $P$, but for a tangent taken at random no one of these is the correspondent of $Q$; it is, in general, only one of the $\mu$ tangents which has upon it $k$ points $P$, one of them being the point corresponding to $Q$; that is, to a given position of $Q$ there corresponds a single position of $P$; and the correspondence of the points $(P, Q)$ is thus a $(1,1)$ correspondence.

We have thus the point $P$ on the curve $U$ and the point $Q$ on the curve $V$, which points have with each other a $(1,1)$ correspondence; and the line $\Pi$ is the line $PQ$ joining these points; this intersects the consecutive line in a point $R$; and the locus of $R$ is the curve $W$.

To a given position of $P$ there corresponds a single line $\Pi$, and therefore a single position of $R$; but to a given position of $R$ there correspond $k$ positions of $P$, viz. drawing at $R$ the tangent to the curve $W$, this is a line $\Pi$ having upon it $k$ points $P$, or the correspondence of $(P, Q)$ is, as stated, a $(k,1)$ correspondence.

The foregoing considerations were suggested to me by the theory of parallel curves. Take a curve parallel to a given curve, for example, the ellipse; this is a curve of the order 8, such that every normal thereto is a normal at two distinct points; and the curve has as its evolute the evolute of the ellipse, or, more accurately, the evolute of the ellipse taken twice; but, attending only to the evolute taken once, each tangent of the evolute is a normal of the parallel curve at two distinct points thereof, and the points of the parallel curve have with those of the evolute not a $(1,1)$ but a $(2,1)$ correspondence.

\section*{674. Note on the Construction of Cartesians.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{674. Note on the Construction of Cartesians}

[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.\ xv.\ (1878), p.\ 34.]

If $p = a + b \cos \theta$, and $r = \frac{1}{2}\{p \pm \surd(p^2 - c^2)\}$, then obviously
\[
r^2 - rp + \tfrac{1}{4}c^2 = 0,
\]
that is,
\[
r^2 - r(a + b \cos \theta) + \tfrac{1}{4}c^2 = 0,
\]
which is the equation of a Cartesian.

Here $p = a + b \cos \theta$ is the equation of a lima\c{c}on or nodal Cartesian, having the origin for the node; and for any given value of $\theta$, deducing from the radius vector of the lima\c{c}on the new radius vector $r$ by the above formula $r = \frac{1}{2}\{p \pm \surd(p^2 - c^2)\}$, we obtain a Cartesian, or by giving different values to $c$, a series of Cartesians having the origin for a common focus.

The construction is a very convenient one.

\section*{675. On the Fleflecnodal Planes of a Surface.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{675. On the Fleflecnodal Planes of a Surface}

[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.\ xv.\ (1878), pp.\ 49---51.]

If at a node (or double point) of a plane curve there is on one of the branches an inflexion, (that is, if the tangent has a 3-pointic intersection with the branch), the node is said to be a \textit{flecnode}; and if there is on each of the branches an inflexion, then the node is said to be a \textit{fleflecnode}.

The tangent plane of a surface intersects the surface in a plane curve having at the point of contact a node; if this is a flecnode or a fleflecnode, the tangent plane is said to be a flecnodal or a fleflecnodal plane accordingly. For a quadric surface each tangent plane is fleflecnodal: this is obvious geometrically (since the section is a pair of lines), and it will presently appear that the analytical condition for such a plane is satisfied.

In fact, if the origin be taken at a point of a surface, so that $z = 0$ shall be the equation of the tangent plane, then in the neighbourhood of the point we have
\[
z = (x,y)^2 + (x,y)^3 + \text{\&c.};
\]
and the condition for a fleflecnodal plane is that the term $(x,y)^2$ shall be a factor of the succeeding term $(x,y)^3$.

Now for a quadric surface the equation is
\[
z = \tfrac{1}{2}\{ax^2 + 2hxy + by^2 + 2(fy + gx)z + cz^2\},
\]
that is,
\[
z(1 - fy - gz - hz) = \tfrac{1}{2}(ax^2 + 2hxy + by^2),
\]
or developing as far as the third order in $(x, y)$, we have
\[
z = \tfrac{1}{2}(ax^2 + 2hxy + by^2)(1 + fy + gx),
\]
so that the condition in question is satisfied.

In what follows, I take for greater simplicity $h = 0$, (viz. $x = 0$, $y = 0$ are here the tangents to the two curves of curvature at the point in question), and to avoid fractions write $2f$, $2g$ in place of $f$, $g$ respectively; the developed equation of the quadric surface is thus
\[
z = \tfrac{1}{2}(ax^2 + by^2) + (ax^2 + by^2)(gx + fy).
\]

I consider the parallel surface, obtained by measuring off on the normal a constant length $k$. If, as usual, $p$, $q$ denote $\frac{dz}{dx}$ and $\frac{dz}{dy}$ respectively, then, in general, $(X, Y, Z)$ being the coordinates of the point on the parallel surface,
\[
Z = z + \frac{kp}{\surd(1 + p^2 + q^2)}, \quad X = x - \frac{kp}{\surd(1 + p^2 + q^2)}, \quad Y = y - \frac{kq}{\surd(1 + p^2 + q^2)}.
\]
But in the present case
\begin{align*}
p &= ax + 3agx^2 + 2afxy + bgy^2,\\
q &= by + afx^2 + 2bgxy + 3bfy^2,
\end{align*}
whence
\begin{align*}
X &= x - k(ax + 3agx^2 + 2afxy + bgy^2),\\
Y &= y - k(by + afx^2 + 2bgxy + 3bfy^2);
\end{align*}
or, putting for convenience
\[
X = (1 - ka)\xi, \quad Y = (1 - kb)\eta,
\]
then, for a first approximation $x = \xi$, $y = \eta$; whence, writing
\[
P = 3ag\xi^2 + 2af\xi\eta + bg\eta^2, \quad Q = af\xi^2 + 2bg\xi\eta + 3bf\eta^2,
\]
we find
\[
x = \xi + \frac{kP}{1 - ka}, \quad y = \eta + \frac{kQ}{1 - kb},
\]
and thence
\begin{align*}
Z &= \tfrac{1}{2}(ax^2 + by^2) + (ax^2 + by^2)(gx + fy) \\
&= \tfrac{1}{2}\{a(1 - ka)\xi^2 + b(1 - kb)\eta^2\} + (a\xi^2 + b\eta^2)(g\xi + f\eta).
\end{align*}

Hence, finally,
\[
Z - k = \tfrac{1}{2}\{a(1 - ka)\xi^2 + b(1 - kb)\eta^2\} + (a\xi^2 + b\eta^2)(g\xi + f\eta).
\]

where, changing the origin to the point $x = 0$, $y = 0$, $z = k$ on the parallel surface, the coordinates of the consecutive point are $Z - k$, $X_1 = (1 - ka)\xi$, and $Y_1 = (1 - kb)\eta$.

We cannot, by any determination of the value of $k$, make the plane $Z - k = 0$ a fleflecnodal plane of the parallel surface; but if
\[
\frac{a(1 - ka)}{a} = \frac{b(1 - kb)}{b}, \quad \text{that is,} \quad ka = kb,
\]
then
\[
\frac{a(1 - ka)}{a\xi^2 + b\eta^2} = \frac{b(1 - kb)}{a\xi^2 + b\eta^2} = 1 - ka = 1 - kb,
\]
and the equation becomes
\[
Z - k = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 - ka)(a\xi^2 + b\eta^2) + (a\xi^2 + b\eta^2)(g\xi + f\eta),
\]
viz. the term of the second order has here a factor $g\xi + f\eta$ which divides the term of the third order, and the plane $Z - k = 0$ is a flecnodal plane of the parallel surface.

\section*{676. Note on a Theorem in Determinants.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{676. Note on a Theorem in Determinants}

[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.\ xv.\ (1878), pp.\ 55---57.]

It is well known that if $12$, \&c., denote the determinants formed with the matrix
\[
\begin{matrix}
a, & \beta, & \gamma, & \delta \\
a', & \beta', & \gamma', & \delta'
\end{matrix}
\]
then, identically,
\[
12.34 + 13.42 + 14.23 = 0.
\]
The proper proof of the theorem is obtained by remarking that we have
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
\cdot & \cdot & \alpha & \beta & \gamma & \delta \\
\cdot & \cdot & \alpha' & \beta' & \gamma' & \delta' \\
\cdot & \cdot & \alpha & \beta & \gamma & \delta \\
\cdot & \cdot & \alpha' & \beta' & \gamma' & \delta' \\
\alpha & \beta & \gamma & \delta & \cdot & \cdot \\
\alpha' & \beta' & \gamma' & \delta' & \cdot & \cdot
\end{vmatrix} = 0,
\]
as at once appears by subtracting the first and second lines from the third and fourth lines respectively; and, this being so, the development of the determinant gives the theorem.

The theorem might, it is clear, have been obtained in four different forms according as in the determinant the missing terms were taken to be as above $(\delta, \delta')$, or to be $(\alpha, \alpha')$, $(\beta, \beta')$, or $(\gamma, \gamma')$; but the four results are equivalent to each other.

There is obviously a like theorem for the sums of products of determinants formed with the matrix
\[
\begin{matrix}
\alpha, & \beta, & \gamma, & \delta, & \epsilon, & \zeta \\
\alpha', & \beta', & \gamma', & \delta', & \epsilon', & \zeta' \\
\alpha'', & \beta'', & \gamma'', & \delta'', & \epsilon'', & \zeta''
\end{matrix}
\]
viz. the theorem is obtained by development of the determinant in an identical equation, such as
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
\cdot & \cdot & \cdot & \alpha & \beta & \gamma & \delta & \epsilon & \zeta \\
\cdot & \cdot & \cdot & \alpha' & \beta' & \gamma' & \delta' & \epsilon' & \zeta' \\
\cdot & \cdot & \cdot & \alpha'' & \beta'' & \gamma'' & \delta'' & \epsilon'' & \zeta'' \\
\cdot & \cdot & \cdot & \alpha & \beta & \gamma & \delta & \epsilon & \zeta \\
\cdot & \cdot & \cdot & \alpha' & \beta' & \gamma' & \delta' & \epsilon' & \zeta' \\
\cdot & \cdot & \cdot & \alpha'' & \beta'' & \gamma'' & \delta'' & \epsilon'' & \zeta'' \\
\alpha & \beta & \gamma & \delta & \epsilon & \zeta & \cdot & \cdot & \cdot \\
\alpha' & \beta' & \gamma' & \delta' & \epsilon' & \zeta' & \cdot & \cdot & \cdot \\
\alpha'' & \beta'' & \gamma'' & \delta'' & \epsilon'' & \zeta'' & \cdot & \cdot & \cdot
\end{vmatrix} = 0,
\]
but we thus obtain 15 results which are not all equivalent.

If, for shortness, we write
\begin{align*}
A &= 123 \cdot 456, & -B &= 124 \cdot 356, & -C &= 125 \cdot 346, \\
-D &= 126 \cdot 345, & -E &= 134 \cdot 256, & -F &= 135 \cdot 246, \\
-G &= 136 \cdot 245, & -H &= 145 \cdot 236, & -I &= 146 \cdot 235, \\
-J &= 156 \cdot 234,
\end{align*}
then the fifteen results are
\begin{align*}
A + B - C - D &= 0, & A + B - E - F &= 0, \\
A - C + F - I &= 0, & A - D + G - H &= 0, \\
A - E + F + G &= 0, & A - H - I - J &= 0, \\
B - C - G + H &= 0, & B - D - F + I &= 0, \\
B - E + H + I &= 0, & B - F - G - J &= 0, \\
C + D - E - J &= 0, & C - E + G + I &= 0, \\
C - F - H - J &= 0, & D - E + F + H &= 0, \\
D - G - I - J &= 0,
\end{align*}
which are all satisfied if only
\begin{align*}
A &= \phantom{B + C + D + E} + H + I + J, \\
B &= \phantom{A + C + D + E +} F + G \phantom{+ H + I} + J, \\
C &= \phantom{A + B + D + E +} F \phantom{+ G} + H \phantom{+ I} + J, \\
D &= \phantom{A + B + C + E +} \phantom{F +} G \phantom{+ H} + I + J, \\
E &= \phantom{A + B + C + D +} F + G + H + I \phantom{+ J};
\end{align*}
and we thus have these five relations between the ten products of determinants $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$, $E$, $F$, $G$, $H$, $I$, $J$.

\section*{677. Addition to Mr Glaisher's Paper ``Proof of Stirling's Theorem.''}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{677. Addition to Mr Glaisher's Paper ``Proof of Stirling's Theorem''}

[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.\ xv.\ (1878), pp.\ 63, 64.]

It is easy to extend Mr Glaisher's investigation so as to obtain from it the more approximate value
\[
n! = \surd(2\pi) \cdot n^{n+\frac{1}{2}} e^{-n + \frac{1}{12n}}.
\]
We, in fact, have
\[
\Gamma(n + 1) = n! = n^{n+\frac{1}{2}} e^{-n + \frac{a}{n} + \frac{b}{n^3} + \ldots},
\]
where $a$, $b$, \dots{} are given functions of $n$, viz.
\[
a = \tfrac{1}{2} \left\{\frac{1}{1 \cdot 3} + \frac{1}{5^2} + \ldots - \frac{1}{4}(2n + 1)\right\}, \quad \text{\&c.}
\]
And hence writing $x = 1$, we have
\[
\xi(1) = \xi \cdot 2^{2n} \cdot n^n \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2n+2} = e^{-a - b - \ldots},
\]
that is,
\begin{align*}
n! &= \left(\frac{2n + 2}{2}\right)^{n+\frac{1}{2}} e^{-n - \frac{1}{2}(a + b + \ldots)} \\
&= (n + 1)^{n+\frac{1}{2}} e^{-n - \frac{1}{2}(a + b + \ldots)} \\
&= n^{n+\frac{1}{2}} \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^{n+\frac{1}{2}} e^{-n - \frac{1}{2}(a + b + \ldots)}.
\end{align*}

Writing
\[
\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^{n+\frac{1}{2}} = e^{\theta},
\]
the whole exponent of $e$ is
\[
(n + \tfrac{1}{2}) \log(1 + \tfrac{1}{n}) - n - \tfrac{1}{2}(a + b + \ldots)
\]
We have
\[
\frac{1}{1} + \frac{1}{2} + \ldots + \frac{1}{n} - \log n = \gamma + \frac{A_1}{n} + \frac{A_2}{n^2} + \ldots,
\]
\[
\frac{1}{1^2} + \frac{1}{2^2} + \ldots + \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6} - \frac{1}{n} + \ldots,
\]
(the constant is in fact $\frac{1}{2}\pi^2$, but the value is not required), hence
\[
a = \text{const.} - \frac{1}{12n} + \text{terms in } \frac{1}{n^2}, \frac{1}{n^3}, \text{\&c.};
\]
as regards $b$, $c$, \&c., there are no terms in $\frac{1}{n}$, but we have
\[
b = \text{const.} + \text{terms in } \frac{1}{n^2}, \text{\&c.}, \quad c = \text{const.} + \text{terms in } \frac{1}{n^2}, \text{\&c.}
\]
Hence the whole exponent of $e$ is
\[
= -\frac{1}{12n} + C + \text{terms in } \frac{1}{n^2}, \text{\&c.}
\]
As in Mr Glaisher's investigation, it is shown that $e^C = \surd(2\pi)$, and hence neglecting the terms in $\frac{1}{n^2}$, \&c., the final result is
\[
n! = \surd(2\pi) \cdot n^{n+\frac{1}{2}} e^{-n + \frac{1}{12n}}.
\]

\section*{678. On a System of Quadric Surfaces.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{678. On a System of Quadric Surfaces}

[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.\ xv.\ (1878), pp.\ 124, 125.]

The following theorem was communicated to me by Dr Klein; ``given in regard to a quadric surface two sibi-reciprocal line-pairs, the two tractors (or lines meeting each of the four lines) form a sibi-reciprocal line-pair.''

This may be presented under a more general form as a theorem relating to the tractors of any two line-pairs. In fact, if a given line-pair is taken to be sibi-reciprocal in regard to a quadric surface, we thereby establish only a four-fold relation between the coefficients of the surface, and the surface will still depend on five arbitrary parameters. Whence if two given line-pairs are taken to be each of them sibi-reciprocal in regard to one and the same quadric surface, we thereby establish only an eight-fold relation and the surface will still depend upon one arbitrary parameter.

The theorem thus is: given any two line-pairs, then each of these, and also the pair of tractors, are sibi-reciprocal in regard to a singly infinite system of quadric surfaces.

The question arises, what is this system of quadric surfaces? It is, in fact, the system of surfaces having in common a skew quadrilateral constructed as follows: starting from the two given line-pairs, construct the two tractors, each of them intersected by the given line-pairs in two point-pairs; and on each tractor construct the double or sibi-reciprocal points of the involution thus determined; these double points are the vertices (those on the same tractor being opposite vertices) of the skew quadrilateral; which is consequently at once obtained by joining the two double points on the one tractor with the two double points on the other tractor.

The construction is an immediate consequence of the following theorem: consider a skew quadrilateral, and drawing its two diagonals, take a pair of lines cutting each diagonal harmonically; these will be sibi-reciprocal in regard to any quadric surface whatever passing through the skew quadrilateral; and conversely, any quadric surface in regard to which they are sibi-reciprocal passes through the skew quadrilateral. Hence the pair of tractors, cutting as they do each diagonal harmonically, are sibi-reciprocal in regard to any quadric surface through the skew quadrilateral; that is, in regard to any quadric surface in regard to which the two given line-pairs are sibi-reciprocal.

Hence, recollecting that $U = k^2 w^2 P - Q^2$, the Hessian curve of the order 32 breaks up into
\begin{align*}
U &= 0, & w^8 &= 0, \text{ that is, } Q^4 = 0, \quad w^8 = 0, \\
  &    &      & \text{or the nodal conic, } w = 0, \quad Q = 0, \quad 8 \text{ times (order 16)}, \\
U &= 0, & F &= 0, \text{ that is, } Q^2 = 0, \quad P = 0, \\
  &    &      & \text{or the quadriquadric, } P = 0, \quad Q = 0, \quad 2 \text{ times (order 8)},
\end{align*}
and into a curve (order 8) which is
\[
4k^4 w^2 P^2 + 24P^2 \cdot F = 0;
\]
viz. this, the intersection of the surface with a quadric surface, is the proper Hessian curve.

\begin{align*}

We may verify any of the equations, though the process is rather laborious, for the particular values
\[
P = x^{\frac{1}{2}(p+\alpha)} y^{\frac{1}{2}(p-\alpha)}, \quad Q = x^{\frac{1}{2}(q+\beta)} y^{\frac{1}{2}(q-\beta)}, \quad R = x^{\frac{1}{2}(r+\gamma)} y^{\frac{1}{2}(r-\gamma)}.
\]
Thus, taking the second equation, we have, omitting common factors,
\begin{align*}
(Q, R)^2 &= \tfrac{1}{4}(q + \beta)(q + \beta - 2)(r - \gamma)(r - \gamma - 2) \\
&\quad - 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4}(q + \beta)(q - \beta)(r + \gamma)(r - \gamma) \\
&\quad + \tfrac{1}{4}(q - \beta)(q - \beta - 2)(r + \gamma)(r + \gamma - 2) \\
&= \beta^2(r^2 - r) + \gamma^2(q^2 - q) - 2\beta\gamma(q - 1)(r - 1) - qr(q + r - 2),
\end{align*}
\begin{multline*}
(P, (Q, R)^2)^2 = (q + \beta)(r - \gamma) \cdot \tfrac{1}{4}(p + \alpha)(q + r - \beta - \gamma - 2) \\
- \tfrac{1}{4}(p - \alpha)(q + r + \beta + \gamma - 2) \\
= (\beta r - \gamma q)(\alpha(q + r - 2) - p(\beta + \gamma)) \\
= \alpha\beta r(r + q - 2) - \alpha\gamma q(q + r - 2) - p\beta r^2 + p(\beta - r)\beta\gamma + pq\gamma^2,
\end{multline*}
and from the first of these the expressions of $Q(P, R)^2$ and $(P, QR)^2$ are at once obtained.

The identity to be verified then becomes
\begin{multline*}
[q + r]^3 [q]^2 \{\alpha^2(\beta^2(r^2 - r) + \gamma^2(q^2 - q) - 2\beta\gamma(q - 1)(r - 1) - pr(p + r - 2))\} = \\
\{q + r\} [q]^2 [p]^2 \{\beta^2(\gamma^2(r^2 - r) + \alpha^2(p^2 - p) - 2\gamma\alpha(p - 1)(r - 1) - qr(q + r - 2))\} \\
+ 2\{q + r - 1\} [q]^2 (p - 1)(r - 1) \{\alpha\beta\gamma r(q + r - 2) - \alpha\gamma q(q + r - 2) \\
- p\beta\gamma^2 + p(q - r)\beta\gamma + pq\gamma^2\} \\
+ \{q + r - 2\} [q]^2 [r]^2 \{\alpha^2(q + r)(q + r - 1) + (\beta + \gamma)^2(p^2 - p) \\
- 2\alpha(\beta + \gamma)(p - 1)(q + r - 1) - p(q + r)(p + q + r - 2)\},
\end{multline*}
which is easily verified, term by term; for instance, the terms with $\alpha$, $\beta$, or $\gamma$, give
\begin{multline*}
[q + r]^3 [q]^2 pr(p + r - 2) = (q + r) [q]^2 [p]^2 qr(q + r - 2) \\
+ \{q + r - 2\} [q]^2 [r]^2 p(q + r)(p + q + r - 2),
\end{multline*}
which, omitting the factor $(q + r)(q + r - 2)[q]^2 pr$, is
\[
(q + r - 1)(p + r - 2) = (p - 1)q + (r - 1)(p + q + r + 2);
\]
viz. the right-hand side is
\[
(p - 1)q + (r - 1)q + (r - 1)(p + r - 2) = (q + r - 1)(p + r - 2),
\]
as it should be.

The equations are useful for the demonstration of a subsidiary theorem employed in Gordan's demonstration of the finite number of the covariants of any binary form $V$. Suppose that a system of covariants (including the quantic itself) is $P, Q, R, S, \ldots$; this may be the complete system of covariants; and if it is so, then, $T$ and $V$ being any functions of the form $P^\alpha Q^\beta R^\gamma \ldots$, every derivative $(T, V)^\theta$ must be a term or sum of terms of the like form $P^\alpha Q^\beta R^\gamma \ldots$; the subsidiary theorem is that in order to prove that the case is so, it is sufficient to prove that every derivative $(P, Q)^\theta$, where $P$ and $Q$ are any two terms of the proposed system, is a term or sum of terms of the form in question $P^\alpha Q^\beta R^\gamma \ldots$.

In fact, supposing it shown that every derivative $(T, V)^\theta$ up to a given value $\theta$ of is of the form $P^\alpha Q^\beta R^\gamma \ldots$, we can by successive application of the equation for $Q(P, R)^{\theta + 1}$ regarded as an equation for the reduction of the last term on the right-hand side $(P, QR)^{\theta + 1}$, bring first $(P, QR)^{\theta + 1}$ and then $(P, QRS)^{\theta + 1}$, \&c., and so ultimately any function $(P, V)^{\theta + 1}$, and then again any functions $(PQ, V)^{\theta + 1}$, $(PQR, V)^{\theta + 1}$, \&c., and so ultimately any function $(T, V)^{\theta + 1}$ into the required form $P^\alpha Q^\beta R^\gamma \ldots$: or the theorem, being true for $\theta$, will be true for $\theta + 1$; whence it is true generally.

\section*{682. Formulae Relating to the Right Line.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{682. Formulae Relating to the Right Line}

[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.\ xv.\ (1878), pp.\ 169---171.]

1. Let $\lambda$, $\mu$, $\nu$ be the direction-angles of a line; $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$ the coordinates of a point on the line; and write
\begin{align*}
a &= \cos \lambda, & f &= \beta \cos \nu - \gamma \cos \mu, \\
b &= \cos \mu, & g &= \gamma \cos \lambda - \alpha \cos \nu, \\
c &= \cos \nu, & h &= \alpha \cos \mu - \beta \cos \lambda,
\end{align*}
whence
\[
a^2 + b^2 + c^2 = 1, \quad af + bg + ch = 0,
\]
or the six quantities $(a, b, c, f, g, h)$, termed the coordinates of the line, depend upon four arbitrary parameters.

2. It is at once shown that the condition for the intersection of any two lines $(a, b, c, f, g, h)$, $(a', b', c', f', g', h')$, is
\[
af' + bg' + ch' + a'f + b'g + c'h = 0.
\]

3. Given two lines $(a, b, c, f, g, h)$, $(a', b', c', f', g', h')$, it is required to find their shortest distance, and the coordinates of their line of shortest distance.

Let
\[
Ax + By + Cz + D = 0, \quad Ax + By + Cz + D' = 0,
\]
be parallel planes containing the two lines respectively; then the first plane contains the point $\alpha + r\cos\lambda$, $\beta + r\cos\mu$, $\gamma + r\cos\nu$, and the second contains the point $\alpha' + r'\cos\lambda'$, $\beta' + r'\cos\mu'$, $\gamma' + r'\cos\nu'$; that is, we have
\begin{align*}
A\alpha + B\beta + C\gamma + D &= 0, \\
A\alpha' + B\beta' + C\gamma' + D' &= 0, \\
A\cos\lambda + B\cos\mu + C\cos\nu &= 0, \\
A\cos\lambda' + B\cos\mu' + C\cos\nu' &= 0,
\end{align*}
which last equations may be written
\[
Aa + Bb + Cc = 0, \quad Aa' + Bb' + Cc' = 0,
\]
giving
\[
A : B : C = bc' - b'c : ca' - c'a : ab' - a'b,
\]
or, if we write
\[
\Theta = aa' + bb' + cc',
\]
and assume, as is convenient,
\[
A^2 + B^2 + C^2 = 1,
\]
then
\[
A, B, C = \frac{bc' - b'c, \quad ca' - c'a, \quad ab' - a'b}{\surd(1 - \Theta^2)},
\]
where $\Theta = \text{cosine-inclination} = aa' + bb' + cc'$.

Hence, shortest distance $= D' - D$
\begin{align*}
&= A(\alpha - \alpha') + B(\beta - \beta') + C(\gamma - \gamma') \\
&= \frac{1}{\surd(1 - \Theta^2)} \{(bc' - b'c)(\alpha - \alpha') + (ca' - c'a)(\beta - \beta') + (ab' - a'b)(\gamma - \gamma')\} \\
&= \frac{1}{\surd(1 - \Theta^2)} \{a'(c\beta - b\gamma) + b'(\alpha\gamma - c\alpha) + c'(b\alpha - a\beta) \\
&\quad + a(c'\beta' - b'\gamma') + b'(a\gamma' - c'\alpha') + c(b'\alpha' - a'\beta')\} \\
&= \frac{1}{\surd(1 - \Theta^2)} (af' + bg' + ch' + a'f + b'g + c'h), \quad = \mathfrak{B} \text{ suppose}.
\end{align*}

The six coordinates of the line of shortest distance are $A$, $B$, $C$, $F$, $G$, $H$, where $A$, $B$, $C$ denote as before, and $F$, $G$, $H$ are to be determined.

Since the line meets each of the given lines, we have
\begin{align*}
Af + Bg + Ch + Fa + Gb + Hc &= 0, \\
Af' + Bg' + Ch' + Fa' + Gb' + Hc' &= 0,
\end{align*}
and we have also
\[
FA + GB + HC = 0,
\]
which equations give $F$, $G$, $H$.

Multiplying the first equation by $b'C - c'B$, the second by $Bc - Cb$, and the third by $bc' - b'c$, we find
\[
(b'C - c'B)(Af + Bg + Ch) + (Bc - Cb)(Af' + Bg' + Ch') + F \cdot \surd(1 - \Theta^2) = 0.
\]
Here
\begin{align*}
b'C - c'B &= \frac{1}{\surd(1 - \Theta^2)} \{b'(ab' - a'b) - c'(ca' - c'a)\} \\
&= \frac{1}{\surd(1 - \Theta^2)} \{a(a'^2 + b'^2 + c'^2) - a'(aa' + bb' + cc')\} \\
&= \frac{1}{\surd(1 - \Theta^2)} (a - a'\Theta),
\end{align*}
and similarly
\[
cB - bG = \frac{1}{\surd(1 - \Theta^2)} (a' - a\Theta).
\]

Also, putting for shortness
\[
\Pi = \begin{vmatrix} a, & b, & c \\ a', & b', & c' \\ f, & g, & h \end{vmatrix}, \quad \Pi' = \begin{vmatrix} a, & b, & c \\ a', & b', & c' \\ f', & g', & h' \end{vmatrix},
\]
we have
\[
Af + Bg + Ch = \frac{1}{\surd(1 - \Theta^2)} \Pi, \quad Af' + Bg' + Ch' = \frac{1}{\surd(1 - \Theta^2)} \Pi',
\]
and finally, the determinant which multiplies $F$ is
\[
\frac{1}{\surd(1 - \Theta^2)} \{(bc' - b'c)^2 + (ca' - c'a)^2 + (ab' - a'b)^2\} = \frac{1}{\surd(1 - \Theta^2)} (1 - \Theta^2), \quad = \surd(1 - \Theta^2).
\]

We have thus the value of $F$; forming in the same way those of $G$ and $H$, we find
\begin{align*}
F &= \frac{-1}{(1 - \Theta^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}} \{(a - a'\Theta)\Pi + (a' - a\Theta)\Pi'\}, \\
G &= \frac{-1}{(1 - \Theta^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}} \{(b - b'\Theta)\Pi + (b' - b\Theta)\Pi'\}, \\
H &= \frac{-1}{(1 - \Theta^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}} \{(c - c'\Theta)\Pi + (c' - c\Theta)\Pi'\},
\end{align*}
which, with the foregoing equations for $A$, $B$, $C$, give the six coordinates of the line of shortest distance.

\section*{683. On the Function $\arcsin(x + iy)$.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{683. On the Function $\arcsin(x + iy)$}

[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.\ xv.\ (1878), pp.\ 171---174.]

The determination of the function in question, the arc to a given imaginary sine, is considered in Cauchy's \textit{Exercises d'Analyse}, \&c., t.\ iii.\ (1844), p.\ 382; but it appears, by two hydrodynamical papers by Mr Ferrers and Mr Lamb, \textit{Quarterly Mathematical Journal}, t.\ xiii.\ (1874), p.\ 115, and t.\ xiv.\ (1875), p.\ 40, that the question is connected with the theory of confocal conics.

Taking $c = \surd(a^2 - b^2)$ a positive real quantity which may ultimately be put $= 1$, the question is to find the real quantities $\xi$, $\eta$, such that
\[
\xi + i\eta = \arcsin \tfrac{1}{c}(x + iy),
\]
or say
\[
x + iy = c \sin(\xi + i\eta),
\]
so that
\[
x = c \sin \xi \cos i\eta, \quad iy = c \cos \xi \sin i\eta.
\]

It is convenient to remark that if a value of $\xi + i\eta$ be $\xi' + i\eta'$, then the general value is $2m\pi + \xi' + i\eta'$ or $(2m + 1)\pi - (\xi' + i\eta')$; hence, $\eta$ may be made positive or negative at pleasure; $\cos i\eta$ is in each case positive, but $-\sin i\eta$ has the same sign as $\eta$; hence $\cos \xi$ has the same sign as $x$, but $\sin \xi$ has the same sign as $y$ or the reverse sign, according as $\eta$ is positive or negative; for any given values of $x$ and $y$, we obtain, as will appear, determinate positive values of $\sin^2 \xi$ and $\cos^2 \xi$; and the square roots of these must therefore be taken so as to give to $\sin \xi$, $\cos \xi$ their proper signs respectively.

Suppose that $\lambda$, $\mu$ are the elliptic coordinates of the point $(x, y)$; viz. that we have
\[
\frac{x^2}{a^2 + \lambda} + \frac{y^2}{b^2 + \lambda} = 1, \quad \frac{x^2}{a^2 + \mu} + \frac{y^2}{b^2 + \mu} = 1,
\]
where $a^2 + \lambda$, $b^2 + \lambda$, and $a^2 + \mu$ are positive, but $b^2 + \mu$ is negative.

Calling $\rho$, $\sigma$ the distances of the point $(x, y)$ from the points $(c, 0)$ and $(-c, 0)$, that is, assuming
\[
\rho = \surd\{(x - c)^2 + y^2\}, \quad \sigma = \surd\{(x + c)^2 + y^2\},
\]
then we have
\begin{align*}
\surd(a^2 + \lambda) &= \tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma + \rho), & \text{whence also } \surd(b^2 + \lambda) &= \tfrac{1}{2} \surd\{(\sigma + \rho)^2 - 4c^2\}, \\
\surd(a^2 + \mu) &= \tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma - \rho), & \surd(b^2 + \mu) &= \tfrac{1}{2} \surd\{(\sigma - \rho)^2 - 4c^2\},
\end{align*}
which equations determine $\lambda$, $\mu$ as functions of $x$, $y$.

Now we have
\[
\rho\sigma = \surd\{(a^2 + \lambda)(a^2 + \mu) - c^2\} = \surd\{(x^2 - y^2 - c^2)^2 + 4x^2y^2\},
\]
\[
\rho^2 + \sigma^2 = 2(x^2 + y^2 + c^2);
\]
substituting herein for $x$, $y$ their values $c \sin \xi \cos i\eta$, $-ci \cos \xi \sin i\eta$, we find
\begin{align*}
x^2 - y^2 - c^2 &= c^2(\sin^2 \xi \cos^2 i\eta + \cos^2 \xi \sin^2 i\eta) - c^2(\sin^2 \xi + \cos^2 \xi)(\sin^2 i\eta + \cos^2 i\eta) \\
&= -c^2(\sin^2 \xi \sin^2 i\eta + \cos^2 \xi \cos^2 i\eta),
\end{align*}
whence
\begin{align*}
(x^2 - y^2 - c^2)^2 &= c^4(\cos^2 \xi \cos^2 i\eta + \sin^2 \xi \sin^2 i\eta)^2, \\
4x^2y^2 &= -4c^4 \sin^2 \xi \cos^2 \xi \sin^2 i\eta \cos^2 i\eta \\
&= c^4(\cos^2 \xi \cos^2 i\eta - \sin^2 \xi \sin^2 i\eta)^2.
\end{align*}
Hence
\[
2\rho\sigma = 2c^2(\cos^2 \xi \cos^2 i\eta - \sin^2 \xi \sin^2 i\eta),
\]
and
\[
\rho^2 + \sigma^2 = 2c^2(\sin^2 \xi \cos^2 i\eta + \cos^2 \xi \sin^2 i\eta + 1);
\]
hence
\[
(\rho + \sigma)^2 = 2c^2(\cos^2 i\eta - \sin^2 i\eta + 1) = 4c^2 \cos^2 i\eta,
\]
\[
(\rho - \sigma)^2 = 2c^2(\sin^2 \xi - \cos^2 \xi + 1) = 4c^2 \sin^2 \xi.
\]
Consequently
\[
a^2 + \lambda = c^2 \cos^2 i\eta, \text{ and thence } b^2 + \lambda = -c^2 \sin^2 i\eta,
\]
\[
a^2 + \mu = c^2 \sin^2 \xi, \text{ and } b^2 + \mu = -c^2 \cos^2 \xi,
\]
values which verify as they should do the equations
\[
\frac{x^2}{a^2 + \lambda} + \frac{y^2}{b^2 + \lambda} = 1, \quad \frac{x^2}{a^2 + \mu} + \frac{y^2}{b^2 + \mu} = 1;
\]
viz. these become
\[
\frac{c^2 \sin^2 \xi \cos^2 i\eta}{c^2 \cos^2 i\eta} + \frac{-c^2 \cos^2 \xi \sin^2 i\eta}{-c^2 \sin^2 i\eta} = \sin^2 \xi + \cos^2 \xi = 1,
\]
\[
\frac{c^2 \sin^2 \xi \cos^2 i\eta}{c^2 \sin^2 \xi} + \frac{-c^2 \cos^2 \xi \sin^2 i\eta}{-c^2 \cos^2 \xi} = \cos^2 i\eta + \sin^2 i\eta = 1.
\]

The same equations, or as we may also write them,
\[
\lambda = -a^2 \sin^2 i\eta - b^2 \cos^2 i\eta, \quad \mu = -a^2 \cos^2 \xi - b^2 \sin^2 \xi,
\]
determine $\eta$ as a function of $\lambda$, and $\xi$ as a function of $\mu$; $\lambda$, $\mu$ being by what precedes, given functions of $x$, $y$.

Or more simply, starting from the last-mentioned values of $\lambda$, $\mu$, and substituting these in the expressions
\[
\xi = \frac{a^2 + \lambda}{a^2 - b^2}, \quad \eta = \frac{b^2 + \lambda}{b^2 - a^2},
\]
we find
\[
x^2 = c^2 \sin^2 \xi \cos^2 i\eta, \quad y^2 = -c^2 \cos^2 \xi \sin^2 i\eta,
\]
or say
\[
x = c \sin \xi \cos i\eta, \quad iy = c \cos \xi \sin i\eta,
\]
whence
\[
x + iy = c \sin(\xi + i\eta),
\]
the original relation between $x$, $y$ and $\xi$, $\eta$.

\section*{684. On a Relation between Certain Products of Differences.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{684. On a Relation between Certain Products of Differences}

[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.\ xv.\ (1878), pp.\ 174, 175.]

Consider the function
\[
3\left\{\begin{array}{l}
abc \cdot de \\
+ bcd \cdot ea \\
+ cde \cdot ab \\
+ dea \cdot bc \\
+ eab \cdot cd
\end{array}\right\}
-
\left\{\begin{array}{l}
abd \cdot ce \\
+ bce \cdot da \\
+ cda \cdot eb \\
+ deb \cdot ac \\
+ eac \cdot bd
\end{array}\right\},
\]
where
\[
abc = (a - b)(b - c)(c - a), \quad ab = (a - b)(b - a) = -(a - b)^2, \quad \text{\&c.},
\]
therefore
\[
abc = bca = cab = -bac, \text{ \&c.}, \quad ab = ba.
\]

It is to be shown that the function vanishes if $e = d$. Writing $e = d$, the value is
\[
3(bcd \cdot da + dab \cdot cd) - abd \cdot cd - bcd \cdot da - cda \cdot db - dac \cdot bd,
\]
viz. this is
\begin{align*}
&3\, bcd \cdot ad - abd \cdot cd + 3\, abd \cdot cd - bcd \cdot ad - 2acd \cdot bd \\
&= 2\, bcd \cdot ad - 2acd \cdot bd + 2abd \cdot cd \\
&= 2(bcd \cdot ad + cad \cdot bd + abd \cdot cd),
\end{align*}
which is easily seen to vanish; the value is
\begin{align*}
&(b - c)(c - a)(d - b)(a - d)^2 = -(b - c)(a - d)^2(b - d)(c - d) \\
&+ (c - a)(a - d)(d - e)(b - d)^2 - (c - a)(a - d)(b - d)^2(c - d) \\
&+ (a - b)(b - d)(d - a)(c - d)^2 - (a - b)(a - d)(b - d)(c - d)^2;
\end{align*}
viz. omitting the factor $(a - d)(b - d)(c - d)$, this is
\[
= -(b - c)(a - d) - (c - a)(b - d) - (a - b)(c - d),
\]
which vanishes.

Hence the function also vanishes if $e = a$, or $a = b$ or $b = c$, or $c = d$; and it is thus a mere numerical multiple of $(a - b)(b - c)(c - d)(d - e)(e - a)$, or say it is $= M \cdot abcde$.

To find $M$ write $e = c$, the equation becomes
\begin{align*}
&3abc \cdot dc - cda \cdot cb = M \cdot abc \cdot dc, \\
&+ 3bcd \cdot ca - acd \cdot bc \\
&+ 3cda \cdot bc + 3cab \cdot cd,
\end{align*}
\[
4abc \cdot dc + 4dbc \cdot ac + 4adc \cdot bc = M \cdot abc \cdot dc,
\]
giving $M = 10$.

In fact, we then have
\[
-4abc \cdot dc + 4dbc \cdot ac + 4adc \cdot bc = 0,
\]
that is,
\[
-abc \cdot dc - bde \cdot ac - dac \cdot bc = 0,
\]
which is right.

And we have thus the identity
\[
3\left\{\begin{array}{l}
abc \cdot de \\
+ bcd \cdot ea \\
+ cde \cdot ab \\
+ dea \cdot bc \\
+ eab \cdot cd
\end{array}\right\}
-
\left\{\begin{array}{l}
abd \cdot ce \\
+ bce \cdot da \\
+ cda \cdot eb \\
+ deb \cdot ac \\
+ eac \cdot bd
\end{array}\right\}
= 10 \cdot abcde,
\]
viz. this is
\[
3[abcde] - [acebd] = 10\{abcde\}.
\]

\section*{685. On Mr Cotterill's Goniometrical Problem.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{685. On Mr Cotterill's Goniometrical Problem}

[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.\ xv.\ (1878), pp.\ 196---198.]

The very remarkable formulae contained in Mr Cotterill's paper, ``A goniometrical problem, to be solved analytically in one move, or more simply synthetically in two moves,'' \textit{Quarterly Mathematical Journal}, t.\ vii.\ (1866), pp.\ 259---272, are presented in a form which, to say the least, is not as easily intelligible as might be; and they have not, I think, attracted the attention which they well deserve.

Using his notation, except that I write for angles small roman letters, in order to be able to have the corresponding italic small letters and capitals for the sines and cosines respectively of the same angles, we consider nine angles $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$, $e$, $f$, $x$, $y$, $z$, which are such that the sum of three angles in the same line, or in the same column, is an odd multiple of $\pi$.

Of course, any four angles such as $a$, $b$, $d$, $e$ are arbitrary, and each of the remaining angles is then determinate save as to an even multiple of $\pi$. And it may be remarked that these angles $a$, $b$, $d$, $e$ may represent the inclinations of any four lines to a fifth line, and that the remaining angles are then at once obtained, as in the figure.

The small roman letters are here used to denote as well angles as points, being so placed as to show what the angles are which they respectively denote; the points $*$, $*$ are constructed as the intersections of the lines $ac$, $bc$ by the circle circumscribed about $fxy$, and the angle $z$ is the angle which the points $*$, $*$ subtend at $x$ or $y$. It will be observed that the sum of the three angles in a line or column is in each case $= \pi$.

But this in passing: the analytical theorem is, first, we can form with the sines and cosines of the angles in any two lines or columns a function $\mathfrak{S}$ presenting itself under two distinct forms, which are in fact equal in value, or say $\mathfrak{S}$ is a symmetrical function of the two lines or columns, viz. for the first and second lines this is
\[
\mathfrak{S}\binom{a,b,c}{d,e,f} = a^2 A\mathfrak{h}c + b^2 Bca + f^2 C\mathfrak{a}b = a'^2 D\mathfrak{e}f + b'^2 E\mathfrak{f}d + c'^2 F\mathfrak{d}e,
\]
where, as already mentioned, $a$, $A$ denote $\sin a$, $\cos a$, and so for the other letters.

Secondly, if to the $\mathfrak{S}$ of any two lines or columns we add twice the product of the six sines, we obtain a sum $\mathfrak{M}$ which has the same value from whichever two lines or columns we obtain it; or, say $\mathfrak{M}$ is a symmetrical function of the matrix of the nine angles.

Thus
\[
\mathfrak{M} = \mathfrak{S}\binom{a,b,c}{d,e,f} + 2abcdef;
\]
which is one of a system of six forms each of which (on account of the two forms of the $\mathfrak{S}$ contained in it) may be regarded as a double form, and the twelve values are all of them equal.

There are, moreover, 15 other forms of $\mathfrak{M}$, viz. 3 line-forms, such as $bcdx + caey + abfz$ (belongs to line $a$, $b$, $c$), 3 column-forms, such as $dxbc + xaef + adyz$ (belongs to column $a$, $d$, $x$), and 9 term-forms, such as $e\beta\gamma x^2 + f\gamma\alpha y^2 + 2efyz A$ (belongs to term $a$), and the $12 + 15 = 27$ values are all equal.

The several identities can of course be verified by means of the relations between the nine angles, or rather the derived sine- and cosine-relations
\[
c = aB + bA, \quad C = ab - AB, \quad \text{\&c.}
\]

As regards the two forms of $\mathfrak{S}$, the identity to be verified may be written
\[
c(a'^2 De + b'^2 Ed - c'^2 F\mathfrak{a}b) = f(a'^2 De + b'^2 Ed - f^2 C\mathfrak{a}b).
\]
Proceeding to reduce the factor $a'^2 De + b'^2 Ed - f^2 C\mathfrak{a}b$, if we first write herein $f = eD + dE$, it becomes
\[
a'^2 De + b'^2 Ed - (eD + dE)C\mathfrak{a}b,
\]
which is
\[
= aDe(a - bC) + bEd(b - aC),
\]
and then writing $C = ab - AB$, we have $a - bC = a(1 - b^2) + bAB = B(aB + bA) = Bc$; and, similarly, $b - aC = Ac$; whence the term is $= c(aeBD + bdAE)$; or, in the equation to be verified, the right-hand side is $= cf(aeBD + bdAE)$, and by a similar reduction, the left-hand side is found to have the same value.

The paper contains various other interesting results.
\hfill C.~X. 38

\section*{686. On a Functional Equation.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{686. On a Functional Equation}

[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.\ xv.\ (1878), pp.\ 315---325; \textit{Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society}, vol.\ ix.\ (1878), p.\ 29.]

I was led by a hydrodynamical problem to consider a certain functional equation: viz. writing for shortness
\[
x_1 = \frac{-1}{cx + d},
\]
this is
\[
\phi x_1 - \phi x = \frac{Ax + B}{Cx + D}.
\]

I find by a direct process, which I will afterwards explain, the solution
\[
\phi x = \frac{A}{\surd\{(a - d)^2 + 4bc\}} \cdot \frac{(AD - BC)}{c(cC - cD)} \int \frac{\sin \xi t \sin \eta t \, dt}{\sin \zeta t \sinh \pi t},
\]
where $\xi$ is a constant, but $\xi$, $\eta$ are complicated logarithmic functions of $x$ (which depend also on the quantities $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$, $C$, $D$); $\sinh \pi t$ denotes as usual the hyperbolic sine, $\tfrac{1}{2}(e^{\pi t} - e^{-\pi t})$.

The values of $\xi$, $\eta$, $\zeta$ are given by the formulae
\[
\cos \zeta = \frac{a + d}{\surd\{(a + d)^2 - 4(ad - bc)\}} = \frac{a + d}{\surd\{(a - d)^2 + 4bc\}},
\]
\begin{align*}
\mathfrak{a} &= ax + b, & \mathfrak{h} &= -dx + b, & \mathfrak{c} &= cx + d, & \mathfrak{d} &= cx - a, \\
W &= C\mathfrak{a} + B\mathfrak{c}, & Z &= C\mathfrak{h} + D\mathfrak{d}, & \\
R &= W + \lambda Z, & S &= -W - \lambda Z, & \\
R' &= W + \lambda' Z, & S' &= -W - \lambda' Z, &
\end{align*}
which determine $\lambda$, $R$, $S$, $R'$, $S'$ and then
\[
\xi = \tfrac{1}{2} \log \frac{RS'}{R'S}, \quad \eta = \tfrac{1}{2} \left(\log \lambda + \log \frac{RR'}{SS'}\right), \quad \zeta = \tfrac{1}{2} \log \lambda.
\]

There is some difficulty as to the definite integral, on account of the denominator factor $\sin \zeta t$, which becomes $= 0$ for the series of values $t = \frac{m\pi}{\zeta}$, but this is a point which I do not enter into.

I will in the first instance verify the result. Writing $x_1$ in place of $x$, and taking $\xi_1$, $\eta_1$ to denote the corresponding values of $\xi$, $\eta$, it will be shown that
\[
\xi_1 = \xi, \quad \eta_1 = \eta + 2\zeta,
\]
see post, (1).

Hence in the difference $\phi x - \phi x_1$ we have the integral
\[
\int \frac{\sin \zeta t \{\sin \eta t - \sin(\eta + 2\zeta)t\} \, dt}{\sin \zeta t \sinh \pi t},
\]
(where and in all that follows the limits are $\infty$, $0$, as before); here, since
\[
\sin \eta t - \sin(\eta + 2\zeta)t = -2\sin \zeta t \cos(\eta + \zeta)t,
\]
the factor $\sin \zeta t$ divides out, and the numerator is $= -2\sin \zeta t \cos(\eta + \zeta)t$, which is
\[
= \sin(\eta + \zeta - \xi)t - \sin(\eta + \zeta + \xi)t.
\]
Hence the integral in question is
\[
\int \frac{\sin(\eta + \zeta - \xi)t \, dt}{\sinh \pi t} - \int \frac{\sin(\eta + \zeta + \xi)t \, dt}{\sinh \pi t}.
\]

Now we have in general
\[
\frac{1}{1 + \exp. \alpha} = \tfrac{1}{2} - \int \frac{\sin \alpha t \, dt}{\sinh \pi t};
\]
(this is, in fact, Poisson's formula
\[
\frac{1}{1 + k\beta^{2n}} = \tfrac{1}{2} - 2\int \frac{\sin(2n \log \beta + \log k)t \cdot dt}{e^{\pi t} - e^{-\pi t}},
\]
in the second Memoir on the distribution of Electricity, \&c., M\'{e}m.\ de l'Inst, 1811, p.\ 223); and hence the value is
\[
-\frac{1}{1 + \exp. (\eta + \zeta - \xi)} + \frac{1}{1 + \exp. (\eta + \zeta + \xi)},
\]
or since
\[
\eta + \zeta = \log\lambda + \tfrac{1}{2}\log\frac{RR'}{SS'}, \quad \xi = \tfrac{1}{2}\log\frac{RS'}{R'S},
\]
we have
\[
\eta + \zeta - \xi = \log\lambda + \tfrac{1}{2}\log\frac{R'^2}{S'^2} = \log\lambda \cdot \frac{R'}{S'},
\]
\[
\eta + \zeta + \xi = \log\lambda + \tfrac{1}{2}\log\frac{R^2}{S^2} = \log\lambda \cdot \frac{R}{S}.
\]
\hfill $38\text{---}2$

And the value is thus
\[
= -\frac{1}{1 + \lambda \dfrac{R'}{S'}} + \frac{1}{1 + \lambda \dfrac{R}{S}}, \quad = -\frac{(RS' - R'S)\lambda}{(\lambda R' + S')(\lambda R + S)}.
\]

Hence, from the assumed value of $\phi x$, we obtain
\[
\phi x - \phi x_1 = \frac{A}{C}(x - x_1) - \frac{\surd\{(a - d)^2 + 4bc\}\,(AD - BC)\,(RS' - R'S)\lambda}{C(dC - cD)(\lambda R' + S')(\lambda R + S)}.
\]

We have
\[
RS' - R'S = \frac{(\lambda - 1)(a + d)^2}{ad - bc} (dC - cD) \{cx^2 + (d - a)x - b\},
\]
\[
R\lambda + S = (\lambda^2 - 1)(cx + d), \qquad \text{see post, (2),}
\]
\[
R'\lambda + S' = (\lambda - 1)(a + d)(Cx + D),
\]
or since
\[
\frac{cx^2 + (d - a)x - b}{cx + d} = x - x_1,
\]
this is
\[
\phi x - \phi x_1 = \frac{A}{C}(x - x_1) - \frac{\surd\{(a - d)^2 + 4bc\}\,(AD - BC)\,(a + d)\lambda}{(ad - bc)(\lambda^2 - 1)(Cx + D)}(x - x_1).
\]

But from the value of $\lambda$,
\[
\frac{\lambda}{\lambda^2 - 1} = \frac{ad - bc}{(a + d)\surd\{(a - d)^2 + 4bc\}},
\]
and the equation thus is
\[
\phi x - \phi x_1 = (x - x_1)\left\{\frac{A}{C} - \frac{AD - BC}{C(Cx + D)}\right\}, \quad = (x - x_1)\frac{Ax + B}{Cx + D},
\]
as it should be.

(1) For the foregoing values of $\xi_1$, $\eta_1$, we require $R_1$, $S_1$, $R_1'$, $S_1'$, the values which $R$, $S$, $R'$, $S'$ assume on writing therein $x_1$ for $x$. We have
\[
R_1 = \lambda(cx_1 + d) + (cx_1 - a), \quad S_1 = -(cx_1 + d) - \lambda(cx_1 - a):
\]
substituting for $x_1$ its value, we find
\[
R_1(cx + d) = (a + d)\lambda(cx + d) - (ad - bc)(\lambda + 1),
\]
or writing herein
\[
ad - bc = \frac{(a + d)^2\lambda}{(\lambda + 1)^2},
\]
this is
\[
R_1(cx + d) = \frac{(a + d)\lambda}{\lambda + 1} R;
\]
and similarly
\[
S_1(cx + d) = \frac{a + d}{\lambda + 1} S.
\]
