\medskip
\textit{The Alternant of two multilinear operators.}

\medskip
If $P$, $Q$ are the two operators, we have as usual
\[ P\,.\,Q = PQ + P*Q, \]
where $P\,.\,Q$ denotes the successive operation first with $Q$ and then with $P$ upon any operand, $PQ$ is the mere algebraical product of the operators, and $P*Q$ is the operator obtained by the operation of $P$ upon $Q$.

Similarly,
\[ Q\,.\,P = QP + Q*P, \]
and since $QP$ is the same thing as $PQ$, we obtain
\[ P\,.\,Q - Q\,.\,P = (P*Q) - (Q*P), \]
either of which equal expressions, or say rather the second of them, is called the alternant of $P$, $Q$ and is written $[P\,.\,Q]$: viz.\ as the definition of the alternant, we have
\[ [P\,.\,Q] = (P*Q) - (Q*P). \]

We have the remarkable theorem, that the alternant of any two operators $(\mu',\nu';\,m',n')$, $(\mu,\nu;\,m,n)$ is an operator $(\mu_{1},\nu_{1};\,m_{1},n_{1})$, where
\begin{align*}
\mu_{1} &= (m+m'-1)\left\{\tfrac{\mu'}{m'}(\mu+n'\nu) - \tfrac{\mu}{m}(\mu'+n\nu')\right\},\\
\nu_{1} &= (n'-n)\,\nu\nu' + \tfrac{m'-1}{m'}\,\mu'\nu - \tfrac{m-1}{m}\,\mu\nu',\\
m_{1} &= m'+m-1,\\
n_{1} &= n'+n,
\end{align*}
or since $m_{1}$, $n_{1}$ have such simple expressions, say it is an operator $(\mu_{1},\nu_{1};\,m'+m-1,\,n'+n)$, where $\mu_{1}$, $\nu_{1}$ have the values just written down.

\medskip
We see at once how these values $m_{1}=m'+m-1$, $n_{1}=n'+n$, arise: $Q=(\mu,\nu;\,m,n)$ contains the letters $(a,b,c,d,\ldots)$ in the degree $m$, and it contains differential symbols $\partial$ which, operating on any function of these letters, diminish the degree by 1; similarly $P=(\mu',\nu';\,m',n')$ contains the letters $(a,b,c,d,\ldots)$ in the degree $m'$, and it contains the differential symbols $\partial$ which, operating on any function of the letters, diminish the degree by 1. Hence $P*Q$ contains the letters in the degree $(m'-1)+m$, and similarly $Q*P$ contains them in the same degree $(m-1)+m'$: thus in the alternant the degree is $m'+m-1$. Again, in $Q$ the weights of the successive functions $A$, $B$, $C,\ldots$ are $0$, $1$, $2,\ldots$ and these are combined with differential symbols $\partial_{a_{n}}$, $\partial_{a_{n+1}},\ldots$ which operating on any function of the letters diminish the weight by $n$, $n+1,\ldots$ respectively: that is, the terms $A\partial_{a_{n}}$, $B\partial_{a_{n+1}},\ldots$ each diminish the weight by $n$. So in $P=(\mu',\nu';\,m',n')$ the weights of the successive terms $A'$, $B'$, $C',\ldots$ are $0$, $1$, $2,\ldots$ and these are combined with differential symbols $\partial_{a_{n'}}$, $\partial_{a_{n'+1}},\ldots$ which operating on any function of the letters diminish the weights by $n'$, $n'+1,\ldots$ respectively. We may say that $Q$ is a sum of terms such as $\Theta_{k}\,\partial_{a_{n+k}}$, where the subscript $k$ of $\Theta$ denotes the weight of $\Theta$; operating hereon with $P$, the corresponding term of $P*Q$ is of the form $\Theta_{k-r}\,\partial_{a_{n+k}}$, or (what is the same thing) the general form of the terms of $P*Q$ is $\Theta_{k}\,\partial_{a_{n+n'+k}}$; and in like manner this is the general form of the terms of $Q*P$; and it is thus also the general form of the terms of the alternant $[P\,.\,Q]$. It thus appears that, admitting the alternant to be an operator $(\mu_{1},\nu_{1};\,m_{1},n_{1})$, the value of $n_{1}$ is $n'+n$.

&\quad = a\partial_{b}\;*\;a\partial_{c} - a\partial_{c}\;*\;a\partial_{b}
\end{align*}

For the general proof, writing for a moment
\[ C_{0} = \mu A_{0},\quad C_{1} = (\mu+\nu)\,A_{1},\quad C_{2} = (\mu+2\nu)\,A_{2},\;\;\&\text{c.}, \]
and similarly
\[ C_{0}' = \mu' A_{0}',\quad C_{1}' = (\mu'+\nu')\,A_{1}',\quad C_{2}' = (\mu'+2\nu')\,A_{2}',\;\;\&\text{c.}, \]
where the accented symbols refer to the values $(\mu',\nu';\,m',n')$, we have
\begin{align*}
[(\mu',\nu';\,m',n')\,(\mu,\nu;\,m,n)]
 &= C_{0}'\partial_{a_{n'}}\;\;\bullet\;\;C_{0}\partial_{a_{n}} - C_{0}\partial_{a_{n}}\;\bullet\;C_{0}'\partial_{a_{n'}}\\
 &\;\;+ C_{1}'\partial_{a_{n'+1}} + C_{1}\partial_{a_{n+1}} + C_{2}\partial_{a_{n+2}}\\
 &\;\;+ C_{2}'\partial_{a_{n'+2}} + C_{2}'\partial_{a_{n'+2}} + C_{2}\partial_{a_{n+2}}\\
 &\;\;\;\;\vdots\qquad\qquad\qquad\;\vdots\\
 &\;\;+ C_{n}'\partial_{a_{n'+n}} + C_{n'}\partial_{a_{n+n'}}\\
 &\;\;+ C_{n+1}'\partial_{a_{n'+n+1}} + C_{n'+1}\partial_{a_{n+n'+1}}
\end{align*}
or observing that in the series $C_{0}$, $C_{1}$, $C_{2},\ldots$, the first term that contains $a_{n'}$ is $C_{n'}$, and the like as regards the series $C_{0}'$, $C_{1}'$, $C_{2}',\ldots$, this is
\begin{align*}
&= [(C_{0}'C_{n'})\,C_{0}' - (C_{0}C_{n})\,C_{0}']\,\partial_{a_{n+n'}}\\
&\quad + [(C_{0}'\partial_{a_{n'}} + C_{1}'\partial_{a_{n'+1}})\,C_{n'+1} - (C_{0}\partial_{a_{n}} + C_{1}\partial_{a_{n+1}})\,C_{n'+1}']\,\partial_{a_{n+n'+1}}\\
&\quad + [(C_{0}'\partial_{a_{n'}} + C_{1}'\partial_{a_{n'+1}} + C_{2}'\partial_{a_{n'+2}})\,C_{n'+2} - \cdots]\,\partial_{a_{n+n'+2}}\\
&\quad\vdots
\end{align*}
and it is thus of the form
\[ C_{0}''\,\partial_{a_{n+n'}} + C_{1}''\,\partial_{a_{n+n'+1}} + C_{2}''\,\partial_{a_{n+n'+2}} + \&\text{c.}, \]
i.e.\ the value of $n_{1}$ is $=n+n'$.

I confine myself to the comparison of the first and second coefficients: substituting for the $C$'s their expressions in terms of the $A$'s, we ought to have
\begin{align*}
&\mu'A_{0}'\partial_{a_{n'}}(\mu+n'\nu)\,A_{n'} - \mu A_{0}\partial_{a_{n}}(\mu'+n\nu')\,A_{n}' = \mu_{1}A_{0}'',\\
&(\mu'A_{0}'\partial_{a_{n'}} + (\mu'+\nu')\,A_{1}'\partial_{a_{n'+1}})\,[\mu+(n'+1)\,\nu]\,A_{n'+1} = (\mu_{1}+\nu_{1})\,A_{1}''.
\end{align*}

Now assuming $m_{1}=m'+m-1$, and attending to the values
\[ A_{0} = \tfrac{1}{m}\,a_{0}^{m},\quad A_{0}' = \tfrac{1}{m'}\,a_{0}^{m'},\quad A_{0}'' = \tfrac{1}{m'+m-1}\,a_{0}^{m'+m-1}, \]
\[ A_{1} = a_{0}^{m-1}\,a_{1},\quad A_{1}' = a_{0}^{m'-1}\,a_{1},\quad A_{1}'' = a_{0}^{m'+m-2}\,a_{1}, \]

\bigskip
we see at once that in the first equation the terms contain each of them the factor $a_{0}^{m'+m-1}$, and in the second equation they contain each of them the factor $a_{0}^{m'+m-2}\,a_{1}$; omitting these factors, we find
\[
\frac{\mu'}{m'}(\mu+n'\nu) - \frac{\mu}{m}(\mu'+n\nu') = \frac{\mu_{1}}{m'+m-1},
\]
\[
\frac{m'-1}{m'}(\mu+n'\nu+\nu)(m-1) + (\mu'+\nu')\,(\mu+n'\nu+\nu) - (\mu+\nu)\,(\mu'+n\nu'+\nu')
\]
\[
\quad - (m'+m-1)\,\tfrac{\mu_{1}}{m'+m-1}(\mu+n'\nu) - (m'+m-1)\,\tfrac{\nu_{1}}{m'+m-1} = \mu_{1}+\nu_{1}.
\]

The first of these gives
\[
\mu_{1} = (m'+m-1)\,\left\{\frac{\mu'}{m'}(\mu+n'\nu) - \frac{\mu}{m}(\mu'+n\nu')\right\},
\]
which is the value of $\mu_{1}$: substituting this in the second equation, we have
\begin{align*}
\nu_{1} &= \tfrac{\mu'}{m'}(\mu+n'\nu+\nu)(m-1) - \tfrac{\mu}{m}(m'-1)(\mu'+n\nu'+\nu')\\
        &\quad + (\mu'+\nu')(\mu+n'\nu+\nu) - (\mu+\nu)(\mu'+n\nu'+\nu')\\
        &\quad - (m'+m-1)\,\tfrac{\mu'}{m'}(\mu+n'\nu) - (m'+m-1)\,\tfrac{\mu}{m}(\mu'+n\nu').
\end{align*}

In the left-hand column, the terms containing $\nu$, $(\mu+n'\nu)$ are
\[
\left\{\tfrac{\mu'}{m'}(m-1) + \mu' + \nu' - \tfrac{\mu'}{m'}(m'+m-1)\right\},
\]
and there are besides the terms $\tfrac{\mu'}{m'}\nu\,(m-1) + \nu\,(\mu'+\nu')$, hence the left-hand column is
\[ = \nu\nu'\,(n'+1) + \mu\nu' + \mu'\nu\left(\tfrac{m-1}{m'} + 1\right). \]

We have therefore
\[
\nu_{1} = \nu\nu'\,(n'+1) + \mu\nu' + \mu'\nu\left(\tfrac{m-1}{m'} + 1\right) - \nu\nu'\,(n+1) - \mu\nu'\left(\tfrac{m-1}{m} + 1\right) - \mu'\nu,
\]
that is,
\[
\nu_{1} = \nu\nu'\,(n'-n) + \tfrac{m'-1}{m'}\,\mu'\nu - \tfrac{m-1}{m}\,\mu\nu'.
\]

To complete the proof, it would be of course necessary to compare the remaining terms on the two sides respectively: but in what precedes it is shown that, the form being assumed, the expression for the alternant must of necessity be $(\mu_{1},\nu_{1};\,m'+m-1,\,n'+n)$, where $\mu_{1}$, $\nu_{1}$ have their foregoing values.

\bigskip

Resuming the equation
\[ [(\mu',\nu';\,m',n')(\mu,\nu;\,m,n)] = (\mu_{1},\nu_{1};\,m_{1},n_{1}), \]
where $\mu_{1}$, $\nu_{1}$, $m_{1}$, $n_{1}$ have the before-mentioned values, then if we herein consider $\mu$, $\nu$, $m$, $n$; $\mu_{1}$, $\nu_{1}$, $m_{1}$, $n_{1}$ as given, we can find $\mu'$, $\nu'$, $m'$, $n'$.

We have $m_{1} = m'+m-1$, $n_{1} = n'+n$, that is, $m' = m_{1}-m+1$, $n' = n_{1}-n$, and substituting these values in the expressions of $\mu_{1}$ and $\nu_{1}$, we find
\[
\frac{\mu_{1}}{m_{1}} = \frac{\mu'}{m_{1}-m+1}\,[\mu + (n_{1}-n)\,\nu] - \frac{\mu}{m}(\mu'+n\nu'),
\]
\[
\nu_{1} = (n_{1}-2n)\,\nu\nu' + \frac{m-1}{m_{1}-m+1}\,\mu'\nu - \frac{m_{1}-m}{m}\,\mu\nu',
\]
that is,
\[
\frac{\mu_{1}}{m_{1}} = \left\{\frac{\mu}{(m_{1}-m+1)} + \frac{\nu\,(n_{1}-n)}{m_{1}-m+1} - \frac{\mu}{m}\right\}\mu' - \frac{\mu n}{m}\,\nu',
\]
\[
\nu_{1} = \left\{\frac{(m-1)\,\nu}{(m_{1}-m+1)}\right\}\mu' + \left\{(n_{1}-2n)\,\nu - \frac{m_{1}-m}{m}\,\mu\right\}\nu',
\]
which are linear equations for the determination of $\mu'$ and $\nu'$.

\textbf{\large 944.}\\[4pt]
\textbf{ON PFAFF-INVARIANTS.}\\[6pt]
[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.\ XXVI.\ (1893), pp.\ 195--205.]
\end{center}

\medskip
\textbf{1.} The functions which I propose to call Pfaff-invariants present themselves and play a leading part in the memoir, Clebsch, ``Ueber das \textit{Pfaff}sche Problem'' (Zweite Abhandlung), \textit{Crelle}, t.\ LXI.\ (1863), pp.\ 146--179: but it is interesting to consider them for their own sake as invariants, and in the notation which I have elsewhere used for the functions called Pfaffians. The great simplification effected by this notation is, I think, at once shown by the remark that Clebsch's expression $R$, which he defines by the periphrasis ``Sei ferner $R$ der \textit{rationale} Ausdruck dessen Quadrat der Determinant der $a_{ik}$ gleich ist'' (l.c.\ p.\ 149), is nothing else than the Pfaffian $1234\ldots 2n-1.\,2n$, and that its differential coefficients $R_{ik}=\dfrac{\partial R}{\partial a_{ik}}$ are the Pfaffians obtained from the foregoing by the mere omission of any two symbolic numbers $i$, $k$.

\medskip
\textbf{2.} I call to mind that the symbols $12$, $13$, \&c., made use of are throughout such that $12=-21$, \&c.; and that the definition of the successive Pfaffians $12$, $1234$, \&c., is as follows:
\begin{align*}
12 &= 12,\\
1234 &= 12\,.\,34 + 13\,.\,42 + 14\,.\,23,\\
123456 &= 12\,.\,3456 + 13\,.\,4562 + 14\,.\,5623 + 15\,.\,6234 + 16\,.\,2345,
\end{align*}
in which last expression $3456$ denotes the Pfaffian $34\,.\,56 + 35\,.\,64 + 36\,.\,45$, and similarly $4562$ \&c.; and so on for any even number of symbols. Of course, instead of the symbolic numbers $1$, $2$, $3$, \&c., we may have any other numbers ($0$ is frequently used in the sequel as a symbolic number), or we may have letters or other symbols.

\medskip
\textbf{3.} I use also a function very analogous to a Pfaffian, which is expressed in the same notation, viz.\ this is
\begin{align*}
\phi\psi 12 &= \phi\psi\,12,\\
\phi\psi 1234 &= \phi\psi 12\,.\,34 + \phi\psi 13\,.\,42 + \phi\psi 14\,.\,23,\\
&\quad\quad + \phi\psi 34\,.\,12 + \phi\psi 42\,.\,13 + \phi\psi 23\,.\,14,\\
\phi\psi 123456 &= \phi\psi 12\,.\,34\,.\,56 + \phi\psi 34\,.\,12\,.\,56 + \phi\psi 56\,.\,12\,.\,34 + \&\text{c.},
\end{align*}
viz.\ taking any term $12\,.\,34\,.\,56$ of the Pfaffian $123456$, $\phi\psi$ is connected successively with each of the binary symbols $12$, $34$, $56$ of the term, so as to give rise to terms containing the quaternary symbols $\phi\psi 12$, \&c. Such function may be called a co-Pfaffian.

\medskip
\textbf{4.} To avoid suffixes I use different letters $(x,y)$, $(x,y,z)$, \&c., as the case may be, associating these with the numbers $(1,2)$, $(1,2,3)$, \&c. In the case of a differential of an even number $2n$ of terms, for instance $Xdx + Ydy + Zdz + Wdw$, I consider the functions $1234$, $\phi 1234$, and $\phi\psi 1234$, the first and second of which are Pfaffians, the last a co-Pfaffian, as explained above. To fix in connexion with the differential $Xdx + Ydy + Zdz + Wdw$ the meanings of these expressions, I assume
\[
12 = \frac{dX}{dy} - \frac{dY}{dx},\quad 13 = \frac{dX}{dz} - \frac{dZ}{dx},\quad \&\text{c.,}
\]
(of course these imply $12=-21$, \&c.),
\[
01 = -10 = X,\quad 02 = -20 = Y,\quad \&\text{c.;}
\]
$\phi$ is an arbitrary function of $x$, $y$, $z$, $w$, and I write
\[
\phi 0 = -0\phi = 0,\quad \phi 1 = -1\phi = \frac{d\phi}{dx},\quad \phi 2 = -2\phi = \frac{d\phi}{dy},\quad \&\text{c.;}
\]
$\psi$ is also an arbitrary function of $x$, $y$, $z$, $w$, and I write
\[
\phi\psi 12 = \frac{d\phi}{dx}\,\frac{d\psi}{dy} - \frac{d\phi}{dy}\,\frac{d\psi}{dx} = \frac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(x,y)},\quad \&\text{c.}
\]
(this implies $\phi\psi 21 = -\phi\psi 12$, \&c.).

\medskip
\textbf{5.} Thus, at full length, the functions are
\begin{align*}
1234 &= 12\,.\,34 + 13\,.\,42 + 14\,.\,23\\
&= \left(\tfrac{dX}{dy} - \tfrac{dY}{dx}\right)\!\left(\tfrac{dZ}{dw} - \tfrac{dW}{dz}\right) + \left(\tfrac{dX}{dz} - \tfrac{dZ}{dx}\right)\!\left(\tfrac{dW}{dy} - \tfrac{dY}{dw}\right) + \left(\tfrac{dX}{dw} - \tfrac{dW}{dx}\right)\!\left(\tfrac{dY}{dz} - \tfrac{dZ}{dy}\right),\\[4pt]
\phi 1234 &= \phi 0\,.\,1234 + \phi 1\,.\,2340 + \phi 2\,.\,3401 + \phi 3\,.\,4012 + \phi 4\,.\,0123\\
&= \frac{d\phi}{dx}(23\,.\,40 + 24\,.\,03 + 20\,.\,34)\\
&\quad + \frac{d\phi}{dy}(34\,.\,01 + 30\,.\,14 + 31\,.\,40)\\
&\quad + \frac{d\phi}{dz}(40\,.\,12 + 41\,.\,20 + 42\,.\,01)\\
&\quad + \frac{d\phi}{dw}(01\,.\,23 + 02\,.\,31 + 03\,.\,12)
\end{align*}

\[
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
\phi 1234 = \frac{d\phi}{dx}\!\left\{-W\!\left(\tfrac{dY}{dz} - \tfrac{dZ}{dy}\right) + Z\!\left(\tfrac{dY}{dw} - \tfrac{dW}{dy}\right) - Y\!\left(\tfrac{dZ}{dw} - \tfrac{dW}{dz}\right)\right\}
+ \frac{d\phi}{dy}\!\left\{X\!\left(\tfrac{dZ}{dw} - \tfrac{dW}{dz}\right) - Z\!\left(\tfrac{dX}{dw} - \tfrac{dW}{dx}\right) - W\!\left(\tfrac{dZ}{dx} - \tfrac{dX}{dz}\right)\right\}
$}
\]
\[
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
+ \frac{d\phi}{dz}\!\left\{-W\!\left(\tfrac{dX}{dy} - \tfrac{dY}{dx}\right) - Y\!\left(\tfrac{dW}{dx} - \tfrac{dX}{dw}\right) + X\!\left(\tfrac{dW}{dy} - \tfrac{dY}{dw}\right)\right\}
+ \frac{d\phi}{dw}\!\left\{X\!\left(\tfrac{dY}{dz} - \tfrac{dZ}{dy}\right) + Y\!\left(\tfrac{dZ}{dx} - \tfrac{dX}{dz}\right) + Z\!\left(\tfrac{dX}{dy} - \tfrac{dY}{dx}\right)\right\}.
$}
\]

\[
\phi\psi 1234 = \phi\psi 12\,.\,34 + \phi\psi 13\,.\,42 + \phi\psi 14\,.\,23 + \phi\psi 34\,.\,12 + \phi\psi 42\,.\,13 + \phi\psi 23\,.\,14
\]
\[
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
= \frac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(x,y)}\!\left(\tfrac{dZ}{dw} - \tfrac{dW}{dz}\right) + \frac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(x,z)}\!\left(\tfrac{dW}{dy} - \tfrac{dY}{dw}\right) + \frac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(x,w)}\!\left(\tfrac{dY}{dz} - \tfrac{dZ}{dy}\right)
+ \frac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(z,w)}\!\left(\tfrac{dX}{dy} - \tfrac{dY}{dx}\right) + \frac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(y,w)}\!\left(\tfrac{dZ}{dx} - \tfrac{dX}{dz}\right) + \frac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(y,z)}\!\left(\tfrac{dW}{dx} - \tfrac{dX}{dw}\right).
$}
\]

\medskip
\textbf{6.} The invariantive property of the functions consists herein, viz.\ if we have
\[ Xdx + Ydy + Zdz + Wdw = Pdp + Qdq + Rdr + Sds, \]
so that $p$, $q$, $r$, $s$, and thence also $P$, $Q$, $R$, $S$ are functions each of them of $x$, $y$, $z$, $w$, then we have
\begin{align*}
1234\;\partial(x,y,z,w) &= (1234)'\,\partial(p,q,r,s),\\
\phi 1234\;\partial(x,y,z,w) &= (\phi 1234)'\,\partial(p,q,r,s),\\
\phi\psi 1234\;\partial(x,y,z,w) &= (\phi\psi 1234)'\,\partial(p,q,r,s),
\end{align*}
where the accented functions refer to $(p,q,r,s,P,Q,R,S)$, and where for greater symmetry I have separated the symbolical numerator and denominator $\partial(p,q,r,s)$ and $\partial(x,y,z,w)$; each of these equations really contains
\[ \frac{\partial(p,q,r,s)}{\partial(x,y,z,w)}, \]
which is the functional determinant $\dfrac{\partial(p,q,r,s)}{\partial(x,y,z,w)}$ in regard to $(x,y,z,w)$: or, if we please, it contains the reciprocal hereof
\[ \frac{\partial(x,y,z,w)}{\partial(p,q,r,s)}, \]
which is the functional determinant $\dfrac{\partial(x,y,z,w)}{\partial(p,q,r,s)}$ in regard to $(p,q,r,s)$.

\medskip
\textbf{7.} The equations give
\[ \frac{\phi 1234}{1234} = \frac{(\phi 1234)'}{(1234)'}, \]
\[ \frac{\phi\psi 1234}{1234} = \frac{(\phi\psi 1234)'}{(1234)'}, \]
and then the expressions on the left-hand are absolute invariants in respect to the transformation of
\[ Xdx + Ydy + Zdz + Wdw\;\;\text{into}\;\;Pdp + Qdq + Rdr + Sds. \]
They are, in fact, (for $2n=4$) Clebsch's derivatives $(\phi)$ and $(\phi,\psi)$.

\medskip
\textbf{8.} For the Pfaffian reduction
\[ Xdx + Ydy + Zdz + Wdw = Fdf + Gdg, \]
we may write
\[ P,\;Q,\;R,\;S = F,\;G,\;0,\;0, \]
\[ p,\;q,\;r,\;s = f,\;g,\;F,\;G, \]
viz.\ we take $f$, $g$, $F$, $G$ as the new independent variables; we thus have
\[ 01' = F,\quad 02' = G,\quad 03' = 0,\quad 04' = 0, \]
\[ 12' = 0,\;\; 13' = 1,\;\; 14' = 0,\;\; 23' = 0,\;\; 24' = 1,\;\; 34' = 0, \]
\[ (1234)' = 12'\,.\,34' + 13'\,.\,42' + 14'\,.\,23' = -1; \]
and similarly
\[ (\phi 1234)' = -\left\{F\,\tfrac{d\phi}{dF} + G\,\tfrac{d\phi}{dG}\right\}, \]
\[ (\phi\psi 1234)' = -\left\{\tfrac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(f,F)} + \tfrac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(g,G)}\right\}, \]
where, in the equations, the $-$ sign presents itself by reason that $2n$, $=4$, is the double of an even number, or say that $n$ is even; in the case of $2n$, the double of an odd number, that is, $n$ odd, the sign would have been $+$.

\medskip
\textbf{9.} We thus have
\[ (\phi) = \frac{\phi 1234}{1234} = F\,\frac{d\phi}{dF} + G\,\frac{d\phi}{dG}, \]
\[ (\phi,\psi) = \frac{\phi\psi 1234}{1234} = \frac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(f,F)} + \frac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(g,G)}, \]
and in particular, by giving to $\phi$ and $\psi$ the values $f$, $g$, $F$, $G$, we find
\[ (f) = 0,\;(g) = 0,\;(F) = F,\;(G) = G, \]
\[ (f,g) = 0,\;(f,F) = -F,\;(f,G) = 0,\;(g,F) = 0,\;(g,G) = -G,\;(F,G) = 1, \]
which are Clebsch's equations; in the case of $2n$ terms, the number of them is
\[ n + n + \tfrac{1}{2}(n^{2}-n) + \tfrac{1}{2}(n^{2}-n) + n^{2} = 2n + n^{2} - n + n^{2}, \]
$=n(2n+1)$, or $\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 2n(2n+1)$, as it should be.

\medskip
\textbf{10.} It may be remarked that we have
\[ Fdf + Gdg = Fd\!\left(f + \tfrac{G}{F}g\right) - Fgd\,\tfrac{G}{F}, \]
or writing this $= F'df' + G'dg'$, we have
\[ F' = F,\quad f' = f + \tfrac{Gg}{F},\quad G' = Fg,\quad g' = \tfrac{G}{F}, \]
whence conversely
\[ F = F',\quad f = f' - \tfrac{G'g'}{F'},\quad G = F'g',\quad g = \tfrac{G'}{F'},\quad (Gg = -G'g'). \]

The ten equations $(f) = 0$, $(g) = 0$, \&c., ought then to lead to the corresponding ten equations $(f') = 0$, $(g') = 0$, \&c., and it is easy to verify that they do so; for instance, we have
\[ (f') = \left(f + \tfrac{Gg}{F}\right) = (f) + \tfrac{1}{F}\,g\,(G) + g\left(\tfrac{G}{F}\right), \]
where
\[ g\left(\tfrac{G}{F}\right) = \tfrac{g}{F}(G) - \tfrac{Gg}{F^{2}}(F),\;= 0, \]
and thus $(f') = 0$. And again,
\[ (f',g') = \left(f + \tfrac{Gg}{F},\,\tfrac{G}{F}\right) = \left(f,\,\tfrac{G}{F}\right) + \left(\tfrac{Gg}{F},\,\tfrac{G}{F}\right) = \left(f,\,\tfrac{G}{F}\right) + \left(\tfrac{Gg}{F},\,\tfrac{G}{F}\right), \]
where the last term vanishes; the remaining terms are
\[ = \tfrac{1}{F}(f,G) - \tfrac{G}{F^{2}}(f,F),\;\text{that is,}\;\;-\tfrac{G}{F} + \tfrac{G}{F^{2}}\,F,\;= 0. \]

There is, of course, the like transformation
\[ Fdf + Gdg = Gd\!\left(g + \tfrac{F}{G}f\right) - Gfd\,\tfrac{F}{G}. \]

\medskip
\textbf{11.} I have, for better exhibiting the results, taken $2n=4$, but the most simple case for an even number of terms is $2n=2$. Here we have $Xdx + Ydy = Pdp + Qdq$, and the functions to be considered are
\begin{align*}
12 &= 12\\
\phi 12 &= \phi 0\,.\,12 + \phi 1\,.\,20 + \phi 2\,.\,01 = -Y\,\tfrac{d\phi}{dx} + X\,\tfrac{d\phi}{dy},\\
\phi\psi 12 &= \phi\psi 12.
\end{align*}
We have here
\[ X = P\,\tfrac{dp}{dx} + Q\,\tfrac{dq}{dx},\quad Y = P\,\tfrac{dp}{dy} + Q\,\tfrac{dq}{dy}, \]
and the invariantive properties are easily verified.

\medskip
\textbf{12.} Thus
\[ 12 = \tfrac{dX}{dy} - \tfrac{dY}{dx},\;=\!\left(\tfrac{dP}{dy}\,\tfrac{dp}{dx} + \tfrac{dP}{dx}\,\tfrac{dp}{dy}\right) + \left(\tfrac{dQ}{dy}\,\tfrac{dq}{dx} - \tfrac{dQ}{dx}\,\tfrac{dq}{dy}\right), \]
or, writing herein
\[ \tfrac{dP}{dx} = \tfrac{dP}{dp}\,\tfrac{dp}{dx} + \tfrac{dP}{dq}\,\tfrac{dq}{dx}, \]
and the like values for $\tfrac{dP}{dy}$ and for $\tfrac{dQ}{dx}$, $\tfrac{dQ}{dy}$, we have
\[ 12 = \!\left(\tfrac{dP}{dq} - \tfrac{dQ}{dp}\right)\!\left(\tfrac{dp}{dy}\,\tfrac{dq}{dx} - \tfrac{dp}{dx}\,\tfrac{dq}{dy}\right) = (12)'\,\tfrac{\partial(p,q)}{\partial(x,y)}. \]

Similarly, we find
\[ \phi 12 = -Y\,\tfrac{d\phi}{dx} + X\,\tfrac{d\phi}{dy} = \left(\tfrac{d\phi}{dp}\,\tfrac{dp}{dx} + \tfrac{d\phi}{dq}\,\tfrac{dq}{dx}\right) X + \cdots = (\phi 12)'\,\tfrac{\partial(p,q)}{\partial(x,y)}, \]
and
\[ \phi\psi 12 = \tfrac{d\phi}{dx}\,\tfrac{d\psi}{dy} - \tfrac{d\phi}{dy}\,\tfrac{d\psi}{dx} = \!\left(\tfrac{d\phi}{dp}\,\tfrac{d\psi}{dq} - \tfrac{d\phi}{dq}\,\tfrac{d\psi}{dp}\right)\!\left(\tfrac{dp}{dx}\,\tfrac{dq}{dy} - \tfrac{dp}{dy}\,\tfrac{dq}{dx}\right) = (\phi\psi 12)'\,\tfrac{\partial(p,q)}{\partial(x,y)}. \]

We thus have
\begin{align*}
12\;\partial(x,y) &= (12)'\,\partial(p,q),\\
\phi 12\;\partial(x,y) &= (\phi 12)'\,\partial(p,q),\\
\phi\psi 12\;\partial(x,y) &= (\phi\psi 12)'\,\partial(p,q),
\end{align*}
or say
\[ \tfrac{\phi 12}{12} = \tfrac{(\phi 12)'}{(12)'},\;\;\text{and}\;\;\tfrac{\phi\psi 12}{12} = \tfrac{(\phi\psi 12)'}{(12)'}. \]

The proof is the same in principle for $2n=4$, or any other even value.

\medskip
\textbf{13.} The theory is very similar in the case of an odd number $2n+1$ of terms; thus $2n+1=3$, the forms are
\[ 0123,\;\;\phi 123,\;\;\text{and}\;\;\phi\psi 0123, \]
the first and second of which are Pfaffians, the third of them co-Pfaffian: the developed expression of this last is
\[ \phi\psi 0123 = \phi\psi 01\,.\,23 + \phi\psi 02\,.\,31 + \phi\psi 03\,.\,12, \]
and to fix the meaning hereof we write
\[ \phi\psi 01 = 0,\quad \phi\psi 02 = 0,\quad \phi\psi 03 = 0. \]

Hence, the differential expression being $Xdx + Ydy + Zdz$, we have
\begin{align*}
0123 &= 01\,.\,23 + 02\,.\,31 + 03\,.\,12\\
&= X\!\left(\tfrac{dY}{dz} - \tfrac{dZ}{dy}\right) + Y\!\left(\tfrac{dZ}{dx} - \tfrac{dX}{dz}\right) + Z\!\left(\tfrac{dX}{dy} - \tfrac{dY}{dx}\right),\\[4pt]
\phi 123 &= \phi 1\,.\,23 + \phi 2\,.\,31 + \phi 3\,.\,12\\
&= \tfrac{d\phi}{dx}\!\left(\tfrac{dY}{dz} - \tfrac{dZ}{dy}\right) + \tfrac{d\phi}{dy}\!\left(\tfrac{dZ}{dx} - \tfrac{dX}{dz}\right) + \tfrac{d\phi}{dz}\!\left(\tfrac{dX}{dy} - \tfrac{dY}{dx}\right),\\[4pt]
\phi\psi 0123 &= \phi\psi 23\,.\,01 + \phi\psi 31\,.\,02 + \phi\psi 12\,.\,03\\
&= X\,\tfrac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(y,z)} + Y\,\tfrac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(z,x)} + Z\,\tfrac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(x,y)}.
\end{align*}

\medskip
\textbf{14.} For the transformation
\[ Xdx + Ydy + Zdz = Pdp + Qdq + Rdr, \]
we have
\begin{align*}
0123\,\partial(x,y,z) &= (0123)'\,\partial(p,q,r),\\
\phi 123\,\partial(x,y,z) &= (\phi 123)'\,\partial(p,q,r),\\
\phi\psi 0123\,\partial(x,y,z) &= (\phi\psi 0123)'\,\partial(p,q,r),
\end{align*}
and consequently
\[ \tfrac{\phi 123}{0123} = \tfrac{(\phi 123)'}{(0123)'},\quad \tfrac{\phi\psi 0123}{0123} = \tfrac{(\phi\psi 0123)'}{(0123)'}, \]
so that the left-hand functions are absolute invariants.

\medskip
\textbf{15.} If in particular, $Xdx + Ydy + Zdz = df + Gdg$, then we may write
\[ P,\;Q,\;R = 1,\;G,\;0, \]
\[ p,\;q,\;r = f,\;g,\;G. \]
Hence
\[ 01' = 1,\;\; 02' = G,\;\; 03' = 0;\;\; 23' = 1,\;\; 31' = 0,\;\; 12' = 0, \]
or say
\[ (0123)' = 1,\;\; (\phi 123)' = \tfrac{d\phi}{df},\;\; (\phi\psi 0123)' = \tfrac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(g,G)} + G\,\tfrac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(f,G)}, \]
and therefore
\[ 0123\,\partial(x,y,z) = \partial(f,g,G), \]
and we thus have
\[ \phi 123\,\partial(x,y,z) = \tfrac{d\phi}{df}\,\partial(f,g,G), \]
\[ \phi\psi 0123\,\partial(x,y,z) = \left[\tfrac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(g,G)} + G\,\tfrac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(f,G)}\right]\partial(f,g,G), \]
and thus
\[ (\phi) = \tfrac{\phi 123}{0123} = \tfrac{d\phi}{df}, \]
\[ (\phi,\psi) = \tfrac{\phi\psi 0123}{0123} = \tfrac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(g,G)} + G\,\tfrac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(f,G)}; \]
viz.\ we thus have derivatives $(\phi)$ and $(\phi,\psi)$ analogous to (but quite different in form from) those of Clebsch in the case of an even number of terms.

In particular, writing $\phi$, $\psi = f$, $g$, $G$, we obtain
\[ (f) = 1,\;\; (g) = 0,\;\; (G) = 0;\;\; (f,g) = 0,\;\; (f,G) = -G,\;\; (g,G) = 1, \]
which are the analogues of Clebsch's formul\ae.

\medskip
\textbf{16.} It is interesting to compare the formul\ae{} for the two cases
\[ Xdx + Ydy + Zdz + Wdw = Fdf + Gdg, \]
and
\[ Xdx + Ydy + Zdz = df + Gdg. \]

In the former case $f$ and $g$ are symmetrically related to each other, and we may say that ($f=$ const.\ and $g=$ const.) is a solution of $Xdx + Ydy + Zdz + Wdw = 0$; we have $(f) = 0$ and $(g) = 0$. In the second case ($f=$ const.\ and $g=$ const.) is still a solution of $Xdx + Ydy + Zdz = 0$, but $f$ and $g$ are not symmetrically related to each other, and we have $(f) = 1$, $(g) = 0$. Moreover, in the first case $(G) = G$, but in the second case $(G) = 0$, an equation of the same form as $(g) = 0$; the reason is that we have
\[ Xdx + Ydy + Zdz = df + Gdg,\;= d(f + Gg) - gdG, \]
so that, besides the solution ($f=$ const.\ and $g=$ const.), we have the solution
\[ (f + Gg = \text{const.\ and } G = \text{const.}). \]

\medskip
\textbf{17.} The remark just made may be further developed: we have
\[ Xdx + Ydy + Zdz = df + Gdg,\;= d(f + Gg) - gdG,\;= df' + G'dg', \]
suppose; where $f' = f + Gg$, $G' = -g$, $g' = G$, and therefore also $f = f' + G'g'$, $G = g'$, $g = -G'$: the equations
\[ (f) = 1,\;(g) = 0,\;(G) = 0,\;(f,g) = 0,\;(f,G) = -G,\;(g,G) = 1, \]
should lead to
\[ (f') = 1,\;(g') = 0,\;(G') = 0,\;(f',g') = 0,\;(f',G') = -G',\;(g',G') = 1. \]

There is no difficulty in verifying this; thus the equations $(g) = 0$, $(G) = 0$, give at once $(g') = 0$, $(G') = 0$; and then the equation $(f) = 1$ gives $(f' + G'g') = 1$, that is,
\[ (f') + g'(G') + G'(g') = 1,\;\text{or}\;(f') = 1. \]
So again $(g,G) = 1$ gives $(g,G') = 1$; and then $(f,g) = 0$ gives $(f' + G'g',\,G') = 0$, that is,
\[ (f',G') + G'(g',G') + g'(G',G') = 0,\;\text{or}\;(f',G') = -G'. \]
And finally, $(f,G) = -G$ gives $(f' + G'g',\,g') + g' = 0$, that is,
\[ (f',g') + G'(g',g') + g'(G',g') + g' = 0,\;\text{or}\;(f',g') = 0. \]

\medskip
I stop to give the direct verification of the equations $(f) = 1$, $(g) = 0$, $(G) = 0$. We have, as above,
\[ Xdx + Ydy + Zdz = df + Gdg, \]
that is,
\[ X = \tfrac{df}{dx} + G\,\tfrac{dg}{dx},\quad Y = \tfrac{df}{dy} + G\,\tfrac{dg}{dy},\quad Z = \tfrac{df}{dz} + G\,\tfrac{dg}{dz}, \]
and thence
\begin{align*}
23 &= \tfrac{dY}{dz} - \tfrac{dZ}{dy} = \tfrac{dG}{dz}\,\tfrac{dg}{dy} - \tfrac{dG}{dy}\,\tfrac{dg}{dz},\\
31 &= \tfrac{dZ}{dx} - \tfrac{dX}{dz} = \tfrac{dG}{dx}\,\tfrac{dg}{dz} - \tfrac{dG}{dz}\,\tfrac{dg}{dx},\\
12 &= \tfrac{dX}{dy} - \tfrac{dY}{dx} = \tfrac{dG}{dy}\,\tfrac{dg}{dx} - \tfrac{dG}{dx}\,\tfrac{dg}{dy}.
\end{align*}

Hence, multiplying first by $\tfrac{df}{dx}$, $\tfrac{df}{dy}$, $\tfrac{df}{dz}$, that is,
\[ X = \tfrac{df}{dx} + G\,\tfrac{dg}{dx},\;\;Y = \tfrac{df}{dy} + G\,\tfrac{dg}{dy},\;\;Z = \tfrac{df}{dz} + G\,\tfrac{dg}{dz}, \]
and adding, we have
\[ 23\,\tfrac{df}{dx} + 31\,\tfrac{df}{dy} + 12\,\tfrac{df}{dz} = X23 + Y31 + Z12, \]
that is, $\phi 123 = 0123$, or $(f) = 1$.

And then multiplying secondly by $\tfrac{dg}{dx}$, $\tfrac{dg}{dy}$, $\tfrac{dg}{dz}$ and adding, and thirdly by $\tfrac{dG}{dx}$, $\tfrac{dG}{dy}$, $\tfrac{dG}{dz}$ and adding, we obtain
\[ 23\,\tfrac{dg}{dx} + 31\,\tfrac{dg}{dy} + 12\,\tfrac{dg}{dz} = 0,\;\text{that is,}\;(g) = 0, \]
and
\[ 23\,\tfrac{dG}{dx} + 31\,\tfrac{dG}{dy} + 12\,\tfrac{dG}{dz} = 0,\;\text{that is,}\;(G) = 0. \]

To exhibit more clearly the formul\ae{} for any odd number of terms, I take $2n+1=5$,
\[ Xdx + Ydy + Zdz + Wdw + Tdt = df + Gdg + Hdh. \]

We have here
\[ (\phi) = \tfrac{\phi 12345}{012345} = \tfrac{d\phi}{df}, \]
\[ (\phi,\psi) = \tfrac{\phi\psi 012345}{012345} = \tfrac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(g,G)} + \tfrac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(h,H)} - G\,\tfrac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(f,G)} - H\,\tfrac{\partial(\phi,\psi)}{\partial(f,H)}; \]
and in particular,
\[ (f) = 1;\;\;(g) = 0,\;\;(h) = 0;\;\;(G) = 0,\;\;(H) = 0; \]
\[ (f,g) = 0,\;\;(f,h) = 0,\;\;(f,G) = -G,\;\;(f,H) = -H, \]
\[ (g,h) = 0,\;\;(G,H) = 0,\;\;(g,G) = 1,\;\;(g,H) = 0, \]
\[ (h,G) = 0,\;\;(h,H) = 1; \]

\medskip
in all
\[ 1 + 2n + 2n + \tfrac{1}{2}(n^{2}-n) + \tfrac{1}{2}(n^{2}-n) + n^{2} \]
\[ = 1 + 4n + n^{2} - n + n^{2},\; = 2n^{2} + 3n + 1,\; = \tfrac{1}{2}(2n+1)(2n+2) \]
equations.

\medskip
We can, by what precedes, at once express the conditions which must be satisfied in order that a differential expression $X_{1}dx_{1} + X_{2}dx_{2} + \cdots + X_{v}dx_{v}$, may be reducible to one of the special forms $df$, $Fdf$, $df + F_{1}df_{1}$, \&c.; viz.\ if we have
\begin{align*}
X_{1}dx_{1} + X_{2}dx_{2} + \cdots + X_{v}dx_{v} &= df,\quad \text{then}\;\;12 = 0,\;\&\text{c.}\\
 &= Fdf,\quad\; \text{,,}\;\;0123 = 0,\;\&\text{c.}\\
 &= df + F_{1}df_{1},\;\;\text{,,}\;\;1234 = 0,\;\&\text{c.}\\
 &= Fdf + F_{1}df_{1},\;\;\text{,,}\;\;012345 = 0,\;\&\text{c.}\\
 &\;\;\&\text{c.}
\end{align*}
where the numbers $12$, $1234$, $12345$, \&c., represent any combinations out of the numbers $1$, $2$, $3,\ldots,v$. Of course, if $v$ is not sufficiently large to furnish such a combination, then there is no condition to be satisfied; thus if
\[ X_{1}dx_{1} + X_{2}dx_{2} + X_{3}dx_{3} = df + F_{1}df_{1}, \]
there is no condition to be satisfied.

\begin{center}
\textbf{\large 945.}\\[4pt]
\textbf{NOTE ON LACUNARY FUNCTIONS.}\\[6pt]
[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.\ XXVI.\ (1893), pp.\ 279--281.]
\end{center}

\medskip
The present note is founded upon Poincar\'{e}'s paper ``Sur les fonctions \`{a} espaces lacunaires,'' \textit{Amer.\ Math.\ Jour.}, t.\ XIV.\ (1892), pp.\ 201--221.

If the complex variable $z = x + iy$ is represented as usual by a point the coordinates of which are $(x,y)$, and if $U_{0}$, $U_{1}$, $U_{2},\ldots$ denote an infinite series of given functions of $z$, then the equation
\[ fz = U_{0} + U_{1} + U_{2} + \cdots \]
defines a function of $z$, but only for those values of $z$ for which the series is convergent, or say for points within a certain region $\Theta$; and within this region, it defines the successive derived functions $f'z$, $f''z$, $f'''z,\ldots$

Taking $l$, $= h + ik$, as an increment of $z$, we define the function of $z + l$ by the equation
\[ f(z + l) = fz + \tfrac{l}{1}\,f'z + \tfrac{l^{2}}{1\,.\,2}\,f''z + \cdots, \]
but only for values of $l$ for which the series is convergent: it may very well be, and it is in general the case, that we thereby extend the definition of $fz$ so as to make it applicable to points within a larger region $\Theta_{1}$; and then considering $fz$ as defined within this larger region $\Theta_{1}$, we may pass from it to a still larger region $\Theta_{2}$; and so on indefinitely, or until we cover the whole infinite plane.

For instance, the equation
\[ fz = 1 + z + z^{2} + z^{3} + \cdots \]
defines the function $1 \div (1 - z)$ for values of $z$ for which mod.\ $z < 1$, that is, for points within the circle $x^{2} + y^{2} - 1 = 0$; and this being so,
\[ f(z+l) = \tfrac{1}{1 - z} + \tfrac{l}{(1 - z)^{2}} + \tfrac{l^{2}}{(1 - z)^{3}} + \cdots \]
extends the definition to the larger region for which this is a convergent series: the condition of convergency is
\[ \text{mod.}\,\tfrac{l}{1 - z} < 1,\;\text{that is, mod.}\,\tfrac{h + ik}{1 - x - iy} < 1,\;\text{or}\;h^{2} + k^{2} < (1 - x)^{2} + y^{2}. \]

The condition is that the distance $\sqrt{(h^{2} + k^{2})}$ must not exceed the distance $\sqrt{\{(1 - x)^{2} + y^{2}\}}$ of the point $z = x + iy$, from the point $(x = 1,\,y = 0)$; the point $z$ is strictly within the circle $x^{2} + y^{2} = 1$, but taking it on the circumference, the condition is that the point $z + l$ must lie within a circle having its centre on the circle $x^{2} + y^{2} - 1 = 0$ and passing through the point $(x = 1,\,y = 0)$. Taking $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ for the coordinates of the centre, the equation of this circle is
\[ (x - \cos\theta)^{2} + (y - \sin\theta)^{2} = (1 - \cos\theta)^{2} + \sin^{2}\theta, \]
that is,
\[ x^{2} + y^{2} - 1 - 2\cos\theta\,(x - 1) - 2y\sin\theta = 0; \]
and the envelope of these circles is
\[ (x^{2} + y^{2} - 1)^{2} - 4(x - 1)^{2} - 4y^{2} = 0, \]
or, as this may be written,
\[ (x^{2} + y^{2})^{2} - 6(x^{2} + y^{2}) + 8x - 3 = 0, \]
or in the form
\[ (x^{2} + y^{2} - 3)^{2} + 4(2x - 1) = 0, \]
\[ y^{4} + 2y^{2}(x^{2} - 3) + (x - 1)^{2}(x + 3) = 0. \]

The curve is a cuspidal Cartesian. To put this in evidence, observe that the equation may be written
\[ -4y^{2} + \{(x - 1)^{2} + y^{2}\}\{(x - 1)^{2} + y^{2} + 4x - 4\} = 0, \]
viz.\ writing
\[ A = x + iy - 1, \]
\[ B = x - iy - 1, \]
\[ Z = -1, \]
then
\begin{align*}
A - B &= 2iy,\\
AB &= x^{2} - 1 + y^{2},\\
A + B &= 2x - 2,
\end{align*}
or the equation is
\[ Z^{2}(A - B)^{2} + AB\{AB - 2Z(A + B)\} = 0, \]
that is,
\[ Z^{2}A^{2} + Z^{2}B^{2} + A^{2}B^{2} - 2Z^{2}AB - 2ZA^{2}B - 2ZAB^{2} = 0, \]
which is the equation of a bicuspidal quartic curve, having for cusps the vertices of the triangle $A = 0$, $B = 0$, $Z = 0$.

The region within which the function is $= \tfrac{1}{1 - z}$ is thus extended to the area within the Cartesian curve, say this is the region $\Theta_{1}$; starting from this curve instead of the circle (viz.\ by considering the envelope of the circle having its centre on the curve and passing through the point $x = 1$, $y = 0$), we obtain a second curve, a closed curve, which instead of having a cusp on the axis of $x$ cuts this axis at right angles at a point the distance of which from the origin is greater than 1; and we thus extend the region to the area within this second curve, say this is the region $\Theta_{2}$. And proceeding in this way, we ultimately extend the region to the whole of the infinite plane.

But the functions $U_{0}$, $U_{1}$, $U_{2},\ldots$ may be such that for every value whatever of $l$, for which the point $z + l$ is outside the region $\Theta$, the series
\[ fz + \tfrac{l}{1}\,f'z + \tfrac{l^{2}}{1\,.\,2}\,f''z + \cdots \]
is divergent, and we are in this case unable to define the function $fz$ for points outside the region $\Theta$; the function then exists only for points inside the region $\Theta$, and for points outside this region it is non-existent; a function such as this, existing only for points within a certain region and not for the whole of the infinite plane, is said to be a lacunary function.

\begin{center}
\textbf{\large 946.}\\[4pt]
\textbf{NOTE ON THE THEORY OF ORTHOMORPHOSIS.}\\[6pt]
[From the \textit{Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics}, vol.\ XXVI.\ (1893), pp.\ 282--288.]

\medskip
The equation of any given curve whatever, $\Omega = 0$, may be expressed in the form
\[ \phi(x + iy) + \phi(x - iy) = 0. \]

Let $\chi$ be any odd function; then since
\[ \phi(x - iy) = -\phi(x + iy), \]
we have
\[ \chi\phi(x - iy) = \chi\{-\phi(x + iy)\} = -\chi\phi(x + iy), \]
that is,
\[ \chi\phi(x + iy) + \chi\phi(x - iy) = 0. \]

Assuming that $\Omega$ is a real function, that is, a function with real coefficients, then also $\phi(x + iy)$ will be a function with real coefficients, or say a real function of $x + iy$; the function $\chi$ may be real or imaginary, but if imaginary, then the $i$ of the coefficients does not change its sign in the passage from $\chi\phi(x + iy)$ to $\chi\phi(x - iy)$.

In proof of the assumed theorem, imagine the equation $\Omega = 0$ expressed as an equation between $x + iy$ and $x - iy$, or, supposing it solved in regard to $x - iy$, take the form of it to be $x - iy = f(x + iy)$: let $u_{n}$ be a function of $n$ satisfying the equation of differences $u_{n+1} = fu_{n}$; and let $\phi(x + iy)$ be determined as a function of $x + iy$ by the elimination of $n$ from the equations
\[ x + iy = u_{n},\quad \phi(x + iy) = \cos n\pi; \]
we thence have
\[ x - iy = fu_{n},\;= u_{n+1}, \]
and consequently
\[ \phi(x - iy) = \cos(n + 1)\pi, \]
that is,
\[ \phi(x + iy) + \phi(x - iy) = 0, \]
viz.\ this equation is a transformation of the equation $\Omega = 0$, and thus it appears that the equation $\Omega = 0$ can always be thrown into the last-mentioned form.

As an example, take the equation $y = ax + b$: which, putting for a moment $\xi = x + iy$, $\eta = x - iy$, is
\[ \tfrac{1}{2i}(\xi - \eta) = \tfrac{1}{2}a(\xi + \eta) + b, \]
that is,
\[ \eta = \tfrac{i + a}{i - a}\,\xi + \tfrac{2b}{i - a}; \]
we have therefore
\[ u_{n+1} = \tfrac{i + a}{i - a}\,u_{n} + \tfrac{2b}{i - a}, \]
a solution of which is
\[ u_{n} = \left(\tfrac{i + a}{i - a}\right)^{n} - \tfrac{b}{a}; \]
putting this $= \xi$, we have
\[ n = \tfrac{1}{\log\tfrac{i + a}{i - a}}\log\!\left(\xi + \tfrac{b}{a}\right), \]
and thence
\[ \phi\xi = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{\log\tfrac{i + a}{i - a}}\log\!\left(\xi + \tfrac{b}{a}\right), \]
where observe that, writing $a + i = Re^{i\alpha}$ and therefore $a - i = Re^{-i\alpha}$, we have
\[ \cos\alpha = \tfrac{a}{\sqrt{(a^{2} + 1)}},\quad \sin\alpha = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{(a^{2} + 1)}}, \]
or say $\cot\alpha = a$, and then
\[ \tfrac{i + a}{i - a} = e^{2\alpha i},\;\text{or}\;\log\tfrac{i + a}{i - a} = i(2\alpha + \pi), \]
whence
\[ \phi\xi = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{i(2\alpha + \pi)}\log\!\left(\xi + \tfrac{b}{a}\right),\;= \cos\tfrac{\pi}{2\alpha + \pi}\log\!\left(\xi + \tfrac{b}{a}\right), \]
a real function of $\xi$.

In verification of the equation $\phi\xi + \phi\eta = 0$, we have
\[ \phi\eta = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{\log\tfrac{i + a}{i - a}}\log\!\left(\eta + \tfrac{b}{a}\right), \]
where
\[ \log\!\left(\eta + \tfrac{b}{a}\right) = \log\!\left(\tfrac{i + a}{i - a}\,\xi + \tfrac{2b}{i - a} + \tfrac{b}{a}\right) = \log\tfrac{i + a}{i - a}\!\left(\xi + \tfrac{b}{a}\right) = \log\tfrac{i + a}{i - a} + \log\!\left(\xi + \tfrac{b}{a}\right), \]
and thence
\[ \phi\eta = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{\log\tfrac{i + a}{i - a}}\!\left\{\log\tfrac{i + a}{i - a} + \log\!\left(\xi + \tfrac{b}{a}\right)\right\} \]
\[ = \cos\!\left\{\pi + \tfrac{\pi}{\log\tfrac{i + a}{i - a}}\log\!\left(\xi + \tfrac{b}{a}\right)\right\} = -\cos\tfrac{\pi}{\log\tfrac{i + a}{i - a}}\log\!\left(\xi + \tfrac{b}{a}\right), \]
that is, $\phi\eta = -\phi\xi$, or $\phi\xi + \phi\eta = 0$, the equation in question.

I remark, in passing, that the same equation $y = ax + b$ might have been put in the form $\phi x + \phi y = 0$, viz.\ assuming
\[ \phi x = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{\log a}\log\!\left(x - \tfrac{b}{1 - a}\right), \]
then
\[ \phi y = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{\log a}\log\!\left(ax + b - \tfrac{b}{1 - a}\right) = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{\log a}\log a\!\left(x - \tfrac{b}{1 - a}\right) \]
\[ = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{\log a}\!\left\{\log a + \log\!\left(x - \tfrac{b}{1 - a}\right)\right\} = \cos\!\left\{\pi + \tfrac{\pi}{\log a}\log\!\left(x - \tfrac{b}{1 - a}\right)\right\} \]
\[ = -\cos\tfrac{\pi}{\log a}\log\!\left(x - \tfrac{b}{1 - a}\right),\; = -\phi x, \]
that is, $\phi x + \phi y = 0$.

If $b = 0$, then
\[ y = ax\;\;\text{and}\;\;\phi x = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{\log a}\log x; \]
in fact, repeating the proof for this particular case,
\[ \phi y = \cos\tfrac{\pi\log ax}{\log a} = \cos\pi\!\left(1 + \tfrac{\log x}{\log a}\right) = -\cos\tfrac{\pi\log x}{\log a},\; = -\phi x, \]
that is,
\[ \phi x + \phi y = 0. \]

Considering then $(x,y)$ as the coordinates of a point on the curve $\Omega = 0$, we have, as above,
\[ \chi\phi(x + iy) + \chi\phi(x - iy) = 0, \]
where $\phi$ is a real function determined as above, and $\chi$ is any real or imaginary odd function. This being so, assume
\[ x_{1} + iy_{1} = e^{\chi\phi(x + iy)}, \]
then also
\[ x_{1} - iy_{1} = e^{\chi\phi(x - iy)}, \]
and consequently
\[ x_{1}^{2} + y_{1}^{2} = e^{\chi\phi(x + iy) + \chi\phi(x - iy)} = 1, \]
that is, we have the circumference of the circle $x_{1}^{2} + y_{1}^{2} - 1 = 0$ corresponding to the given curve $\Omega = 0$.

Suppose that the curve $\Omega = 0$ is a closed curve: and then writing
\[ \xi + i\eta = \chi\phi(x + iy), \]
and therefore
\[ \xi - i\eta = \chi\phi(x - iy), \]
we thence have
\[ 2\xi = \chi\phi(x + iy) + \chi\phi(x - iy), \]
a real function of $(x,y)$.

\textbf{(1)} Assume that it is possible to find $\chi$, such that $\xi$ as defined by this last equation shall be throughout the area of the curve $\Omega = 0$ finite and continuous, except only that in the neighbourhood of a given point, taken to be the point $x = 0$, $y = 0$, it is $= \log\sqrt{(x^{2} + y^{2})}$.

\textbf{(2)} At the boundary of the area $\Omega = 0$, $\xi$ is $= 0$.

\textbf{(3)} Throughout the area, $\xi$ satisfies the partial differential equation
\[ \tfrac{d^{2}\xi}{dx^{2}} + \tfrac{d^{2}\xi}{dy^{2}} = 0. \]

These conditions being satisfied, the equation
\[ x_{1} + iy_{1} = e^{\xi + i\eta}, \]
that is,
\[ x_{1} + iy_{1} = e^{\chi\phi(x + iy)}, \]
gives an orthomorphosis of the area $\Omega = 0$ into the circle $x_{1}^{2} + y_{1}^{2} - 1 = 0$, the point $x = 0$, $y = 0$ corresponding to the centre of the circle; (2) and (3) are satisfied as above: it remains only to satisfy (1), viz.\ the function $\chi$ is determined not by any equation, but only by this condition as to finiteness and continuity; and if it be thus determined, then the foregoing equation $x_{1} + iy_{1} = e^{\chi\phi(x + iy)}$ gives the required orthomorphosis.

For instance, let the curve $\Omega = 0$ be the parabola $y^{2} = 4(1 - x)$, which may be regarded as a closed curve bounding the infinite parabolic area. We have $2x = \xi + \eta$, $2iy = \xi - \eta$, whence the equation is
\[ -\tfrac{1}{4}(\xi - \eta)^{2} = 4 - 2\xi - 2\eta, \]
that is,
\[ \xi^{2} - 2\xi\eta + \eta^{2} - 8\xi - 8\eta + 16 = 0, \]
whence $\sqrt{\xi} + \sqrt{\eta} - 2 = 0$, or writing this in the form
\[ \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\xi} + \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\eta} - 1 = 0, \]
we have $\phi\xi = \sqrt{\xi} - 1$, and assuming that $\chi$ can be found so that the condition as to finiteness and continuity is satisfied, then the orthomorphosis is given by
\[ x_{1} + iy_{1} = \exp\chi(\sqrt{\xi} - 1),\;=\exp\chi\{\sqrt{(x + iy)} - 1\}. \]

Assuming
\[ \chi\omega = \tfrac{1}{2}i\pi\omega + \log\tfrac{1 - i\exp\tfrac{1}{2}i\pi\omega}{1 - i\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}i\pi\omega\right)}, \]
which is obviously an odd function, we have
\[ \exp\chi\omega = \exp\tfrac{1}{2}i\pi\omega\cdot\tfrac{1 - i\exp\tfrac{1}{2}i\pi\omega}{1 - i\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}i\pi\omega\right)} = \tfrac{\exp\tfrac{1}{2}i\pi\omega - i\exp\,i\pi\omega}{1 - i\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}i\pi\omega\right)} \]
\[ = \tfrac{\exp\tfrac{1}{2}i\pi\omega(1 - i\exp\tfrac{1}{2}i\pi\omega)}{1 - i\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}i\pi\omega\right)}, \]
which is
\[ = \tan^{2}\tfrac{1}{4}\pi(\omega + 1), \]
and hence, for $\omega$ writing $\sqrt{(x + iy)} - 1$, we have
\[ x_{1} + iy_{1} = \exp\chi\{\sqrt{(x + iy)} - 1\},\;= \tan^{2}\tfrac{1}{4}\pi\sqrt{(x + iy)}. \]

This satisfies the required conditions as to finiteness and continuity; and in particular, we have
\[ \xi + i\eta = \log\tan^{2}\tfrac{1}{4}\pi\sqrt{(x + iy)}, \]
so that, $x$ and $y$ being small,
\[ \xi + i\eta = \log\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{16}(x + iy),\;\;\xi - i\eta = \log\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{16}(x - iy), \]
that is,
\[ \xi = \log\tfrac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{(x^{2} + y^{2})}. \]

Hence we have the known result: the orthomorphosis of the parabola $y^{2} = 4(1 - x)$ into the circle $x_{1}^{2} + y_{1}^{2} - 1 = 0$ is given by the equation $x_{1} + iy_{1} = \tan^{2}\tfrac{1}{4}\pi\sqrt{(x + iy)}$.

Consider the ellipse, where $a^{2} - b^{2} = 1$, or say
\[ \tfrac{x^{2}}{\tfrac{1}{4}(M + \tfrac{1}{M})^{2}} + \tfrac{y^{2}}{\tfrac{1}{4}(M - \tfrac{1}{M})^{2}} = 1. \]

I show, by a less direct process, how to express this equation in the required form $\phi\xi + \phi\eta = 0$. In fact, writing
\[ \xi = x + iy,\quad \eta = x - iy, \]
the equation of the ellipse is the rationalised form of
\[ i\eta + \sqrt{(1 - \eta^{2})} = M^{2}\{i\xi + \sqrt{(1 - \xi^{2})}\}. \]

To show that this is so, call for a moment the right-hand side $\Omega$, the equation is
\[ \sqrt{(1 - \eta^{2})} = \Omega - i\eta, \]
hence
\[ 1 - \eta^{2} = \Omega^{2} - 2\Omega i\eta - \eta^{2}, \]
\[ 2\Omega i\eta = \Omega^{2} - 1, \]
or
\[ 2i\eta = \Omega - \tfrac{1}{\Omega} = M^{2}\{i\xi + \sqrt{(1 - \xi^{2})}\} - \tfrac{1}{M^{2}}\{i\xi + \sqrt{(1 - \xi^{2})}\}^{-1}, \]
therefore
\[ 2i\eta = \Omega - \tfrac{1}{\Omega} = M^{2}\{i\xi + \sqrt{(1 - \xi^{2})}\} + \tfrac{1}{M^{2}}\{i\xi - \sqrt{(1 - \xi^{2})}\}, \]
that is,
\[ -4\eta^{2} = -4\xi^{2}\!\left(M^{2} + \tfrac{1}{M^{2}}\right)\xi^{2} - \!\left(M^{2} - \tfrac{1}{M^{2}}\right)^{2}, \]
or say
\[ -\xi^{2} - \eta^{2} + \!\left(M^{2} + \tfrac{1}{M^{2}}\right)\xi\eta - \tfrac{1}{4}\!\left(M^{2} - \tfrac{1}{M^{2}}\right)^{2} = 0; \]
viz.\ substituting for $\xi$, $\eta$ their values, this is
\[ -2(x^{2} - y^{2}) + \!\left(M^{2} + \tfrac{1}{M^{2}}\right)(x^{2} + y^{2}) - \tfrac{1}{4}\!\left(M^{2} - \tfrac{1}{M^{2}}\right)^{2} = 0, \]
that is,
\[ \!\left(M - \tfrac{1}{M}\right)^{2}x^{2} + \!\left(M + \tfrac{1}{M}\right)^{2}y^{2} - \tfrac{1}{4}\!\left(M^{2} - \tfrac{1}{M^{2}}\right)^{2} = 0, \]
or finally, it is
\[ \tfrac{x^{2}}{\tfrac{1}{4}\!\left(M + \tfrac{1}{M}\right)^{2}} + \tfrac{y^{2}}{\tfrac{1}{4}\!\left(M - \tfrac{1}{M}\right)^{2}} - 1 = 0, \]
as it should be.

Starting then from the relation
\[ i\eta + \sqrt{(1 - \eta^{2})} = M^{2}\{i\xi + \sqrt{(1 - \xi^{2})}\}, \]
and writing
\[ \phi\xi = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{2\log M}\log\{i\xi + \sqrt{(1 - \xi^{2})}\}, \]
we have
\[ \phi\eta = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{2\log M}\log\{i\eta + \sqrt{(1 - \eta^{2})}\} \]
\[ = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{2\log M}\!\left[\log M^{2} + \log\{i\xi + \sqrt{(1 - \xi^{2})}\}\right] \]
\[ = \cos\!\left[\pi + \tfrac{\pi}{2\log M}\log\{i\xi + \sqrt{(1 - \xi^{2})}\}\right] \]
\[ = -\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2\log M}\log\{i\xi + \sqrt{(1 - \xi^{2})}\},\; = -\phi\xi, \]
that is, we have
\[ \phi\xi + \phi\eta = 0, \]
as the required transformation of the equation of the ellipse
\[ \tfrac{x^{2}}{\tfrac{1}{4}\!\left(M + \tfrac{1}{M}\right)^{2}} + \tfrac{y^{2}}{\tfrac{1}{4}\!\left(M - \tfrac{1}{M}\right)^{2}} = 1. \]

We hence derive the known formula for the orthomorphosis of the ellipse into the circle $x_{1}^{2} + y_{1}^{2} - 1 = 0$.
