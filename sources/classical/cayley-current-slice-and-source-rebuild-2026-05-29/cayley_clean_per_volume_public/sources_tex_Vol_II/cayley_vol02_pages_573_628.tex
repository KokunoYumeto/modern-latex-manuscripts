\section*{157.\quad On the Tangential of a Cubic.}

\begin{flushleft}
\small
[From the \textit{Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London}, vol.~XLVIII.
for the year 1858, pp.~461---463. Received February 11,---Read March 18, 1858.]
\end{flushleft}

In my ``Memoir on Curves of the Third Order''\footnote{Philosophical Transactions, vol.~cxlvii.~(1857), [146].}, I had occasion to consider a
derivative which may be termed the ``tangential'' of a cubic, viz. the tangent at
the point $(x, y, z)$ of the cubic curve $(\ast\!\backslash x, y, z)^3 = 0$ meets the curve in a point
$(\xi, \eta, \zeta)$ which is the tangential of the first-mentioned point; and I showed that when
the cubic is represented in the canonical form $x^3+y^3+z^3+6lxyz=0$, the coordinates of
the tangential may be taken to be $x(y^2-z^2) : y(z^2-x^2) : z(x^2-y^2)$. The method given for
obtaining the tangential may be applied to the general form $(a,b,c,f,g,h,i,j,k,l\!\backslash x,y,z)^3=0$;
it seems desirable, in reference to the theory of cubic forms, to give the expression of
the tangential for the general form; and this is what I propose to do, merely indicating
the steps of the calculation, which was performed for me by Mr Greedy.

The cubic form is
\[
(a,b,c,f,g,h,i,j,k,l\!\backslash x,y,z)^3,
\]
which means
\[
ax^3+by^3+cz^3+3fyz^2+3gz^2x+3hz^2y+3izx^2+3jx^2y+3kxy^2+3ly^2z;
\]
and the expression for $\xi$ is obtained from the equation
\[
a x^2 \xi = (j,f,i,c\!\backslash x,y,z)^3 - (h,b,i,f,l,k\!\backslash x,y,z)^3 -(a,b,c,f,g,h,i,j,k,l\!\backslash x,y,z)^3 (\xi x+\eta y+\zeta z),
\]
where the second line is in fact equal to zero, on account of the first factor, which
vanishes. And $\mathfrak{C}$, $\mathfrak{D}$ denote respectively quadric and cubic functions of $(y, z)$, which
are to be determined so as to make the right-hand side divisible by $x^2$; the resulting
value of $\xi$ may be modified by the adjunction of the evanescent term
\[
(\alpha x+\beta y+\gamma z)(a,b,c,f,g,h,i,j,k,l\!\backslash x,y,z)^3,
\]
where $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$ are arbitrary coefficients; but as it is not obvious how these coefficients
should be determined in order to present the result in the most simple form, I have
given the result in the form in which it was obtained without the adjunction of any
such term.

Write for shortness,
\begin{align*}
P &= (k,l\!\backslash y,z), & Q &= (b,f,i\!\backslash y,z)^2,\\
R &= (l,g\!\backslash y,z), & S &= (f,i,c\!\backslash y,z)^2,\\
\mathfrak{B} &= (h,j\!\backslash y,z), & \mathfrak{C} &= (k,l,g\!\backslash y,z)^2,\\
\mathfrak{D} &= (h,f,i,c\!\backslash y,z)^3, &&
\end{align*}
so that
\begin{align*}
(h,b,i,f,l,k\!\backslash x,y,z)^3 &= (h,P,Q\!\backslash x,1)^3,\\
(j,f,i,c,g,l\!\backslash x,y,z)^3 &= (j,R,S\!\backslash x,1)^3,\\
(a,b,c,f,g,h,i,j,k,l\!\backslash x,y,z)^3 &= (a,\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D}\!\backslash x,1)^3,\\
\xi x+\eta y+\zeta z &= (\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B}\!\backslash x,1),
\end{align*}
and then for greater convenience writing $(h,2P,Q\!\backslash x,1)$, \&c.\ for $(h,P,Q\!\backslash x,1)$, \&c.,
and omitting the $(x,1)^3$, \&c.\ and the arrow-heads, or representing the functions simply
by $(h,2P,Q)$, \&c., we have
\begin{multline*}
a x^2 \xi = c(j,2R,S)^3 - 3f(j,2R,S)^2\cdot(h,2P,Q) \\
+ 3i(j,2R,S)\cdot(h,2P,Q)^2 - c\cdot(h,2P,Q)^3 - (a,3\mathfrak{B},3\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})\cdot(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B}),
\end{multline*}
which can be developed in terms of the quantities which enter into it. The con-
ditions, in order that the coefficients of $x$, $x^0$ may vanish, are thus seen to be
\begin{align*}
\mathfrak{D}\mathfrak{A} &= cS^3 - 3fS^2Q + 3iSQ^2 - cQ^3,\\
\mathfrak{D}\mathfrak{B} - 3\mathfrak{C}\mathfrak{A} &= b(6RS^2) - 3f(2S^2P+4RSQ) + 3i(2RQ^2+4SPQ) - c(6P^2Q),
\end{align*}
and from these we obtain
\begin{align*}
\mathfrak{A} &= \{ch^2-3fig+cgi+3fhj-2ci^2\}(y,z)^4,\\
\mathfrak{B} &= \{-3bfj+bgi+3bch-2bk^2\}(y,z)^3,
\end{align*}

\section*{158.\quad A Sixth Memoir upon Quantics.}

\begin{flushleft}
\small
[From the \textit{Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London}, vol.~CXLIX.
for the year 1859, pp.~61---90. Received November 18, 1858,---Read January 6, 1859.]
\end{flushleft}

I \textsc{propose} in the present memoir to consider the geometrical theory: I have
alluded to this part of the subject in the articles Nos.~3 and 4 of the Introductory
Memoir, [139]. The present memoir relates to the geometry of one dimension and the
geometry of two dimensions, corresponding respectively to the analytical theories of
binary and ternary quantics. But the theory of binary quantics is considered for its
own sake; the geometry of one dimension is so immediate an interpretation of the
theory of binary quantics, that for its own sake there is no necessity to consider it at
all; it is considered with a view to the geometry of two dimensions. A chief object
of the present memoir is the establishment, upon purely descriptive principles, of the
notion of distance. I had intended in this introductory part to make a reference to
Mr W.~R.~Hamilton's memoir, ``On Symbolical Geometry,'' \textit{Camb.~and Dubl.~Math.~Jour.}
t.~iv.~(1849), pp.~124---137, but I am unable to do so: in fact, without at all under-
taking to understand the principle or philosophy of the author's symbolical method, I
find that the notion of distance enters into his definitions of the symbolical imaginaries
$i$, $j$, $k$ (see p.~128). The memoir contains (p.~135) a ``construction for a fourth pro-
portional to three given lines in the same plane,'' which it is proper to notice is the
construction already given by M.~Chasles for the product of two lines, \textit{Comptes Rendus,}
t.~xxxv.\ (1852), p.~292. See also the memoir by the same author, ``Sur la construction
de la notion de distances dans la g\'{e}om\'{e}trie descriptive'' (see in particular No.~2, p.~757,
Sur les Multiplications, \&c.), \textit{Comptes Rendus,} t.~xliii.\ (1856), pp.~753---759; and the memoir
by M.~Cayley (the author of the present memoir), ``Sur la G\'{e}om\'{e}trie de la distance
g\'{e}n\'{e}rale,'' \textit{Comptes Rendus,} t.~xlvii.\ (1858), pp.~489---492, the references in which last-
mentioned memoir require to be completed as follows:---The theory of the anharmonic
ratio, including the theorem that a pencil of four lines and the transversal have the
same anharmonic ratio, is due to M.~Chasles (\textit{Comptes Rendus,} t.~xxi.\ (1845), p.~383),---
the theorem that an involution of points gives rise to a pencil in involution, and the
theory of poles and polars, including the theorem of the anharmonic property of the
four points formed by a variable point on a conic with the four foci, and including the
definition of a circle by means of the two imaginary foci, are due to M.~Chasles (\textit{Comptes}
\textit{Rendus,} t.~xxx.\ (1850), p.~326, and t.~xxxii.\ (1851), p.~560); the theorem of the anharmonic
property of the foci of a conic was in fact demonstrated by Mr J.~T.~Graves in the year
1850 (see \textit{Philosophical Magazine}, February, 1850); the theorem of the pencil in involu-
tion is due to Mr Kirkman, \textit{Philosophical Transactions,} 1849, p.~83; the definition of a
circle by means of the two circular points at infinity is to be found in Poncelet's
\textit{Trait\'{e} des Propri\'{e}t\'{e}s Projectives,} [1822], p.~371.

The foregoing extracts show, and it is besides obvious, that the theory of distance
generalizes the ordinary theory of similar figures, by substituting for the Absolute, viz.
the two circular points at infinity, a conic (the Absolute) lying in the plane or other
space of two dimensions: the generalized notion of distance is applicable not only to
ordinary plane geometry, but to descriptive geometry, and to geometry in a superficies.
The leading idea is as old as Desargues: thus in the theorem that six points in a conic
have a single anharmonic ratio, Desargues considered the conic as a conic of the figure;
the notion is very explicitly contained in a posthumous memoir of Lagrange, ``Solution
de quelques probl\`{e}mes d'astronomie sph\'{e}rique par le moyen des analogies,'' \textit{OEuvres de}
\textit{Lagrange,} t.~iii.\ pp.~331---354, see p.~351, Nos.~27---30: in particular, considering on a
sphere a triangle, the sides whereof are arcs of great circles, and substituting for the
angles at the centre (these being the distances) the trigonometrical functions (the ratios
of the coordinates to the distance from the centre), or, as we should now say, projecting
on the osculating plane of the point which is the pole of the hemisphere, the sides of the
spherical triangle become lines in a plane; and instead of the spherical triangle we have
the projection thereof on the osculating plane; and Lagrange expressly remarks that the
formul\ae\ so obtained are in fact those of plane trigonometry. The memoir just referred
to may be considered as containing the germ at least of the notion of distance.

\medskip
\textbf{On Geometry of One Dimension, Nos.~149 to 168.}

\textbf{149.} In geometry of one dimension we have the line as a space or locus in
quo of the points of the figure; a point is determined by means of its coordinate
ratio $(x:y)$, and a figure whatever consists of a system of points, in general distinct
from each other. The coordinates $(x,y)$ may be considered as denoting two given
points of the line, or as the multiples of the distances of the variable point from these
points; but it is not necessary to do so; the coordinate ratio is all that it is necessary
to attend to. Any function whatever of the coordinates, equated to zero, is an equa-
tion of the figure; but only those equations which are homogeneous in regard to the
coordinates are equations of any figure; or if the equation is not homogeneous, then it
must be capable of being made so by the adjunction of a proper constant. The order
of a figure is the order of its equation in regard to the coordinates.

\textbf{150.} A linear equation $(a,b\!\backslash x,y)^1=0$ denotes a single point; the single point
may be considered as a point-pair, the two points of which coincide together.

\textbf{151.} A quadric equation $(a,b,c\!\backslash x,y)^2=0$ denotes a pair of points. The point
$(a,b\!\backslash x,y)^1=0$ is in a certain sense the harmonic of either of the points of the point-pair
in respect to the other; but as the harmonic of a point in respect to itself and another
point is altogether indefinite, it is proper to speak of the point as the centre or mid-point
of the point-pair, and to denote it as such by the centre of the point-pair; the notion
of the mid-point has reference to the theory of distance which will be explained in the
sequel.

\textbf{152.} A system of points, the number of which is equal to the order, is a harmonic
system, if the several points are the centres of the point-pairs formed by the remaining
points; thus a point-pair is always a harmonic system of two points; a system of
three points cannot be a harmonic system; and a system of four points is a harmonic
system, when each point of it is the centre of the remaining point-pair.

\textbf{153.} In geometry of one dimension the Absolute is a pair of points; any point
whatever and the Absolute determine a point-pair, and the centre of this point-pair is
the harmonic of the point in respect to the Absolute; or more simply, the harmonic of
the point in respect to the Absolute is the point which is the harmonic of the first point
in respect to the two points of the Absolute. The harmonic of a point in respect to the
Absolute may also be termed the opposite point. The Absolute may be real, or it may
consist of a pair of conjugate imaginary points. The equation of the Absolute may
always be taken to be $x^2+y^2=0$, or, what is the same thing, $(1,0,1\!\backslash x,y)^2=0$.

\textbf{154.} Taking the equation of the Absolute to be $x^2+y^2=0$, the harmonic of
the point $(x,y)$ in respect to the Absolute is the point $(x,-y)$. Any two points
$(x,y)$ and $(x',y')$ determine a point-pair, the centre of which is the point
$xx'+yy'$, $xy'-x'y$; and if the coordinates of the first point are $(x,y)$ and those of the
second point are $(x',y')$, then the distance between the two points is
\[
\sin^{-1}\frac{xy'-x'y}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\sqrt{x'^2+y'^2}};
\]
or what is the same thing, the distance is
\[
\cos^{-1}\frac{xx'+yy'}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\sqrt{x'^2+y'^2}}.
\]

\textbf{155.} It follows at once, from the sine formula, that the distance of a point
from itself is always $=0$; and from the cosine formula, that the distance of the point
$(x,y)$ from the point $(x,-y)$ is $=\pi$; and from the sine formula, that the distance
of either of the points of the Absolute from any point whatever is $=0$; and from the
cosine formula, that the distance of either of the points of the Absolute from any point
whatever is $=\frac{\pi}{2}$.

\textbf{156.} The quadrant or distance of either of the points of the Absolute from any
other point, is the unit of distance; but since either of the points of the Absolute is at
a distance $=0$ from itself, there is here a certain apparent inconsistency. It may be
observed, that in the case where the Absolute is a pair of imaginary points, the unit of
distance is imaginary.

\textbf{157.} A lineo-linear equation
\[
(a,b,c\!\backslash x,y)^2(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\!\backslash x',y')^2-(ac-b^2)(xy'-x'y)^2=0
\]
denotes that the distance of the points $(x,y)$ and $(x',y')$ is a given constant; for this
equation is equivalent to
\[
\cos^{-1}\frac{xx'+yy'}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\sqrt{x'^2+y'^2}}=\text{const.}
\]
The curve (the only curve in geometry of one dimension) represented by the above
equation may be called a circle; the point $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\!\backslash x',y')^2=0$ is the centre of the circle;
and the sine of the radius is $=\frac{\sqrt{ac-b^2}}{\sqrt{a}}$, where $a$ means $(a,b,c\!\backslash \alpha,\beta)^2$.

\textbf{158.} The equation of the Absolute being $x^2+y^2=0$, the distance of the points
$(x:y)$ and $(x':y')$ is in fact
\[
\log\frac{xy'-x'y+\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\sqrt{x'^2+y'^2}}{xy'-x'y-\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\sqrt{x'^2+y'^2}};
\]
or, as this expression may also be written,
\[
\log\frac{(x+iy)(x'-iy')}{(x-iy)(x'+iy')},
\]
which, putting $\frac{x+iy}{x-iy}=u$ and $\frac{x'+iy'}{x'-iy'}=u'$, becomes $\log\frac{u}{u'}$; and the theorem that
the distance of the first and second of three points is equal to the sum of the distances
of the first and second and second and third respectively, is at once verified: we have
only to write $\log\frac{u}{u'} + \log\frac{u'}{u''} = \log\frac{u}{u''}$.

\textbf{159.} What precedes is not to be understood as precluding the existence of a
relation between the spaces which are the loci in quibus of the coordinates $(x,y)$ and
$(x',y')$ respectively: not only may these be spaces of the same kind, but they may
be one and the same space or line; and the points of the two systems may then be
points of the same kind; and further, the coordinates $(x,y)$ and $(x',y')$ may belong to
the same system of coordinates, that is, the equations $(x:y)=(\alpha:1)$ and
$(x':y')=(\alpha:1)$ may denote one and the same point.

\textbf{160.} If the two point-systems are systems of the same kind, and are in one
and the same line, then there are in general two points of the first system which
coincide each of them with the corresponding point of the second system; such two
points may be said to be the \textit{sibiconjugate points} of the homography. In particular,
the two sibiconjugate points of the homography may coincide together.

\textbf{161.} A system in involution affords an example of two homographic systems in
the same line; in fact, taking arbitrarily a point out of each pair, the points so
obtained form a system which is homographic with the system formed with the other
points of the several pairs; and in this case the sibiconjugate points of the involution
are also the sibiconjugate points of the homography. Thus if $A$ and $A'$, $B$ and $B'$,
$C$ and $C'$, $D$ and $D'$ are pairs of the system in involution, then $(A,B,C,D)$ and
$(A',B',C',D')$ will be homographic point-systems; and, as a particular case,
$(A,B,C,C')$ and $(A',B',C,C')$ will be homographic point-systems. It is proper
to notice that if $F$ is a sibiconjugate point of the involution, then $(A,B,F,F)$ and
$(A',B',F,F)$ are not (what at first sight they appear to be) homographic point-systems.

\textbf{162.} Imagine an involution of points; take on the line which is the locus in
quo of the point-system a point $O$, and consider the point-system formed by the
harmonics of $O$ in respect to the several pairs of the involution; and in like manner
take on the line any other point $O'$, and consider the point-system formed by the
harmonics of $O'$ in respect to the several pairs of the involution; these two point-
systems are homographically related to each other. See Fifth Memoir, No.~111.

\textbf{163.} Two involutions may be homographically related to each other; in fact,
take on the line which is the locus in quo of the first involution a point $O$, and
consider the point-system formed by the harmonics of $O$ in relation to the several
pairs of the involution; take in like manner on the line which is the locus in quo
of the second involution a point $O'$, and consider the point-system formed by the
harmonics of $O'$ with respect to the several pairs of the involution; then if the two
point-systems are homographically related, the two involutions are said to be them-
selves homographically related: the last preceding article shows that the nature of
the relation does not in anywise depend on the choice of the points $O$ and $O'$.
And it is not necessary that, as regards the two involutions respectively, the words
line and point should have the same significations. See Fifth Memoir, No.~111.

\textbf{164.} Four or more tetrads of points in a line may be homographically related
to the same number of tetrads in another line. This is the case when the anharmonic
ratios of the tetrads of the first system are homographically related to the anharmonic
ratios of the tetrads of the second system. And it is not material which of the three
anharmonic ratios of a tetrad of either system is selected, provided that the same
selection is made for each of the other tetrads of the same system. The order of the
points of a tetrad must be attended to, but there are in all four admissible permutations
of the points of a tetrad, viz.\ if $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ are the points of a tetrad, then $(A,B,C,D)$,
$(B,A,D,C)$, $(C,D,A,B)$, $(D,C,B,A)$ may be considered as one and the same tetrad.
Any three tetrads whatever in the second system may correspond to any three tetrads
of the first system; and then given a fourth tetrad of the first system, and three out
of the four points of the corresponding tetrad of the second system, the remaining
point of the tetrad may be determined. The words line and point need not, as regards
the two systems of tetrads respectively, be understood in the same significations.
See Fifth Memoir, No.~112.

\textbf{165.} The foregoing theory of the harmonic relation shows that if we have a
point-pair
\[
(a,b,c\!\backslash x,y)^2=0,
\]
the equation of any other point-pair whatever can be expressed, and that in two
different ways, in the form
\[
(a,b,c\!\backslash x,y)^2+(lx+my)^2=0;
\]
the points $(lx+my=0)$ corresponding to the two admissible values of the linear
function being in fact the harmonics of the point-pair in respect to the given point-
pair $(a,b,c\!\backslash x,y)^2=0$, or what is the same thing, the sibiconjugate points of the
involution formed by the two point-pairs (see Fifth Memoir, No.~105). The point-pair
represented by the equation in question does not in itself stand in any peculiar
relation to the given point-pair $(a,b,c\!\backslash x,y)^2=0$; but when thus represented it is said
to be \textit{inscribed} in the given point-pair, and the point $lx+my=0$ is said to be the
\textit{axis of inscription}. And the harmonic of this point with respect to the given point-pair
(that is, the other sibiconjugate point of the involution of the two point-pairs) is said
to be the \textit{centre of inscription}.\footnote{The words inscribed, inscription, are used not in opposition to, but as identical with, the words circumscribed, circumscription; and in like manner the words centre and axis of inscription.}

\textbf{166.} We may, if we please, $(x',y')$ and $\theta$ being constants, exhibit the equation
of the inscribed point-pair in the form
\[

\textbf{On the Geometry of Two Dimensions, Nos.~169 to 231.}

\textbf{169.} In geometry of two dimensions we have the plane as a space or locus in
quo of the points and lines of the figure. A point is determined by means of the
coordinate ratios $(x:y:z)$, and a line by means of the coordinate ratios $(\xi:\eta:\zeta)$;
the equation
\[
\xi x+\eta y+\zeta z=0
\]
expresses that the point $(x,y,z)$ and the line $(\xi,\eta,\zeta)$ are corresponding point and line;
and in like manner the equation
\[
\xi x+\eta y+\zeta z=0
\]
expresses that the point $(x,y,z)$ and the line $(\xi,\eta,\zeta)$ are corresponding point and line.
It is convenient to use the word \textit{ineunt} (from ineo) to denote a point considered as
belonging to a curve; thus a point on a curve is an ineunt of the curve; and in like
manner a line touching a curve is a tangent of the curve. A line may of course be
considered as a curve of the first class, and a point as a curve of the first order.

\textbf{170.} The point-coordinates $(x,y,z)$ and the line-coordinates $(\xi,\eta,\zeta)$ are abso-
lutely independent of each other; the point $(x,y,z)$ and the line $(\xi,\eta,\zeta)$ are a corre-
sponding point and line, when they satisfy the equation $\xi x+\eta y+\zeta z=0$. The
coordinates of any point or line may be replaced by any constant multiples of their
values; but it is in general not allowable to do this simultaneously for the point and
line coordinates: when it is allowable, the equations of the figure are said to be
\textit{reduced}. The unreduced form of the equation of a line in point-coordinates is
\[
\xi x+\eta y+\zeta z=0,
\]
and the unreduced form of the equation of a point in line-coordinates is
\[
X\xi+Y\eta+Z\zeta=0.
\]

\textbf{171.} A linear equation $(a,b,c\!\backslash x,y,z)^1=0$ denotes a line, and a linear equation
$(A,B,C\!\backslash \xi,\eta,\zeta)^1=0$ denotes a point.

\textbf{172.} A quadric equation $(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash x,y,z)^2=0$ denotes a conic; the conic
may be considered as a proper conic, or as a line-pair or a point-pair. The line-pair
may be two real lines, or two conjugate imaginary lines; the point-pair may be two
real points, or two conjugate imaginary points.

\textbf{173.} But the same equation, considering $(x,y,z)$ as constant coefficients, and

same equation, considering $(\xi,\eta,\zeta)$ as constant coefficients and $(x,y,z)$ as point-
coordinates, is the equation of a line, viz.\ the polar of the point $(x,y,z)$ in respect to
the conic.

\textbf{191.} A system of points in a line is said to be a \textit{range}, and a system of
lines through a point is said to be a \textit{pencil}. The theories of ranges and pencils,
considered irrespectively of each other, are in fact a single theory, constituting the
geometry of one dimension. It has been seen how in geometry of one dimension a
range of points and a pencil of lines, although considered (as they must be considered)
as existing in distinct spaces, may nevertheless stand in certain relations to each other.
In geometry of two dimensions, the range and pencil may of course coexist in one
and the same plane as their common locus in quo; and such coexistence occurs in
fact very frequently: thus if we have a line and a point, and if lines are drawn
joining the point with the several points of the line, these lines constitute a pencil,
and the points of the line constitute a range, and such pencil and range are homo-
graphically related.

\textbf{192.} The theory of homography in geometry of two dimensions may be made to
depend upon the corresponding theory in geometry of one dimension, or what is the
same thing, upon the homography of ranges or pencils. For consider two figures existing
in distinct planes or spaces of two dimensions, any four points (not in a line) in the
second figure may correspond to any four points (not in a line) in the first figure; and
when this is so, we may, by the process about to be explained, given any other point
of the first figure, construct the corresponding point of the second figure; and the
two figures are then, by definition, homographically related. Suppose that the points
$D'$, $B'$, $C'$, $E'$ of the second figure correspond respectively to the points $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ of
the first figure, and let $E$ be any other point of the first figure; suppose that $E'$ is the
corresponding point of the second figure; the pencils $A(B,C,D,E)$ and $A'(B',C',D',E')$
should be homographic to each other, that is, $E'$ must lie on a given line through $A'$;
and in like manner the pencils $B(A,C,D,E)$ and $B'(A',C',D',E')$ should be homographic
to each other, that is, $E'$ must lie on a given line through $B'$. And then, as a theorem,
$C(A,B,D,E)$ and $C'(A',B',D',E')$, or $D(A,B,C,E)$ and $D'(A',B',C',E')$ will be homographic
pencils, that is, the construction will be a determinate one whichever two of the four
points are selected for the points $A$ and $B$. The foregoing construction leads to an
analytical relation, which I think constitutes a better foundation of the theory. Consider
the first plane as the locus in quo of the coordinates $(x,y,z)$, and the second plane as
the locus in quo of the coordinates $(X,Y,Z)$, these two coordinate systems being
absolutely independent of each other. Consider any point of the first plane and a
corresponding point of the second plane such that its coordinates $(X,Y,Z)$ are given
linear functions of the coordinates $(x,y,z)$ of the point in the first plane. Any figure
whatever in the first plane gives rise to a corresponding figure in the second plane, and
the two figures are said to be homographic to each other. To a point of the first figure
there corresponds in the second figure a point, to a line a line, to a range of points or
pencil of lines, a homographic range of points or pencil of lines; the line or point which
is the locus in quo of the range or pencil in the one figure corresponding with the line
or point which is the locus in quo of the range or pencil in the other figure. And
generally, to any curve of any order and class in the first figure, and to its ineunts and
tangents, there correspond in the second figure a curve of the same order and class, and
the ineunts and tangents of such curve.

\textbf{193.} It is to be remarked, that it is not by any means necessary that the word
plane, or the words point and line, or consequently the words order and class, should
have the same significations as regards the two figures respectively. The theory of
homography, as above explained, in fact comprises what is commonly termed the theory
of homography and also the theory of reciprocity.

\textbf{194.} Let the word plane have the ordinary signification as regards the two figures
respectively; and suppose, first, that the words point and line, and therefore order
and class, have also the ordinary significations as regards the two figures respectively;
we have here the ordinary theory of homography, in which, to any range of points
or pencil of lines in the first figure, there corresponds a homographic range of points
or pencil of lines in the second figure, and to a curve of any order and class in the
first figure there corresponds a curve of the same order and class in the second figure.

\textbf{195.} We may, as a specialization giving rise to further developments, assume that
the two figures exist in one and the same plane. There is here in general a triangle,
each of whose angles or sides, considered as a point or line in the first figure, corre-
sponds to itself, considered as a point or line in the second figure: such triangle may
be called the \textit{sibiconjugate triangle}. Any one point of the plane, considered as belonging
to the first figure, may correspond to any other point of the plane, considered as
belonging to the second figure, and the second figure is then completely determined.
The equations
\[
(x:y:z) = (\alpha X+\beta Y+\gamma Z : \alpha' X+\beta' Y+\gamma' Z : \alpha'' X+\beta'' Y+\gamma'' Z)
\]
show that the points
\[
(\alpha:\alpha':\alpha''),\quad (\beta:\beta':\beta''),\quad (\gamma:\gamma':\gamma'')
\]
are the angles of the sibiconjugate triangle; for to a line through any one of these
points in the second figure there corresponds in the first figure a line through the same
point; or, what is the same thing, the point considered as belonging to either figure
is a point corresponding to itself.

\textbf{196.} It is proper to notice that the sibiconjugate triangle may be a real triangle,
or one or two of its angles may be imaginary. In particular, the sibiconjugate triangle
may be one of the infinity of triangles the angles of which are each of them one of the
two circular points at infinity; these are the triangles which are similar each to each,
and in this case the homography gives rise to the theory of similar figures.

\textbf{197.} Let the word plane have the ordinary signification as regards the two figures
respectively; and suppose, secondly, that the words point and line, order and class,
have interchanged significations, that is, to a point in the first figure there corresponds
a line in the second figure, and to a line in the first figure there corresponds a point
in the second figure; the locus in quo of the line corresponding to a variable point
on a curve of the first figure is a curve, the ineunts of which are the lines corresponding
to the tangents of the curve in the first figure; or the ineunts of the curve in the first
figure correspond to the tangents of the curve in the second figure. The two curves
are said to be reciprocal to each other; the order of the one is equal to the class of the
other, and vice vers\^{a}. In particular, the two figures may exist in one and the same
plane; the sibiconjugate triangle has here a different signification.

\textbf{207.} The theory of distance in geometry of two dimensions is founded upon the
following definitions. The distance of two points is equal to the angle which the
normal lines of the points subtend at any point of the Absolute; the distance of two
lines is equal to the angle subtended at the centre of the Absolute by the two normal
points of the lines; the distance of a point and a line is equal to the complement of
the angle subtended at the point by the two points of intersection of the line with
the Absolute. The quadrant is the distance of two quadrantal lines, or of two
quadrantal points; it is the distance of a point from any line through its normal point,
or of a line from any point on its normal line. The quadrant is the unit of distance.

\textbf{208.} Write for shortness

\Pi &= (a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash x,y,z)^2, & \Pi' &= (a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash x',y',z')^2,\\

\end{align*}

or as it may also be written,

\cos^{-1}\frac{\nabla}{\sqrt{\Pi}\sqrt{\Pi'}};
\]
and the distance of the lines $(\xi,\eta,\zeta)$ and $(\xi',\eta',\zeta')$ is

\sin^{-1}\frac{\sqrt{(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash \xi\eta'-\xi'\eta,\; \eta\zeta'-\eta'\zeta,\; \zeta\xi'-\zeta'\xi)^2}}{\sqrt{\Sigma}\sqrt{\Sigma'}};

or as it may also be written,
\[
\cos^{-1}\frac{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{H}\!\backslash \xi,\eta,\zeta\,\backslash\,\xi',\eta',\zeta')^1}{\sqrt{\Sigma}\sqrt{\Sigma'}},
\]
where $\Sigma$ and $\Sigma'$ denote respectively $(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{H}\!\backslash \xi,\eta,\zeta)^2$ and
$(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{H}\!\backslash \xi',\eta',\zeta')^2$.

\textbf{209.} It follows from the sine formula, that the distance of a point from itself,
or of a line from itself, is always $=0$; and from the cosine formula, that the distance
of two quadrantal points, or of two quadrantal lines, is $=\frac{\pi}{2}$; and from the sine formula,
that the distance of a point from any point on the Absolute, or of a line from any
tangent of the Absolute, is $=0$; and from the cosine formula, that the distance of a
point from its normal line is $=\frac{\pi}{2}$.

\textbf{210.} It follows from the cosine formula that, if $P$, $P'$, $P''$ be three points on the
same line, then
\[
\text{Dist.}(P,P') + \text{Dist.}(P',P'') = \text{Dist.}(P,P'').
\]
And the like theorem holds for three lines through the same point.

\textbf{211.} The condition that the distance of the points $(x,y,z)$ and $(x',y',z')$ may be
a given constant is
\[
\Pi\,\Pi'\sin^2\theta - (\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{H}\!\backslash \Omega)^2 = 0.
\]
This is the equation of a conic, the point-equation of which, considering $(x',y',z')$ as
given, is as just written down; and the line-equation of which, considering $(\xi,\eta,\zeta)$
as the line-coordinates, is
\[
\Pi\,\Pi'\sin^2\theta - \nabla^2 = 0.
\]

\textbf{212.} The conic represented by either of these equations may be called a \textit{circle};
the point $(x',y',z')$ is the \textit{centre} of the circle; and the normal line of the centre is the
\textit{axis} of the circle. The sine of the radius is $=\sin\theta$. The point-equation of the circle
shows that all the ineunts of the circle are at the same distance from the centre; and
the line-equation shows that all the tangents of the circle are at the same distance
from the axis. In the particular case where the Absolute is a pair of points, the circle
is any conic through the two points of the Absolute, and the centre is the pole of the
line joining the two points.

\textbf{213.} The equation of a circle, centre $(x',y',z')$ and radius $\theta$, may be written in
the form
\[
(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash x,y,z)^2\,(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash x',y',z')^2\sin^2\theta
- [(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash x,y,z\,\backslash\,x',y',z')^1]^2 = 0.
\]

\textbf{214.} The line-equation of the same circle, the line-coordinates of the axis being
$(\xi',\eta',\zeta')$, is
\begin{multline*}
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{H}\!\backslash \xi,\eta,\zeta)^2\,(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{H}\!\backslash \xi',\eta',\zeta')^2\sin^2\theta\\
- [(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{H}\!\backslash \xi,\eta,\zeta\,\backslash\,\xi',\eta',\zeta')^1]^2 = 0.
\end{multline*}

\textbf{215.} The properties of the circle just referred to lead at once to the following
theorem. If through the centre of a circle we draw any two lines, and if from the
points where these lines meet the circle we draw lines parallel respectively to the two
lines first drawn, these lines will meet in a point on the axis of the circle. And again,
if we draw through the centre two other lines, and from the points where these meet
the circle we draw lines parallel respectively to the lines last drawn, these lines will
meet in a point on the axis of the circle; and so on. The theorem is, in fact, equivalent
to Euclid's definition of similar figures.

\textbf{216.} These properties of the circle lead immediately to the analytical expressions
for the distances of points or lines in terms of the coordinates. In fact, take
\[
(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash x,y,z)^2=0
\]
for the point-equation of the Absolute; its line-equation will be
\[
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{H}\!\backslash \xi,\eta,\zeta)^2 = 0.
\]
The point-equation of the circle having the point $(x',y',z')$ for its centre, is
\[
(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash x,y,z)^2\,(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash x',y',z')^2\cos^2\theta
- [(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash x,y,z\,\backslash\,x',y',z')^1]^2 = 0,
\]
or
\begin{multline*}
(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash x,y,z)^2\,(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash x',y',z')^2\sin^2\theta\\
- [(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash yz'-y'z,\; zx'-z'x,\; xy'-x'y)^1]^2 = 0,
\end{multline*}
from which (by the same reasoning as for the case of geometry of one dimension) it
follows that the distance of the points $(x,y,z)$, $(x',y',z')$ is
\[
\sin^{-1}\frac{(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash x,y,z\,\backslash\,x',y',z')^1}{\sqrt{(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash x,y,z)^2}\sqrt{(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash x',y',z')^2}},
\]
or what is the same thing,
\[
\sin^{-1}\frac{(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash yz'-y'z,\; zx'-z'x,\; xy'-x'y)^1}{\sqrt{(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash x,y,z)^2}\sqrt{(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash x',y',z')^2}};
\]
and it appears from the cosine formula (see ante, No.~208), that if $P$, $P'$, $P''$ be
points on the same line, then we have, as we ought to have,
\[
\text{Dist.}(P,P') + \text{Dist.}(P',P'') = \text{Dist.}(P,P'').
\]

\textbf{217.} In like manner, the line-equation of the same circle, the line-coordinates of
the axis being $(\xi',\eta',\zeta')$, is
\begin{multline*}
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{H}\!\backslash \xi,\eta,\zeta)^2\,(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{H}\!\backslash \xi',\eta',\zeta')^2\sin^2\theta\\
- [(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{H}\!\backslash \xi,\eta,\zeta\,\backslash\,\xi',\eta',\zeta')^1]^2 = 0,
\end{multline*}
from which it follows that the distance of the lines $(\xi,\eta,\zeta)$ and $(\xi',\eta',\zeta')$ is
\[
\sin^{-1}\frac{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{H}\!\backslash \xi,\eta,\zeta\,\backslash\,\xi',\eta',\zeta')^1}{\sqrt{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{H}\!\backslash \xi,\eta,\zeta)^2}\sqrt{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{H}\!\backslash \xi',\eta',\zeta')^2}},
\]
or what is the same thing
\[
\sin^{-1}\frac{(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash \eta\zeta'-\eta'\zeta,\; \zeta\xi'-\zeta'\xi,\; \xi\eta'-\xi'\eta)^1}{\sqrt{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{H}\!\backslash \xi,\eta,\zeta)^2}\sqrt{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{H}\!\backslash \xi',\eta',\zeta')^2}}.
\]

\textbf{218.} And we may from the first formula of either set, deduce for the distance
of the point $(x,y,z)$ and the line $(\xi,\eta,\zeta)$, the expression
\[
\sin^{-1}\frac{\xi x+\eta y+\zeta z}{\sqrt{(a,b,c,f,g,h\!\backslash x,y,z)^2}\sqrt{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{H}\!\backslash \xi,\eta,\zeta)^2}},
\]
as may be easily seen by writing $\xi\xi'+\eta\eta'+\zeta\zeta'$ for $x'$, $y'$, $z'$, or $ax+hy+gz$, \&c.\ for
$\xi$, $\eta$, $\zeta$, and putting $\sin^{-1}$ for $\cos^{-1}$.

\textbf{219.} It may be noticed that there are certain lines, viz.\ the tangents of the
Absolute, in regard to which, considered as a space of one dimension, the Absolute is
a pair of coincident points; and in like manner certain points, viz.\ the ineunts of the
Absolute, in regard to which, considered as a space of one dimension, the Absolute is
a pair of coincident lines.

\textbf{220.} We may, in particular, suppose that the Absolute, instead of being a proper
conic, is a pair of points. The line through the two points may be called the \textit{Absolute
line}; such line is to be considered as a pair of coincident lines. Any point whatever
determines with the Absolute, two lines, viz.\ the lines joining the point with the two
points of the Absolute; this line-pair is the Absolute for the point considered as a
space of one dimension or locus in quo of a pencil of lines, and the theory of the
distances of lines through a point is therefore precisely the same as in the general case.
But any line whatever determines with the Absolute (meets the Absolute line in) a
pair of coincident points, which pair of coincident points is the Absolute in regard to
such line considered as a space of one dimension or locus in quo of a range of points,
and the theory of the distance of points on a line is therefore the theory before
explained for this special case. But we cannot, in the same way as before, compare
the distances of points upon different lines, since we have not in the present case the
quadrant as a unit of distance. The comparison must be made by means of the circle,
viz.\ in the present case any conic passing through the two points of the Absolute is
termed a circle, and the point of intersection of the tangents to the circle at the two
points of the Absolute (or what is the same thing, the pole of the Absolute line in
respect to the circle) is the centre of the circle. The Absolute line itself may, if it is
necessary to do so, be considered as the axis of the circle. It is assumed that the
points of the circle are all of them equidistant from the centre, and by this assumption
we are enabled to compare distances upon different lines. In fact we may, by a
construction precisely similar to that of Euclid, Book~I.\ Prop.~II., from a given point
draw a finite line equal to a given finite line $BC$, and thence also upon a given line
through $A$, determine the finite line $AD$ equal to the given finite line $BC$. Since the
unit of distance for points on a line is arbitrary, we cannot of course compare the
distances of points with the distances of lines. The distance of a point from a line
does, however, admit of comparison with the distance of two points; we have only to
assume as a definition that the distance of a point from a line is the distance of the
point from the point of intersection of the line with the quadrantal line through the
point.

\textbf{221.} As regards the analytical theory, suppose that the point-coordinates of the
two points of the Absolute are $(p,q,r)$, $(p_0,q_0,r_0)$, then the line-equation of the Absolute is
\[
(pp_0, qq_0, rr_0, qr_0+q_0r, rp_0+r_0p, pq_0+p_0q\!\backslash \xi,\eta,\zeta)^2 = 0,
\]
or, as this may be written,
\[
(\xi p+\eta q+\zeta r)(\xi p_0+\eta q_0+\zeta r_0) = 0;
\]
so that the line-equation of the Absolute is the equation of the two points, or, what
is the same thing, the equation of the Absolute line regarded as a pair of coincident
lines. And in like manner, the point-equation of the Absolute is the equation of the
Absolute line regarded as a pair of coincident lines.

\textbf{222.} In the foregoing case of a pair of points as the Absolute, the formul\ae\ for the
distance of two points and of two lines continue to subsist; and the formula for the
distance of a point and a line also continues to subsist. The line-equation of the
circle, centre $(x',y',z')$ and radius $\theta$, is
\begin{multline*}
(\xi x'+\eta y'+\zeta z')^2\,(pp_0,qq_0,rr_0,qr_0+q_0r,rp_0+r_0p,pq_0+p_0q\!\backslash \xi,\eta,\zeta)^2\sin^2\theta\\
- [(pp_0,qq_0,rr_0,qr_0+q_0r,rp_0+r_0p,pq_0+p_0q\!\backslash \xi,\eta,\zeta\,\backslash\,x',y',z')^1]^2 = 0,
\end{multline*}
where $\sin^2\theta$ is arbitrary. The point-equation of the same circle is
\begin{multline*}
(pp_0,qq_0,rr_0,qr_0+q_0r,rp_0+r_0p,pq_0+p_0q\!\backslash x,y,z)^2\,(\xi x'+\eta y'+\zeta z')^2\sin^2\theta\\
- [(pp_0,qq_0,rr_0,qr_0+q_0r,rp_0+r_0p,pq_0+p_0q\!\backslash x,y,z\,\backslash\,x',y',z')^1]^2 = 0.
\end{multline*}

\textbf{223.} In particular, if the two points of the Absolute are the circular points at
infinity, the theory of distance becomes the ordinary theory of distance in plane
geometry. The Absolute line is the line at infinity. The distance of two points is the
ordinary distance; the distance of two lines is the angle between them; and the distance
of a point from a line is the perpendicular distance. The circle is the ordinary circle.
The quadrant is $=\frac{\pi}{2}$.

\textbf{224.} It may be remarked, that the condition in order that the distance of two
points may be $=0$ is that the line joining the two points may pass through one of the
points of the Absolute; and in particular, if the Absolute is the two circular points at
infinity, that the line joining the two points may pass through one of the circular points,
that is, that the two points may be in one and the same direction from each other. The
condition in order that two lines may be at the distance $=0$ from each other is that
the two lines may meet at a point of the Absolute; and in particular, if the Absolute is
the two circular points at infinity, that the two lines may meet at a circular point, that
is, that the two lines may be parallel to each other.

\textbf{225.} The condition that two points may be at a quadrantal distance from each
other is that the line joining them may be a tangent of the Absolute; and the condition
that two lines may be at a quadrantal distance from each other is that their point of
intersection may lie on the Absolute.

\textbf{226.} The normal of a point is the line joining the point with the pole of the
Absolute line; that is, the normal of a point is the line through the point and the
centre of any circle; and the normal of a line is the line through the intersection of
the line with the Absolute line, and the pole of the line in respect to any circle.

\textbf{227.} If the Absolute is a pair of points, the harmonic of a point with respect to
the Absolute is the point such that the two points of the Absolute, the given point and
its harmonic, form an involution; that is, the point-pair consisting of the two points of
the Absolute, and the point-pair consisting of the given point and its harmonic, form
an involution. The harmonic of a line with respect to the Absolute is the line through
the pole of the given line in respect to any circle; that is, the line through the centre
of any circle and the intersection of the given line with the Absolute line.

\textbf{228.} If the Absolute is a proper conic, the harmonic of a point is the pole of
the line joining the point with its normal point; that is, the point of intersection of
the tangents to the Absolute from the given point. And the harmonic of a line is the
polar of the point of intersection of the line with its normal line; that is, the line
joining the points of contact of the Absolute with the tangents from the point of
intersection of the line and its normal line.

\textbf{229.} In ordinary plane geometry, the Absolute degenerates into a pair of points,
viz.\ the two circular points at infinity. The general formul\ae\ for distance, \&c.,
thus become the ordinary formul\ae\ of plane geometry. It is hardly necessary to
remark that in this case the notion of the quadrantal relation of two points has
altogether disappeared, and that the unit of distance has become the right angle.

\textbf{230.} In the geometry of similar figures, the Absolute is a proper conic; but the
homography is such that the two circular points at infinity are the sibiconjugate points
of the homography; that is, the homography is a similarity. The theory of distance
applies to similar figures; but the distances in different figures are not comparable with
each other, except in the case of similar figures, where the ratio of the distances is the
ratio of similarity.

\textbf{231.} In spherical geometry, the Absolute is a proper conic; the plane of the
Absolute is the plane at infinity; and the points and lines of the figure are the points
and great circles of a sphere. The theory of distance applies to spherical geometry;
and the distances upon different great circles are comparable with each other, since the
Absolute is the same for all the great circles.

\textbf{137.} The theory developed in the Sixth Memoir upon Quantics is, I think,
one of the most important contributions which I have made to Mathematics. I find
that the first communication of it to the Mathematical Society was made on November
9, 1858; the memoir was received by the Royal Society November 18, 1858. Prof.\ Klein
has called my attention to a reference (p.~132 of his second paper) to the theory
developed in Staudt's \textit{Geometrie der Lage}, 1847 (more fully in the \textit{Beitr\'{a}ge zur Geometrie}
\textit{der Lage, Zweites Heft}, 1857). The logarithm of the formula is $\log(A,B,P,Q)$, and,
according to Staudt's theory $(A,B,P,Q)$, the anharmonic ratio of any four points, has
independently of any notion of distance the fundamental properties of a numerical
magnitude, viz.\ any two such ratios have a sum and also a product, such sum and
product being each of them a like ratio of four points determinable by purely descriptive
constructions. The proof is easiest for the product: say the ratios are $(A,B,P,Q)$ and
$(A',B',P',Q')$; then considering these as given points we can construct $R$ such that
$(A',B',P',Q') = (A,B,Q,R)$: the two ratios are thus $(A,B,P,Q)$ and $(A,B,Q,R)$, and we
say that their product is $(A,B,P,R)$ (observe as to this that introducing the notion of
distance, the two factors are $\frac{PQ}{BQ}$ and $\frac{QR}{PR}$, and then their product $=\frac{PR}{BR}$, which is $(A,B,P,R)$,
which is the foundation of the definition). Next for the sum, we construct $Q_1$ such that
$(A',B',P',Q') = (A,B,P,Q_1)$; the sum then is $(A,B,P,Q)+(A,B,P,Q_1)$; and if we then
construct $S$ such that $(A,R)$, $(Q,Q_1)$, $(B,S)$ are an involution, we say that
$(A,B,P,Q)+(A,B,P,Q_1) = (A,B,P,S)$. (Observe as to this that again introducing the
notion of distance the last mentioned equation is $\frac{PQ}{BQ}+\frac{PQ_1}{BQ_1}=\frac{PS}{BS}$, that is
$\frac{q}{b-q}+\frac{q_1}{b-q_1}=\frac{s}{b-s}$, which expresses that $S$ is determined as above; in fact the equation
$\begin{vmatrix}1,b+s&bs\\1,q+q_1&qq_1\\1,2a&a^2\end{vmatrix}=0$ is readily seen to be equivalent to
$\frac{q}{b-q}+\frac{q_1}{b-q_1}=\frac{s}{b-s}$.)

It must however be admitted that, in applying this theory of Staudt's to the theory
of distance, there is at least the appearance of arguing in a circle, since the construction
for the product of the two ratios, is in effect the assumption of the relation,
\[
\text{dist.}\ PQ + \text{dist.}\ QR = \text{dist.}\ PR.
\]

\textbf{138.} In regard to the ``Academy problem,''---I may refer to Boole's memoir,
\textit{Philosophical Transactions}, 1862, pp.~225---252; and to Wilbraham's memoir, \textit{Phil.~Mag.}

by one or both of two causes $A$ and $B$ acting, and the probability of $A$ acting is $a$, of

happening if $B$ acts is $q$, what is the probability of the event?'' Boole's answer is

u = \frac{a\beta(1-p)(1-q)}{a(1-p)+\beta(1-q)-\nu'},

where $\nu'$ is a root of the equation
\[
a(1-p)(a-\nu')(\beta-\nu')+\beta(1-q)\nu'(1-a-\beta+\nu')=0.
\]

\textit{Lage, Zweites Heft}, 1857).
