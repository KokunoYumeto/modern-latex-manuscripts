\section*{147.}
\begin{center}
\Large\bfseries
A MEMOIR ON THE SYMMETRIC FUNCTIONS\\OF THE ROOTS\\OF AN\\EQUATION.
\end{center}

\begin{flushright}
\small
[From the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London,\\
vol.~CXLVII. for the year 1857, pp.~489---499.\\
Received December 18, 1856,---Read January 8, 1857.]
\end{flushright}

\normalsize
\setcounter{footnote}{0}

There are contained in a work, which is not, I think, so generally known
as it deserves to be, the ``Algebra'' of Meyer Hirsch\footnote{The work
referred to is entitled \textit{Sammlung von Beispielen Formeln und Aufgaben
aus der Buchstabenrechnung und Aufgaben aus der Buchstabenrechnung und
Algebra}, 8vo. Berlin, 1804 (8 ed.~1853), English translation by Ross, 8vo.
London, 1827.} some very useful tables of the symmetric functions up to the
tenth degree of the roots of an equation of any order. It seems desirable to
join to these a set of tables, giving reciprocally the expressions of the
powers and products of the coefficients in terms of the symmetric functions of
the roots. The present memoir contains the two sets of tables, viz.\ the new
tables distinguished by the letter $(a)$, and the tables of Meyer Hirsch
distinguished by the letter $(b)$; the memoir contains also some remarks as to
the mode of calculation of the new tables, and also as to a peculiar symmetry
of the numbers in the tables of each set, a symmetry which, so far as I am
aware, has not hitherto been observed, and the existence of which appears to
constitute an important theorem in the subject. The theorem in question might,
I think, be deduced from a very elegant formula of M.~Borchardt (referred to in
the sequel), which gives the generating function of any symmetric function of
the roots, and contains potentially a method for the calculation of the
Tables~$(b)$, but which, from the example I have given, would not appear to be
a very convenient one for actual calculation.

Suppose in general
\[
(1, b, c \ldots \mid x)^n = (1 - \theta x)(1 - \beta x)(1 - \gamma x)\ldots,
\]
so that
\begin{alignat*}{2}
-b &= \Sigma\alpha, &\quad +c &= \Sigma\alpha\beta, \\
-d &= \Sigma\alpha\beta\gamma, &\quad \&\text{c.},
\end{alignat*}
and if in general
\[
(pqr\ldots) = \Sigma\alpha^p \beta^q \gamma^r \ldots,
\]
where as usual the summation extends only to the distinct terms, so that
e.g.\ $(p^2)$ contains only half as many terms as $(pq)$, and so in all
similar cases, then we have
\begin{gather*}
-b = (1), \qquad +c = (1^2), \qquad -d = (1^3), \quad \&\text{c.};
\end{gather*}
and the two problems which arise are, first to express any combination
$b^p c^q d^r \ldots$ in terms of the symmetric functions
$(1^{\lambda} 2^{\mu} 3^{\nu}\ldots)$, and secondly, or conversely, to express any
symmetric function $(1^{\lambda} 2^{\mu} 3^{\nu}\ldots)$ in terms of the
combinations $b^p c^q d^r \ldots$.

It will conduce materially to brevity if $1^p 2^q \ldots$ be termed the
\emph{partition} belonging to the combination $b^p c^q \ldots$; and in like
manner if $1^{\lambda} 2^{\mu} 3^{\nu}\ldots$ be termed the \emph{partition}
belonging to the symmetric function $(1^{\lambda} 2^{\mu} 3^{\nu}\ldots)$, and if
the sum of the component numbers of the partition is termed the \emph{weight}.

Consider now a line of combinations corresponding to a given weight, e.g.\ the
weight 4, this will be
\[
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
& e & bd & c^2 & b^2 c & b^4 \\ \hline
\text{(line)} & 4 & 13 & 2^2 & 1^2 2 & 1^4
\end{array}
\]
where I have written under each combination the partition which belongs to it,
and in like manner a column of symmetric functions of the same weight, viz.
\[
\begin{array}{c}
(4) \\
(31) \\
(2^2) \\
(21^2) \\
(1^4)
\end{array}
\qquad
\begin{array}{c}
\text{(column)}
\end{array}
\]
where, as the partitions are obtained by simply omitting the $(\quad)$, I have
not separately written down the partitions. It is at once obvious that the
different combinations of the line will be made up of numerical multiples of
the symmetric functions of the column; and conversely, that the symmetric
functions of the column will be made up of numerical multiples of the
combinations of the line; but this implies the existence of two square tables,
viz.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item A table giving the expressions of the combinations of the line in terms
of the symmetric functions of the column.
\item A table giving reciprocally the expressions of the symmetric functions
of the column in terms of the combinations of the line.
\end{enumerate}

The Tables $(a)$ are the new tables above referred to; the Tables $(b)$ are
those of Meyer Hirsch.

The mode of calculation of the Tables~$(a)$ is as follows. Suppose the table
for any weight $w$ has been calculated, and that it is required to calculate
the table for the next superior weight $w + 1$. If the last column of the new
table is known, then all the rest is known. For any combination
$b^p c^q \ldots$ is the product of $b$ into the combination $b^{p-1} c^q \ldots$
of the next inferior weight; the expression of the latter combination in terms
of the symmetric functions of the corresponding column is given by the table
for the weight $w$, and multiplying by $b$, i.e.\ by $-(1)$, we have the
expression of the combination $b^p c^q \ldots$ in terms of the symmetric
functions of the column for the weight $w + 1$. It therefore only remains to
show how the last column is to be calculated. Let the combination belonging to
the last place be $b^p$, and let the partition belonging to it be
$1^p = (11 \ldots \text{to } p \text{ units})$; let the partitions of the same
weight $(p)$, arranged in the order of the column, be
$\lambda, \lambda', \lambda'', \ldots$, and write
\[\begin{array}{ccccc}
\lambda, & \lambda', & \lambda'', & \ldots \\
\alpha, & \alpha', & \alpha'', & \ldots
\end{array}\]
where $\alpha, \alpha', \alpha'', \ldots$ are arbitrary; then we have
\begin{align*}
& \alpha\lambda + \alpha'\lambda' + \alpha''\lambda'' + \ldots \\
&\qquad = b^p = (-1)^p (1)^p \\
&\qquad = (-1)^p \{(p) + A(1)(p-1) + A'(1^2)(p-2) + A''(1^3)(p-3) + \ldots\},
\end{align*}
where $A$, $A'$, $A''$ are the coefficients of the equation
\[
z^p - pz^{p-1} + A\, p(p-1)\, z^{p-2} - A'\, p(p-1)(p-2)\, z^{p-3} + \ldots = 0,
\]
i.e.\ the equation
\[
(z - 1)^p - 1 = 0,
\]
so that $A$, $A'$, $A''$ are given explicitly by
\begin{align*}
A &= \frac{1}{2}, \\
A' &= \frac{1}{2\cdot 3}, \\
A'' &= \frac{1}{2\cdot 3\cdot 4},
\end{align*}
\&c.; and we have
\begin{alignat*}{3}
\alpha &= (-1)^p, &\quad \alpha' &= (-1)^p A, &\quad \alpha'' &= (-1)^p A', \quad \&\text{c.};
\end{alignat*}
or the terms of the last column are obtained from the binomial coefficients
with the signs alternately positive and negative.

The following example will serve to render the process intelligible. Suppose
the table for the weight 6 has been calculated, and that it is required to
calculate the table for the weight 7. Write
\[
\begin{array}{c|ccccccc}
& 7 & 61 & 52 & 51^2 & 43 & 421 & \ldots \\ \hline
& \alpha & \beta & \gamma & \delta & \epsilon & \zeta & \ldots
\end{array}
\]
then the first six lines of the new table are obtained from the table for the
weight 6, by multiplying in succession the combinations
$c^2$, $bcd$, $bd^2$, $b^2 c$, $b^3$, $b^2 c$ (the six combinations of the
line for the weight 6) by $b$, i.e.\ by $-(1)$; the last line is obtained
from the equation
\[
\alpha(7) + \beta(61) + \gamma(52) + \delta(51^2) + \epsilon(43) + \zeta(421)
+ \ldots = b^7 = -(1)^7,
\]
which gives
\[\alpha = -1, \quad \beta = \frac{7}{2}, \quad \gamma = -\frac{7\cdot 6}{2\cdot 3},
\quad \delta = \frac{7\cdot 6\cdot 5}{2\cdot 3\cdot 4}, \quad \&\text{c.};\]
so that the line in question is
\[
-1(7) + 7(61) - 21(52) + 35(51^2) - 35(43) + 21(421) - \ldots;
\]
and we thus obtain the complete table for the weight 7.

The theorem as to the symmetry of the numbers of the Tables~$(a)$ and~$(b)$
respectively is as follows:
\begin{quote}
\textit{In either table, if the numbers be read in the reverse order, i.e.\ in
reading each line from the last column towards the first, and the lines in
order from the last to the first, then (the signs being disregarded) the
numbers will be the same as before; i.e.\ there is the same number in the
first line and first column as in the last line and last column, and so for
the other terms.}
\end{quote}

If in the Tables~$(a)$ we attend to the signs, then the rule is, that the
number in the first line and first column is equal to that in the last line
and last column, but that for any other two corresponding numbers, those in
the places which are symmetrical with respect to the centre of the table are
to each other in the ratio of the numbers of the partitions to which they
respectively belong; or, what is the same thing, the number in any place,
divided by the number of terms of the corresponding symmetric function, is
equal to the number in the reverse place, divided by the number of terms of
the corresponding combination.

I was led to the foregoing theorem by the consideration of a formula of
M.~Borchardt (Crelle, tom.~LIII. p.~193, 1855), for the generating function
of any symmetric function of the roots. Writing the equation under the form
\[
1 - bz + cz^2 - dz^3 + \ldots = (1 - \theta z)(1 - \beta z)(1 - \gamma z)\ldots,
\]
and putting
\[
\{1 - bz + cz^2 - dz^3 + \ldots\}^m = 1 - Bz + Cz^2 - Dz^3 + \ldots,
\]
then if $\lambda, \mu, \nu\ldots$ denote the partitions of any number $w$
into not more than $m$ parts, none of which exceed $n$, the coefficient of
\[
(-1)^{\lambda + \mu + \nu + \ldots}\, (\lambda)\, (\mu)\, (\nu) \ldots
\]
in $B^\alpha C^\beta D^\gamma \ldots$ is given explicitly as a function of
$\lambda, \mu, \nu\ldots$ and of $m$. In fact, if $u$ be the number of
numbers in the complex $(\lambda, \mu, \nu\ldots)$ which are equal to $r$,
then the coefficient in question is
\[
\frac{m(m+1)\ldots(m+\Sigma u - 1)}{\prod\limits_r \{u\, !\, (n-r+1)^{u_r}\}}.
\]
This formula contains potentially a method for the calculation of the
Tables~$(b)$; but I proceed to explain what the formula becomes for the first
few cases.

And in general it is easy to see that the left-hand side of M.~Borchardt's
formula is equal to
\[
[0] + [1]\,(1)\,(1)' + [2]\,(2)\,(2)' + [1^2]\,(1^2)\,(1^2)' + \&\text{c.},
\]
where $(1)$, $(2)$, $(1^2)$, \&c.\ are the symmetric functions of the roots
$(\alpha, \beta, \ldots \kappa)$, $(1)'$, $(2)'$, $(1^2)'$, \&c.\ are the
corresponding symmetric functions of $(x, y, \ldots u)$, and $[0]$, $[1]$,
$[2]$, $[1^2]$, \&c.\ are mere numerical coefficients; viz.\ $[0]$ is equal to
$1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \ldots n$, and $[1]$, $[2]$, $[1^2]$, \&c.\ are such that the
product of one of these factors into the number of terms in the corresponding
symmetric function $(1)$, $(2)$, $(1^2)$, \&c.\ may be equal to
$1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \ldots n$. The right-hand side of M.~Borchardt's formula is
therefore, as in the particular case, the generating function of the symmetric
functions of the roots $(\alpha, \beta, \ldots \kappa)$, and if a convenient
expression of such right-hand side could be obtained, we might by means of it
express all the symmetric functions of the roots in terms of the coefficients.

\begin{center}
\textit{Tables relating to the Symmetric Functions of the Roots of an Equation.}
\end{center}

The outside line of letters contains the combinations (powers and products) of
the coefficients, the coefficients being all with the positive sign, and the
coefficient of the highest power being unity; thus in the case of a cubic
equation the equation is
\[
x^3 + bx^2 + cx + d = (x - \alpha)(x - \beta)(x - \gamma) = 0.
\]
The outside line of numbers is obtained from that of letters merely by writing
1, 2, 3\ldots for $b$, $c$, $d\ldots$, and may be considered simply as a
different notation for the combinations. The outside column contains the
different symmetric functions in the notation above explained, viz.\ $(1)$
denotes $\Sigma\alpha$, $(2)$ denotes $\Sigma\alpha^2$, $(1^2)$ denotes
$\Sigma\alpha\beta$, and so on. The Tables~$(a)$ are to be read according to
the columns; thus Table~II~$(a)$ means $b^2 = 1\,(2) + 2\,(1^2)$,
$c = (1^2)$. The Tables~$(b)$ are to be read according to the lines; thus
Table~II~$(b)$ means $(2) = -2c + 1\,b^2$, $(1^2) = +1\,c$.

\section*{148.}
\begin{center}
\Large\bfseries
MEMOIR ON THE RESULTANT OF A SYSTEM OF TWO EQUATIONS.
\end{center}

\begin{flushright}
\small
[From the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London,\\
vol.~CXLVII. for the year 1857, pp.~703---715.\\
Received December 18, 1856,---Read January 8, 1857.]
\end{flushright}

\normalsize

The Resultant of two equations such as
\[
(a, b, \ldots \mid x, y)^m = 0,
\qquad
(p, q, \ldots \mid x, y)^n = 0,
\]
is, it is well known, a function homogeneous in regard to the coefficients of
each equation separately, viz.\ of the degree $n$ in regard to the
coefficients $(a, b, \ldots)$ of the first equation, and of the degree $m$
in regard to the coefficients $(p, q, \ldots)$ of the second equation; and it
is natural to develope the resultant in the form
\[
kAP + k'A'P' + \&\text{c.},
\]
where $A$, $A'$, \&c.\ are the combinations (powers and products) of the
degree $n$ in the coefficients $(a, b, \ldots)$, $P$, $P'$, \&c.\ are the
combinations of the degree $m$ in the coefficients $(p, q, \ldots)$, and $k$,
$k'$, \&c.\ are mere numerical coefficients. The object of the present memoir
is to show how this may be conveniently effected, either by the method of
symmetric functions, or from the known expression of the Resultant in the form
of a determinant, and to exhibit the developed expressions for the resultant of
two equations, the degrees of which do not exceed 4. With respect to the first
method, the formula in its best form, or nearly so, is given in the Algebra of
Meyer~Hirsch, and the application of it is very easy when the necessary tables
are calculated: as to this, see my ``Memoir on the Symmetric Functions of the
Roots of an Equation''\footnote{Philosophical Transactions, 1857,
pp.~489---497, [147].}. But when the expression for the Resultant of two
equations is to be calculated without the assistance of such tables, it is I
think by far the most simple process to develope the determinant according to
the second of the two methods.

Consider first the method of symmetric functions, and to fix the ideas, let
the two equations be
\[
(a, b, c, d \mid x, y)^3 = 0,
\qquad
(p, q, r \mid x, y)^2 = 0.
\]
Then writing
\[
(a, b, c, d \mid 1, z)^3 = a(1 - \alpha z)(1 - \beta z)(1 - \gamma z),
\]
so that
\[
-\frac{b}{a} = \alpha + \beta + \gamma = (1), \quad
\frac{c}{a} = \alpha\beta + \alpha\gamma + \beta\gamma = (1^2), \quad
-\frac{d}{a} = \alpha\beta\gamma = (1^3);
\]
the Resultant is
\[
(p, q, r \mid \alpha, 1)^2 \cdot
(p, q, r \mid \beta, 1)^2 \cdot
(p, q, r \mid \gamma, 1)^2,
\]
which is equal to
\[
r^6 + qr^5(\alpha + \beta + \gamma)
+ pr^4(\alpha^2 + \beta^2 + \gamma^2)
+ pqr(\alpha^2\beta + \alpha\beta^2 + \beta^2\gamma + \beta\gamma^2

\begin{vmatrix}

  &   & a & b & c & d & \ldots \\

\section*{149.}
\begin{center}
\Large\bfseries
ON THE SYMMETRIC FUNCTIONS OF THE ROOTS OF CERTAIN\\SYSTEMS OF TWO EQUATIONS.
\end{center}

\begin{flushright}
\small
[From the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London,\\
vol.~CXLVII. for the year 1857, pp.~717---726.\\
Received December 18, 1856,---Read January 8, 1857.]
\end{flushright}

\normalsize

Suppose in general that $\phi = 0$, $\psi = 0$, \&c.\ denote a system of
$(n - 1)$ equations between the $n$ variables $(x, y, z, \ldots)$, where the
functions $\phi$, $\psi$, \&c.\ are quantics (i.e.\ rational and integral
homogeneous functions) of the variables. Any values $(x_1, y_1, z_1, \ldots)$
satisfying the equations, are said to constitute a \emph{set} of roots of the
system; the roots of the same set are, it is clear, only determinate to a
common factor pres, i.e.\ only the ratios inter se and not the absolute
magnitudes of the roots of a set are determinate. The number of sets, or the
degree of the system, is equal to the product of the degrees of the component
equations.

Imagine a function of the roots which remains unaltered when any two sets
$(x_1, y_1, z_1, \ldots)$ and $(x_2, y_2, z_2, \ldots)$ are interchanged
(that is, when $x_1$ and $x_2$, $y_1$ and $y_2$, \&c.\ are simultaneously
interchanged), and which is besides homogeneous of the same degree as regards
each entire set of roots, although not of necessity homogeneous as regards the
different roots of the same set; thus, for example, if the sets are
$(x_1, y_1)$, $(x_2, y_2)$, then the functions $x_1 x_2$,
$x_1 y_2 + x_2 y_1$, $y_1 y_2$ are each of them of the form in question; but
the first and third of these functions, although homogeneous of the first
degree in regard to each entire set, are not homogeneous as regards the two
variables of each set. A function of the above-mentioned form may, for
shortness, be termed a \emph{symmetric function of the roots}; such function
(disregarding an arbitrary factor depending on the common factors which enter
implicitly into the different sets of roots) will be a rational and integral
function of the coefficients of the equations, i.e.\ any symmetric function of
the roots may be considered as a rational and integral function of the
coefficients.

The general process for the investigation of such expression for a symmetric
function of the roots is as follows. Consider in the first place a single set
of roots $(x_1, y_1, z_1, \ldots)$; it will be convenient to deal with the
ratios rather than with the actual values of the roots, and I write accordingly
$(f_1, g_1, h_1, \ldots)$ in place of $(x_1, y_1, z_1, \ldots)$, or, to
avoid subscripts, $(f, g, h, \ldots)$; the actual values are $x_1 = \theta f_1$,
$y_1 = \theta g_1$, \&c., where $\theta$ is indeterminate. The symmetric function
to be considered will, as regards each set of roots, be a function only of the
ratios $(f, g, h, \ldots)$; or, what is the same thing, it will be a
homogeneous function, of any degree, as regards each set of roots. Let the
degree as regards each set be $k$, and let $n$ be the number of variables
(which is one more than the number of equations); then we have
\[
(\Sigma f)^k = \Sigma f^k + A\, \Sigma f^{k-1}g + B\, \Sigma f^{k-2}g^2
+ C\, \Sigma f^{k-2}gh + \ldots,
\]
where $A$, $B$, $C$, \&c.\ are the coefficients of the expansion of
$(f + g + h + \ldots)^k$, divided respectively by the numbers of terms of the
corresponding symmetric functions. The several functions $\Sigma f^k$,
$\Sigma f^{k-1}g$, \&c.\ are termed \emph{elementary symmetric functions}, and
the number of such functions is equal to the number of partitions of $k$ into
not more than $n$ parts. And any symmetric function of the roots can be
expressed as a linear function of these elementary symmetric functions, with
coefficients which are rational and integral functions of the coefficients of
the given equations.

The process in detail is as follows. Let the equations be, for instance,
\[
\phi = (a, b, c \mid x, y, z)^2 = 0,
\qquad
\psi = (p, q, r \mid x, y, z)^1 = 0,
\]
so that the degrees are 2 and 1 respectively, and the number of variables is
3. The degree of the system (number of sets of roots) is $2 \times 1 = 2$.
Let the symmetric function to be considered be $\Sigma x_1 x_2$ (which is of
the first degree in each set). We have $x_1 x_2 = f_1 f_2 \cdot \theta\theta'$,
where $\theta$, $\theta'$ are the indeterminate factors; but it is more
convenient to consider the function as homogeneous of the degree 2 in each set
by writing $x_1^2 x_2^2$ in place of $x_1 x_2$ (or rather by considering the
symmetric function $x_1^2 x_2^2 + x_1^2 y_2^2 + \ldots$), and then restoring
the proper degree at the end of the calculation by dividing by the proper
power of the resultant. The process then consists in forming the product
\[
(x_1^2 + y_1^2 + z_1^2)(x_2^2 + y_2^2 + z_2^2),
\]
and expressing this in terms of the coefficients by means of the equations
$\phi = 0$, $\psi = 0$.

symmetric functions of the roots of systems of two equations. Tables are given
showing the developed expressions for the elementary symmetric functions in
terms of the coefficients, for various degrees of the equations.]}

\section*{150.}
\begin{center}
\Large\bfseries
A MEMOIR ON THE CONDITIONS FOR THE EXISTENCE OF GIVEN\\SYSTEMS OF EQUALITIES AMONG THE ROOTS OF\\AN EQUATION.
\end{center}

\begin{flushright}
\small
[From the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London,\\
vol.~CXLVII. for the year 1857, pp.~727---731.\\
Received December 18, 1856,---Read January 8, 1857.]
\end{flushright}

\normalsize

It is well known that there is a symmetric function of the roots of an
equation, viz.\ the product of the squares of the differences of the roots,
which vanishes when any two roots are put equal to each other, and that
consequently such function expressed in terms of the coefficients and equated
to zero, gives the condition for the existence of a pair of equal roots. And
it was remarked long ago by Professor Sylvester, in some of his earlier papers
in the Philosophical Magazine, that the like method could be applied to
finding the conditions for the existence of other systems of equalities among
the roots, viz.\ that it was possible to form symmetric functions, each of
them a sum of terms containing the product of a certain number of the
differences of the roots, and such that the entire function might vanish for
the particular system of equalities in question and that such functions
expressed in terms of the coefficients and equated to zero would give the
required conditions.

The object of the present memoir is to extend this theory and render it
exhaustive, by showing how to form a series of types of all the different
functions which vanish for one or more systems of equalities among the roots;
and in particular to obtain by the method distinctive conditions for all the
different systems of equalities between the roots of a quartic or a quintic
equation, viz.\ for each system conditions which are satisfied for the
particular system, and are not satisfied for any other systems, except, of
course, the more special systems included in the particular system.

The question of finding the conditions for any particular system of equalities
is essentially an indeterminate one, for given any set of functions which
vanish, a function syzygetically connected with these will also vanish; the
discussion of the nature of the syzygetic relations between the different
functions which vanish for any particular system of equalities, and of the
order of the system composed of the several conditions for the particular
system of equalities, does not enter into the plan of the present memoir.
I have referred here to the indeterminateness of the question for the sake of
the remark that I have availed myself thereof, to express by means of
invariants or covariants the different systems of conditions obtained in the
sequel.

\textbf{1.} Let the roots of an equation be $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$,
$\delta$, $\epsilon$, \&c.; and let it be required to find the condition in
order that there may be a given system of equal roots. Suppose, for instance,
that the roots are $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, $\delta$, $\epsilon$, the
condition to be found is that which expresses that $\alpha = \beta$. A
function which vanishes for $\alpha = \beta$ is evidently
$(\alpha - \beta)$, but this not being a symmetric function, we must, in
order to obtain a symmetric function, take the product of all the like
functions; the function so obtained will vanish if any two roots are equal,
and it is therefore the discriminant of the equation. Or, what is the same
thing, instead of $(\alpha - \beta)$, we may take $(\alpha - \beta)^2$,
which gives at once the discriminant without any superfluous factor.

\textbf{2.} Suppose, in the case of a quartic equation, that the condition to
be found is that which expresses that there are two pairs of equal roots,
i.e.\ $\alpha = \beta$, $\gamma = \delta$. The function
$(\alpha - \beta)(\gamma - \delta)$ is not symmetrical, but the product of
the three functions
\[
(\alpha - \beta)(\gamma - \delta), \quad
(\alpha - \gamma)(\beta - \delta), \quad
(\alpha - \delta)(\beta - \gamma),
\]
i.e.\ the square root of the product of these functions by their several
like functions obtained by permutations of the letters, is a symmetric
function; and the square of this symmetric function is the discriminant. But
instead of forming the product of the three functions, we may take the sum of
their squares, or more simply, we may consider the symmetric function
\[
(\alpha - \beta)^2 (\gamma - \delta)^2
+ (\alpha - \gamma)^2 (\beta - \delta)^2
+ (\alpha - \delta)^2 (\beta - \gamma)^2,
\]
which, as will appear, contains the discriminant as a factor, and gives, when
equated to zero, the condition for two pairs of equal roots.

\textbf{3.} Suppose, for a quintic equation, that the condition is that there
may be a single pair of equal roots, i.e.\ $\alpha = \beta$. The function
$\prod(\alpha - \beta)^2$, where the product extends to all pairs of roots,
is the discriminant, which vanishes when any two roots are equal. This of
course gives, as the function to be equated to zero, the discriminant of the
quartic.

\textbf{4.} Suppose, for a quintic equation, that the condition is that there
may be two pairs of equal roots, i.e.\ $\alpha = \beta$, $\gamma = \delta$.
The type of the system of roots is $221$. The simplest type to be considered
is the product of all functions of the form
$(\alpha - \beta)^2 (\gamma - \delta)^2$, where $\alpha$, $\beta$ are any
two of the equal roots, and $\gamma$, $\delta$ are any other two. But this
is not a symmetric function, and the symmetric function formed from it is too
complicated. The simplest symmetric function which vanishes for this system is
\[
\sum (\alpha - \beta)^2 (\gamma - \delta)^2,
\]
where the summation extends to all distinct terms.

\textbf{5.} Suppose, for a quintic equation, that the condition is that there
may be three equal roots, i.e.\ $\alpha = \beta = \gamma$. The type is
$311$. The simplest symmetric function which vanishes for this system is
\[
\sum (\alpha - \beta)^2 (\alpha - \gamma)^2 (\beta - \gamma)^2,
\]
which is in fact the discriminant of the cubic formed by equating to zero the
first derived function of the quintic.

\textbf{6.} In order that the quintic may have two pairs of equal roots, or
that the system of roots may be $221$, the simplest type to be considered is
the product of all functions of the form
$(\alpha - \beta)^2 (\gamma - \delta)^2 (\epsilon - \zeta)^2$, where
$\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, $\delta$, $\epsilon$, $\zeta$ are the roots of
the equation. But the function in its most simple form is the symmetric
function
\[
\sum (\alpha - \beta)^2 (\gamma - \delta)^2,
\]
which vanishes for the system $221$, and gives when equated to zero the
required condition.

\textbf{Summary of Conditions for Equalities among Roots.}
\end{center}

\textbf{Quartic Equation.} The different systems of equalities are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item One pair of equal roots: type $211$. Condition: discriminant $= 0$.
\item Two pairs of equal roots: type $22$. Condition:
  $\sum (\alpha - \beta)^2 (\gamma - \delta)^2 = 0$.
\item Three equal roots: type $31$. Condition:
  $\sum (\alpha - \beta)^2 (\alpha - \gamma)^2 = 0$.
\item Four equal roots: type $4$. Condition: $b^2 - 4c = 0$, etc.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Quintic Equation.} The different systems of equalities are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item One pair of equal roots: type $2111$. Condition: discriminant $= 0$.
\item Two pairs of equal roots: type $221$. Condition:
  $\sum (\alpha - \beta)^2 (\gamma - \delta)^2 = 0$.
\item Three equal roots: type $311$. Condition:
  $\sum (\alpha - \beta)^2 (\alpha - \gamma)^2 (\beta - \gamma)^2 = 0$.
\item Three equal roots and a pair: type $32$. Condition:
  $\sum (\alpha - \beta)^2 (\alpha - \gamma)^2 (\beta - \gamma)^2
   (\delta - \epsilon)^2 = 0$.
\item Four equal roots: type $41$. Condition:
  $\sum (\alpha - \beta)^2 (\alpha - \gamma)^2 (\alpha - \delta)^2
   (\beta - \gamma)^2 (\beta - \delta)^2 (\gamma - \delta)^2 = 0$.
\item Five equal roots: type $5$. Condition: $b^2 - 4c = 0$, etc.
\end{enumerate}
