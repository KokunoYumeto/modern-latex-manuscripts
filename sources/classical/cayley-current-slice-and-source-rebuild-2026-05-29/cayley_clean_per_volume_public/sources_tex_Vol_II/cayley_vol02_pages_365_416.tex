\section*{[145]}
\subsection*{A MEMOIR UPON CAUSTICS.}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{XXVII.}
\textit{(Secondary Caustic by Refraction at a Circle is the Cartesian.)}

Suppose that rays proceeding from a point are refracted at a circle,
and let it be required to find the equation of the secondary caustic:
take the centre of the circle as origin, let $c$ be the radius,
$\xi$, $\eta$ the coordinates of the radiant point, $\alpha$,~$\beta$
the coordinates of a point upon the circle, $\mu$ the index of refraction;
the secondary caustic will be the envelope of the circle,
\[
\mu^{2} \{(x - \alpha)^{2} + (y - \beta)^{2}\}
- \{(\xi - \alpha)^{2} + (\eta - \beta)^{2}\} = 0,
\]
where $\alpha$, $\beta$ are variable parameters connected by the equation
$\alpha^{2} + \beta^{2} - c^{2} = 0$;
the equation of the circle may be written in the form
\[
\mu^{2} (x^{2} + y^{2} + c^{2}) - (\xi^{2} + \eta^{2} + c^{2})
- 2(\mu^{2}\xi - x)\alpha - 2(\mu^{2}\eta - y)\beta = 0.
\]
But in general the envelope of $A\alpha + B\beta + C = 0$,
where $\alpha$, $\beta$ are connected by the equation
$\alpha^{2} + \beta^{2} - c^{2} = 0$, is
$c^{2}(A^{2} + B^{2}) - C^{2} = 0$, and hence in the present case
the equation of the envelope is
\[
\{ \mu^{2} (x^{2} + y^{2} + c^{2}) - (\xi^{2} + \eta^{2} + c^{2}) \}^{2}
= 4c^{2} \, [ (\mu^{2}\xi - x)^{2} + (\mu^{2}\eta - y)^{2} ],
\]
which may also be written
\[
\{ \mu^{2} (x^{2} + y^{2} - c^{2}) - (\xi^{2} + \eta^{2} - c^{2}) \}^{2}
= 4c^{2}\mu^{2} \, [ (\xi - x)^{2} + (\eta - y)^{2} ].
\]
If the axis of $x$ be taken through the radiant point,
then $\eta = 0$, and writing also $\xi = a$, the equation becomes
\[
\{ \mu^{2} (x^{2} + y^{2} - c^{2}) - a^{2} + c^{2} \}^{2}
= 4c^{2}\mu^{2} \, [ (a - x)^{2} + y^{2} ];
\]
or taking the square root of each side,
\[
\mu^{2} (x^{2} + y^{2} - c^{2}) - a^{2} + c^{2}
= 2c\mu \, \surd\{ (x - a)^{2} + y^{2} \};
\]
whence multiplying by $\dfrac{1}{\mu}$ and adding on each side
$\dfrac{c}{\mu} \left( \mu - \dfrac{1}{\mu} \right) + (x - a)^{2} + y^{2}$, we have
\[
\frac{1}{\mu} \sqrt{(x - a)^{2} + y^{2}}
= \sqrt{(x - a)^{2} + y^{2} + c^{2} \left( \mu - \frac{1}{\mu} \right)^{2}},
\]
which shows that the secondary caustic is the Oval of Descartes,
or as it will be convenient to call it, the \textbf{Cartesian}.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{XXVIII.}
\textit{(Equivalent Forms of the Equation of the Cartesian; Six Systems giving
the same Caustic.)}

Reverting to the original form of the equation of the secondary
caustic, multiplying by $-\dfrac{3}{4}(1 - \mu^{2})$ and adding on each side
$-\dfrac{1}{\mu} \left( \mu - \dfrac{1}{\mu} \right) + \dfrac{1}{\mu^{2}} \{ (x - a)^{2} + y^{2} \}$,
the equation becomes
\[
\left( \frac{1}{\mu} \sqrt{(x - a)^{2} + y^{2}} \right)^{2}
- \left( \sqrt{(x - a)^{2} + y^{2} + c^{2} \left( \mu - \frac{1}{\mu} \right)^{2}} \right)^{2}
= \frac{c^{2}}{4} \left( \mu - \frac{1}{\mu} \right)^{2},
\]
or extracting the square root,
\[
\frac{1}{\mu} \sqrt{(x - a)^{2} + y^{2}}
- \sqrt{(x - a)^{2} + y^{2} + c^{2} \left( \mu - \frac{1}{\mu} \right)^{2}}
= \pm \frac{c}{2} \left( \mu - \frac{1}{\mu} \right).
\]
Combining this with the former result, we see that the equation
may be expressed indifferently in any one of the four forms.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{XXIX.}
\textit{(Particular Cases: Theorems of St.~Laurent.)}

The preceding formulae, which were first given by me in the
\textit{Philosophical Magazine}, December 1853, [124], include as particular
cases a preceding theorem with respect to the caustic by refraction
of parallel rays, and also two theorems of St.~Laurent,
\textit{Gergonne}, t.~XVII.\ [1827, pp.~1--19], \viz{} if we suppose first
that $a = c$, \ie{} that the radiant point is in the circumference
of the refracting circle, then the system~($\alpha$) shows that the
same caustic would be obtained by writing $c$, $\dfrac{a}{c\mu}$, $1$
(or what is the same thing $-1$) in the place of $c$, $c$, $\mu$,
and we have

\begin{theorem}
The caustic by refraction for a circle when the radiant point
is in the circumference is also the caustic by reflexion for the
same radiant point, and for a reflecting circle concentric with
the refracting circle, but having its radius equal to the quotient
of the radius of the refracting circle by the index of refraction.
\end{theorem}

Next, if we write $a = c\mu$, then the refracted rays all of them
pass through a point which is a double point of the secondary
caustic, the entire curve being in this case the orthogonal
trajectory, not of the refracted rays, but of the false refracted rays;
the formula~($\delta$) shows that the same caustic is obtained by
writing $\dfrac{a\alpha}{c^{2}\mu^{2}}$, $c$, $1$ (or what is the same thing $-1$)
in the place of $a$, $c$, $\mu$ ($= \dfrac{a}{c}$), and we have

\begin{theorem}
The caustic by refraction for a circle when the distance of
the radiant point from the centre is to the radius of the circle
in the ratio of the index of refraction to unity, is also the
caustic by reflexion for the same circle considered as a reflecting
circle, and for a radiant point the image of the former radiant point.
\end{theorem}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{XXX.}
\textit{(Description of the Forms of the Cartesian.)}

The curve is most easily traced by means of the preceding
construction; thus if we take the radiant point outside the
refracting circle, and consider $\mu$ as varying from a small to a
large value (positive or negative values of $\mu$ give the same curve),
we see that when $\mu$ is small the curve consists of two ovals,
one of them within and the other without the refracting circle
(see fig.~14).

As $\mu$ increases the exterior oval continually increases, but
undergoes modifications in its form; the interior oval in the
first instance diminishes until we arrive at a curve, in which
the interior oval is reduced to a conjugate point (see fig.~15);
then as $\mu$ continues to increase the interior oval reappears
(see fig.~16), and at last connects itself with the exterior oval,
so as to form a curve with a double point (see fig.~17);
and as $\mu$ increases still further the curve again breaks up
into an exterior and an interior oval (see fig.~18); and
henceforward as $\mu$ goes on increasing consists always of two ovals;
the shape of the exterior oval is best perceived from the figures.
An examination of the figures will also show how the same
curves may originate from a different refracting circle and radiant point.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{XXXI.}
\textit{(Deduction of a Theorem.)}

The theorem, ``If a variable circle have its centre upon a
circle~$S$, and its radius proportional to the tangential distance
of the centre from a circle~$C$, the envelope is a Cartesian,''
is at once deducible from the theorem ``If a variable circle
have its centre upon a circle~$S$ and its radius proportional to
the distance of the centre from a point~$C$, the locus is a Cartesian,''
which last theorem was in effect given in discussing the theory
of the secondary caustic.

In fact, the locus of a point~$P$ such that its tangential distances
from the circles $C$, $C'$ are in a constant ratio, is a circle~$S$.
Conversely, if there be a circle $C$, and the locus of $P$ be a
circle~$S$, then the circle~$C'$ may be found such that the tangential
distances of $P$ from the two circles are in a constant ratio,
and the circle~$C'$ may be taken to be a point, \ie{} if there be
a circle~$C$ and the locus of $P$ be a circle~$S$, then a point~$C$
may be found such that the tangential distance of $P$ from the
circle~$C$ is in a constant ratio to the distance from the point~$C$.
Hence treating $P$ as the centre of the variable circle, it is clear
that the variable circle is determined in the two cases by
equivalent constructions, and the envelope is therefore the same
in both cases.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{XXXIV.}
\textit{(The Cartesian as a Quartic Curve: Dirigent Circle and Directrix.)}

The Cartesian is a curve of the fourth order, symmetrical
about a certain line which it intersects in four arbitrary points,
and these points determine the curve.
Taking the line in question (which may be called the \textbf{axis})
as the axis of~$x$, and a line at right angles to it as the axis of~$y$,
let $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ be the values of~$x$ corresponding to the points
of intersection with the axis, then the equation of the curve is
\begin{multline*}
y^{2} + x^{4} - (a + b + c + d)x^{3} \\
+ \tfrac{1}{2}(a^{2} + b^{2} + c^{2} + d^{2} - 2ab - 2ac - 2ad - 2bc - 2bd - 2cd)x^{2} \\
+ (abc + abd + acd + bcd)x \\
- \tfrac{1}{4}(a^{2}b^{2} + a^{2}c^{2} + a^{2}d^{2} + b^{2}c^{2} + b^{2}d^{2} + c^{2}d^{2}
- 2abcd) = 0,
\end{multline*}
which is of the form
\[
(x^{2} + y^{2} - \alpha)^{2} + 16A(x - m) = 0,
\]
and, as already remarked, signifies that the fourth power of
the tangential distance of a point in the curve from a given circle,
is proportional to the distance of the same point from a given line.

The circle in question (which may be called the \textbf{dirigent circle})
has for its equation
\[
x^{2} + y^{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}(ab + ac + ad + bc + bd + cd) = 0;
\]
the line in question, which may be called the \textbf{directrix}, has for its
equation
\[
x = \frac{a^{2}b^{2} + a^{2}c^{2} + a^{2}d^{2} + b^{2}c^{2} + b^{2}d^{2} + c^{2}d^{2} - 2abcd}
{4(abc + abd + acd + bcd)};
\]
the multiplier of the distance from the directrix is
$abc + abd + acd + bcd$.

It may be remarked that $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ being real, the dirigent
circle is real; the equation may, in fact, be written
\[
x^{2} + y^{2} = \tfrac{1}{8} [ (a + b)^{2} + (a + c)^{2} + (a + d)^{2}
+ (b + c)^{2} + (b + d)^{2} + (c + d)^{2} ].
\]

\medskip\noindent\textbf{XXXV.}
\textit{(Discussion of the Equation: Conditions for Double Points and Cusps.)}

Considering the equation of the Cartesian under the form
\[
(x^{2} + y^{2} - \alpha)^{2} + 16A(x - m) = 0,
\]
the centre of the dirigent circle $x^{2} + y^{2} - \alpha = 0$ must
be considered as a real point, but $\alpha$ may be positive or negative,
\ie{} the radius may be either a real or a pure imaginary distance:
the coefficients $A$, $m$ must be real, the directrix is therefore
a real line.

The equation shows that for all points of the curve $x - m$
is always negative or always positive, according as $A$ is
positive or negative, \ie{} that the curve lies wholly on one side
of the directrix, \viz{} on the same side with the centre of
the dirigent circle if $A$ is positive, but on the contrary side
if $A$ is negative. In the former case the curve may be said
to be an ``inside'' curve, in the latter an ``outside'' curve.
If $m = 0$, or the directrix passes through the centre of the
dirigent circle, then the distinction between an inside curve
and an outside curve no longer exists.

It is clear that the curve touches the directrix in the points
of intersection of this line and the dirigent circle, and that
the points in question are the only points of intersection of
the curve with the directrix or the dirigent circle; hence if
the directrix and dirigent circle do not intersect, the curve
does not meet either the directrix or the dirigent circle.

To discuss the equation
\[
(x^{2} + y^{2} - \alpha)^{2} + 16A(x - m) = 0,
\]
I write first $y = 0$, which gives
\[
x^{4} - 2\alpha x^{2} + 16Ax + \alpha^{2} - 16Am = 0
\]
for the points of intersection with the axis of~$x$.
If this equation has equal roots, there will be a double point
on the axis of~$x$, and it is important to find the condition
that this may be the case.

The equation may be written in the form
\[
(1,\ 0,\ -\alpha,\ 12A,\ \alpha^{2} - 48Am \pitchfork x,\ 1)^{2} = 0,
\]
the condition for a pair of equal roots is then at once seen to be
\[
-(\alpha^{2} - 12Am)^{3} + (\alpha^{3} - 18A m \alpha + 54A^{2})^{2} = 0;
\]
or reducing and throwing out the factor~$A^{2}$,
this is
\[
27A^{2} + 2m(8m^{2} - 9\alpha)A - \alpha^{2}(m^{2} - \alpha) = 0.
\]

This equation will give two equal values for~$A$ if
\[
m^{2}(8m^{2} - 9\alpha)^{2} + 27\alpha^{2}(m^{2} - \alpha) = 0,
\]
an equation which reduces itself to
\[
(4m^{2} - 3\alpha)^{3} = 0.
\]
Hence, if $4m^{2} - 3\alpha$ be negative, \ie{} if $\alpha > \tfrac{4}{3}m^{2}$,
the values of~$A$ will be imaginary, but if $4m^{2} - 3\alpha$ be positive,
or $\alpha < \tfrac{4}{3}m^{2}$, the values of~$A$ will be real.
If $\alpha = \tfrac{4}{3}m^{2}$, then there will be two equal values of~$A$,
which in fact corresponds to a cusp upon the axis of~$x$.

Whenever the curve is real there will be at least two real
points on the axis of~$x$; and when $\alpha < \tfrac{4}{3}m^{2}$, but not
otherwise, then for properly selected values of~$A$ there will be
four real points on the axis of~$x$.

Differentiating the equation of the curve, we have
\[
\{ (x^{2} + y^{2} - \alpha)x + 4A \}\, dx + (x^{2} + y^{2} - \alpha)y\, dy = 0;
\]
and if in this equation we put $dx = 0$, we find $y = 0$,
or $x^{2} + y^{2} - \alpha = 0$, \ie{} that the points on the axis of~$x$,
and the points of intersection with the circle $x^{2} + y^{2} = \alpha$,
are the only points at which the curve is perpendicular to the axis of~$x$.

To find the points at which the curve is parallel to the axis of~$x$,
we must write $dy = 0$, this gives
\[
(x^{2} + y^{2} - \alpha)x + 4A = 0,
\]
and thence
\[
x^{2} + y^{2} - \alpha = - \frac{4A}{x};
\]
this equation will have three real roots if $A^{2} < \tfrac{4}{27}\alpha^{3}$,
and only a single real root if $A^{2} > \tfrac{4}{27}\alpha^{3}$;
for $A^{2} = \tfrac{4}{27}\alpha^{3}$, the equation in question will have
a pair of equal roots.
It is easy to see that there is always a single real root of
the equation which gives rise to a real value of~$y$, \ie{} to a
real point upon the curve; but, when the equation has three
real roots, two of the roots may or may not give rise to real
points upon the curve.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{XXXVI.}
\textit{(Tracing of the Curve.)}

It is now easy to trace the curve.

First, when $m = 0$, or the directrix passes through the centre
of the dirigent circle, the curve is here an oval bent in so as
to have double contact with the directrix, and lying on the one
or the other side of the directrix according to the sign of~$A$.
See fig.~$a$.

Next, when the directrix does not pass through the centre of
the dirigent circle, it will be convenient to suppose always that
$m$ is positive, and to consider $A$ as passing first from $0$ to $\infty$
and then from $0$ to $-\infty$, \ie{} to consider first the different
inside curves, and then the different outside curves.

Suppose $\alpha > \tfrac{4}{3}m^{2}$, the inside curve is at first an oval,
as in fig.~$b$, where (attending to one side only of the axis)
it will be noticed that there are three tangents parallel to the
axis, \viz{} one for the convexity of the oval, and two for the concavity.
For $A^{2} = \tfrac{4}{27}\alpha^{3}$ the two tangents for the concavity come
together, and give rise to a stationary tangent (\ie{} a tangent
at an inflection) parallel to the axis, and for $A^{2} > \tfrac{4}{27}\alpha^{3}$
the two tangents for the concavity disappear.
The outside curve is an oval (of course on the opposite side
of, and) bent in so as to have double contact with the directrix.

Next, if $\alpha = \tfrac{4}{3}m^{2}$, the inside curve is at first an oval,
as in fig.~$c$, and there are, as before, three tangents parallel
to the axis: for $A^{2} = \tfrac{4}{27}\alpha^{3}$, the tangents for the concavity
of the oval come to coincide with the axis, and are tangents
at a cusp, and for $A^{2} > \tfrac{4}{27}\alpha^{3}$ the cusp disappears, and
there are not for the concavity of the oval any tangents
parallel to the axis.
The outside curve is an oval as before, but smaller and more compressed.

Next, $\alpha < \tfrac{4}{3}m^{2} > m^{2}$, then the inside curve is at first
an oval, as in fig.~$d$, and there are, as before, three tangents
parallel to the axis; when $A$ attains a certain value which is
less than $\tfrac{4}{27}\alpha^{3}$, the curve acquires a double point; and
as $A$ further increases, the curve breaks up into two separate
ovals, and there are then only two tangents parallel to the axis,
\viz{} one for the exterior oval and one for the interior oval.
As $A$ continues to increase, the interior oval decreases; and
when $A$ attains a certain value which is less than $\tfrac{4}{27}\alpha^{3}$,
the interior oval reduces itself to a conjugate point, and it
afterwards disappears altogether.
The outside curve is an oval as before, but smaller and more compressed.

Next, if the directrix touch the dirigent circle, \ie{} if $\alpha = m^{2}$.
Then the inside curve is at first composed of an exterior oval
which touches the dirigent circle, and of an interior oval
which lies wholly within the dirigent circle. As $A$ increases
the interior oval decreases, reduces itself to a conjugate point,
and then disappears. The outside curve is an oval which always
touches the dirigent circle.

Next, when the directrix does not meet the dirigent circle,
\ie{} if $\alpha < m^{2}$. The inside curve consists at first of two ovals,
an exterior oval lying without the dirigent circle, and an interior
oval lying within the dirigent circle. As $A$ increases the interior
oval decreases, reduces itself to a conjugate point and disappears.
The outside curve is at first imaginary, but when $A$ attains a
sufficiently large negative value, it makes its appearance as a
conjugate point, and afterwards becomes an oval which gradually
increases.

Next, when the dirigent circle reduces itself to a point,
\ie{} if $\alpha = 0$. The inside curve makes its appearance as a
conjugate point (corresponding to $A = 0$), and as $A$ increases
it becomes an oval and continually increases. The outside curve
comports itself as in the last preceding case.

Finally, when the dirigent circle becomes imaginary, or has
for its radius a pure imaginary distance, \ie{} if $\alpha$ is negative.
The inside curve is at first imaginary, but when $A$ attains a
certain value it makes its appearance as a conjugate point,
and as $A$ increases becomes an oval and continually increases.
The outside curve, as in the preceding two cases, comports
itself in a similar manner.

The discussion, in the present section, of the different forms
of the curve is not a very full one, and a large number of
figures would be necessary in order to show completely the
transition from one form to another. The forms delineated in
the four figures were selected as forms corresponding to imaginary
values of the parameters by means of which the equation of the
curve is usually represented, e.g.\ the equations in Section~XXVIII.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{XXXVII.}
\textit{(Tangential Equation of the Caustic: the Caustic is of Class~6.)}

It has been shown that for rays proceeding from a point
and refracted at a circle, the secondary caustic is the Cartesian;
the caustic itself is therefore the evolute of the Cartesian;
this affords a means of finding the tangential equation of the caustic.

In fact, the equations which give the normal to the secondary
caustic at the point $(x, y)$ are
\[
X = \partial_{x}Z, \quad Y = \partial_{y}Z,
\]
where $Z$ is the value obtained from the equation of the secondary
caustic by writing it in the form $Z = 0$; and then writing the
equation of the normal in the form $Xx + Yy + Z = 0$, the envelope
of this line is the caustic.

The equation of the secondary caustic being
\[
(x^{2} + y^{2} - \alpha)^{2} + 16A(x - m) = 0,
\]
we have
\begin{align*}
X &= 4(x^{2} + y^{2} - \alpha)x + 16A, \\
Y &= 4(x^{2} + y^{2} - \alpha)y,
\end{align*}
and thence, writing for shortness $x^{2} + y^{2} - \alpha = P$,
\begin{align*}
X &= 4Px + 16A, \\
Y &= 4Py,
\end{align*}
and from these equations we obtain
\begin{align*}
X^{2} + Y^{2} &= 16P^{2}(x^{2} + y^{2}) + 128APx + 256A^{2}, \\
&= 16P^{2}(P + \alpha) + 128APx + 256A^{2}, \\
&= 16P^{3} + 16\alpha P^{2} + 128APx + 256A^{2}.
\end{align*}

Also $Xx + Yy = 4P(x^{2} + y^{2}) + 16Ax = 4P(P + \alpha) + 16Ax$,
and therefore
\[
Z = -\tfrac{1}{4}(X^{2} + Y^{2}) + \alpha P^{2} + 4Ax(3P + 2\alpha) + 16A^{2}.
\]

The equation $Xx + Yy + Z = 0$ becomes therefore
\begin{multline*}
Z^{6} + 2Z^{4}X(mZ^{2} - AX^{2}) + (Z^{2} + Y^{2})(mZ^{2} - AX^{2})^{2} \\
- Z^{2}Y^{2}(\alpha Z^{2} + 4AX^{2}) = 0,
\end{multline*}
where, if as before, $c$ denotes the radius of the refracting circle,
$a$ the distance of the radiant point from the centre, and $\mu$
the index of refraction, we have
\[
\alpha = -a^{2} + (1 + \mu^{2})c^{2}, \quad
A = \frac{a^{2}}{c^{2}\mu^{2}} \{ a^{2} + (1 + \mu^{2})c^{2} \}, \quad
m = \frac{1}{2c^{2}\mu^{2}} \{ 1 + (1 + \mu^{2})a^{2} \}.
\]

The above equation is the condition in order that the line
$Xx + Yy + Z = 0$ may be a normal to the secondary caustic
$(x^{2} + y^{2} - \alpha)^{2} + 16A(x - m) = 0$, or it is the tangential
equation of the caustic, which is therefore a curve of the class~6 only.
The equation may be written in the more convenient form
\begin{multline*}
Z^{6} + 2Z^{4}X(mZ^{2} - AX^{2}) + (Z^{2} + Y^{2})(mZ^{2} - AX^{2})^{2} \\
- Z^{2}Y^{2}(\alpha Z^{2} + 4AX^{2}) = 0.
\end{multline*}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{XXXVIII.}
\textit{(Comparison with the result for Caustic by Reflexion.)}

To compare the last result with that previously obtained for
the caustic by reflexion, I write $\mu = -1$, and putting also
$c = 1$ and $Z = a$ (for the equation of the reflected ray was
assumed to be $Xx + Yy + a = 0$), we have
\[
\alpha = a^{2} + 2, \quad A = \tfrac{1}{4}a, \quad m = \tfrac{1}{4}(1 + 2a^{2}).
\]
and the equation becomes, after a slight reduction,
\[
4a^{4} + 4a^{2}Z(2a^{2} + 1 - Z^{2}) + (X^{2} + Y^{2})(2a^{2} + 1 - Z^{2})^{2}
- 4a^{2}Y^{2}(a^{2} + 2 + 2Z) = 0,
\]
which may be written
\[
(2a^{2} + Z(2a^{2} + 1 - Z^{2}))^{2} + Y^{2}(-4a^{4} + 1 - 8a^{2}Z
- 2(2a^{2} + 1)Z^{2} + Z^{4}) = 0;
\]
this divides out by the factor $(Z + 1)^{2}$, and the equation then becomes,
\[
(Z^{2} - Z - 2a^{2})^{2} + Y^{2}((Z - 1)^{2} - 4a^{2}) = 0,
\]
which agrees with the result before obtained.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{XXXIX.}
\textit{(Comparison with the result for Parallel Rays refracted at a Circle.)}

Again, to compare the general equation with that previously
obtained for parallel rays refracted at a circle, we must write
$\mu = \infty$, $c = 1$, $a = \infty$, $Z = k$ (for the equation of the
refracted ray was taken to be $Xx + Yy + k = 0$); we have then
\[
\alpha = 1 + \mu^{-2} + k^{2}a^{2}, \quad
A = \tfrac{1}{4}k^{2}a^{2}, \quad
m = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 + (1 + k^{2})a^{2}),
\]
and, after the substitution, $a = \infty$.

The equation becomes in the first instance
\begin{multline*}
k^{4} + 2k^{2}Z \{ \tfrac{1}{2}(1 + (1 + k^{2})a^{2}) - \tfrac{1}{4}k^{2}a^{2} \}
+ (X^{2} + Y^{2}) \{ \tfrac{1}{2}(1 + (1 + k^{2})a^{2}) - \tfrac{1}{4}k^{2}a^{2} \}^{2} \\
- k^{2}Y^{2} \{ 1 + k^{-2} + k^{2}a^{2} + 2k^{2}aZ \} = 0;
\end{multline*}
and then putting $a = \infty$, or, what is the same thing, attending
only to the terms which involve $a^{2}$, and throwing out the constant
factor~$k^{4}$, we obtain
\[
(X^{2} + Y^{2})(X^{2} - 1 - k^{2}) - k^{2}Y^{2} = 0,
\]
or
\[
X^{2}\{X^{2} - (1 + k^{2})\} + Y^{2}\{X^{2} + 1 + k^{2}\}
(X^{2} - 1 - k^{2})(X^{2} + 1 - k^{2}) = 0,
\]
which agrees with the former result.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{XL.}
\textit{(Secondary Caustic for Parallel Rays: Equation of 8th order.)}

It was remarked that the ordinary construction for the secondary
caustic could not be applied to the case of parallel rays (the
entire curve would in fact pass off to an infinite distance),
and that the simplest course was to measure the distance~$GQ$
from a line through the centre of the refracting circle perpendicular
to the direction of the rays.

To find the equation of the resulting curve, take the centre
of the circle as the origin and the direction of the incident
rays for the axis of~$x$; let the radius of the circle be taken
equal to unity, and let $\mu$ denote, as before, the index of refraction.
Then if $\alpha$, $\beta$ are the coordinates of the point of incidence
of a ray, we have $\alpha^{2} + \beta^{2} = 1$, and considering $\alpha$,
$\beta$ as variable parameters connected by this equation, the
required curve is the envelope of the circle,
\[
\mu^{2} \{ (x - \alpha)^{2} + (y - \beta)^{2} \} - \alpha^{2} = 0.
\]
Write now $\alpha = \cos\theta$, $\beta = \sin\theta$, then multiplying the
equation by $-2$, and writing $1 + \cos 2\theta$ instead of $2\cos^{2}\theta$,
the equation becomes
\[
1 + \cos 2\theta - 2\mu^{2}(x^{2} + y^{2} - 2x\cos\theta - 2y\sin\theta + 1) = 0,
\]
which is of the form
\[
A\cos 2\theta + B\sin 2\theta + C\cos\theta + D\sin\theta + E = 0,
\]
and the values of the coefficients are
\[
A = 1, \quad B = 0, \quad C = 4\mu^{2}x, \quad D = 4\mu^{2}y, \quad
E = -2\mu^{2}(x^{2} + y^{2}) - 2\mu^{2} + 1.
\]
Substituting these values in the equation
\begin{multline*}
\{ 12(A^{2} + B^{2}) - 3(C^{2} + D^{2}) + 4E^{2} \}^{2} \\
- \{ 11A(C^{2} - D^{2}) + 5BCD - (7A^{2}(C^{2} + D^{2}) + 9(C^{4} + D^{4}))E + 8E^{3} \}^{2}
= 0,
\end{multline*}
the equation of the envelope is found to be
\begin{multline*}
16\{ (1 - \mu^{2})^{2} + 4\mu^{4} \} - (\mu^{2} + \mu^{4})(x^{2} + y^{2})
+ 16\mu^{4}(x^{2} + y^{2})^{2} \\
- (6\mu^{2} + 3\mu^{4} + 6\mu^{6})(x^{2} + y^{2}) - 27\mu^{4}(x^{2} - y^{2})
+ 4\mu^{6}(x^{2} + y^{2})^{2} = 0,
\end{multline*}
which is readily seen to be only of the 8th order.

But to simplify the result, write first
$(x^{2} + y^{2} - 1) + 1$, and $2x^{2} - 1 = (x^{2} + y^{2} - 1) + (x^{2} - y^{2})$
in the place of $x^{2} + y^{2}$ and $x^{2} - y^{2}$ respectively,
the equation becomes
\begin{multline*}
4\{ (1 - \mu^{2})^{2} - \mu^{2}(1 - \mu^{2})(x^{2} + y^{2} - 1)
+ \mu^{4}(x^{2} + y^{2} - 1)^{2} \} \\
- 16\mu^{2}(1 - \mu^{2})^{2}(x^{2} + y^{2} - 1) - 27\mu^{4}x^{2}
+ 4\mu^{4}(x^{2} + y^{2} - 1)^{2} = 0.
\end{multline*}
Write for a moment $1 - \mu^{2} = q$,
$\mu^{2}(x^{2} + y^{2} - 1) = p$, the equation becomes
\[
4(q^{2} - qp + p^{2})^{2} - 16(q^{2} - 3q^{2}p - 3qp^{2} + 2p^{2})^{2}
+ 54(q^{2} - 3q^{2}p - 3qp^{2} + 2p^{2})p^{2}x^{2} - 72p^{3}x^{2} = 0,
\]
or developing,
\begin{multline*}
4(q^{2} - qp + p^{2})^{2} - (22q^{2} - 8q^{2}p - 3qp^{2} + 2p^{2})^{2} \\
+ 54(q^{2} - 3q^{2}p - 3qp^{2} + 2p^{2})p^{2}x^{2} - 72p^{3}x^{2} = 0,
\end{multline*}
and reducing and dividing out by $27$, this gives
\[
\{ p^{2}(p - q)^{2} + 2(p + q)(2p - q)(p - 2q)p^{2}x^{2} - 27p^{3}x^{4} \} = 0,
\]
\ie{} $p = 0$, or
\[
p^{2}(p - q)^{2} + 2(p + q)(2p - q)(p - 2q)x^{2} - 27px^{4} = 0.
\]

\medskip\noindent\textbf{XLI.}
\textit{(Final Equation; Orthogonal Trajectory of Refracted Rays.)}

Whence replacing $q$, $p$ by their values, the required equation is
\begin{multline*}
(1 - \mu^{2})^{2} (x^{2} + y^{2} - 1)^{2}
\{ \mu^{2}(x^{2} + y^{2}) - 1 \}^{2} \\
+ 2(\mu^{2}(x^{2} + y^{2}) - 2\mu^{2} + 1)
(2\mu^{2}(x^{2} + y^{2}) - \mu^{4} - 1) \\
\times (\mu^{2}(x^{2} + y^{2}) - 2 + \mu^{2}) x^{2} - 27\mu^{2}x^{4} = 0,
\end{multline*}
which is the equation of an orthogonal trajectory of the refracted rays.
In the case of reflexion, $\mu = -1$, and the equation becomes
\[
4(x^{2} + y^{2} - 1)^{3} - 27x^{2} = 0.
\]
Comparing this with the equation of the caustic, it is easy to see,

\begin{theorem}
In the case of parallel rays and a reflecting circle, there is a
secondary caustic which is a curve similar to and double the
magnitude of the caustic, the position of the two curves differing
by a right angle.
\end{theorem}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{XLI.}
\textit{(System of Orthogonal Trajectories.)}

The entire system of the orthogonal trajectories of the refracted
rays might in like manner be determined by finding the envelope
of the circle (where, as before, $\alpha$, $\beta$ are variable parameters
connected by the equation $\alpha^{2} + \beta^{2} = 1$),
\begin{multline*}
(3 - 12[\mu^{2} + 2\mu^{2}\xi x + \mu^{2}(x^{2} + y^{2})]
+ [1 - 2\mu^{2} + 2\mu^{4} - 2\mu^{2}(x^{2} + y^{2})]^{2})^{2} \\
- ([1 - 2\mu^{2} + 2\mu^{4} - 2\mu^{2}(x^{2} + y^{2})]
[9 + 18\mu^{2} + 36\mu^{2}x + 18\mu^{2}(x^{2} + y^{2})] \\
- 54[\mu^{2} + 2\mu^{2}\xi x + \mu^{2}(x^{2} - 3y^{2})]
- [1 - 2\mu^{2} + 2\mu^{4} - 2\mu^{2}(x^{2} + y^{2})]^{2})^{2} = 0,
\end{multline*}
which, it is easy to see, is an equation of the order~8 only.

\section*{[146]}
\subsection*{A MEMOIR ON CURVES OF THE THIRD ORDER.}

\noindent
[From the \textit{Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London},
vol.~CXLVII.\ for the year 1857, pp.~415--446.
Received October~30,---Read December~11, 1856.]

\medskip
A \textsc{curve} of the third order, or cubic curve, is the locus
represented by an equation such as $U = (\,\pitchfork\!\,\mathord{\mkern1mu\vrule\mkern1mu}\, x, y, z \,\mathord{\mkern1mu\vrule\mkern1mu}\,)^{3} = 0$;
and it appears by my ``Third Memoir on Quantics,'' [144],
that it is proper to consider, in connexion with the curve of
the third order $U = 0$, and its Hessian $HU = 0$ (which is also
a curve of the third order), two curves of the third class, \viz{}
the curves represented by the equations $PU = 0$ and $QU = 0$.

These equations, I say, represent curves of the third class;
in fact, $PU$ and $QU$ are contravariants of~$U$, and therefore,
when the variables $x$, $y$, $z$ of $U$ are considered as point
coordinates, the variables $\xi$, $\eta$, $\zeta$ of $PU$ and $QU$ must
be considered as line coordinates, and the curves will be curves
of the third class.

I propose (in analogy with the form of the word Hessian) to
call the two curves in question the \textbf{Pippian} and \textbf{Quippian}
respectively.

\medskip
[The curve $PU = 0$ is now usually called the \textbf{Cayleyan}.]

\medskip
A geometrical definition of the Pippian was readily found;
the curve is in fact Steiner's curve mentioned in the memoir
``Allgemeine Eigenschaften der algebraischen Curven,''
\textit{Crelle}, t.~XLVII.\ [1854] pp.~1--6, in the particular case of a
basis-curve of the third order; and I also found that the
Pippian might be considered as occurring implicitly in my
``M\'{e}moire sur les courbes du troisi\`{e}me ordre,'' \textit{Liouville},
t.~IX.\ [1844] pp.~285--291 [26] and ``Nouvelles remarques
sur les courbes du troisi\`{e}me ordre,'' \textit{Liouville}, t.~X.\ [1845]
pp.~102--109 [27].

As regards the Quippian, I have not succeeded in obtaining
a satisfactory geometrical definition; but the search after it
led to a variety of theorems, relating chiefly to the first-mentioned
curve, and the results of the investigation are contained in the
present memoir. Some of these results are due to Mr~Salmon,
with whom I was in correspondence on the subject. The character
of the results makes it difficult to develope them in a systematic
order; but the results are given in such connexion one with
another as I have been able to present them.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Article No.~1. Definitions, \&c.}

\medskip
1. It may be convenient to premise as follows:---Considering,
in connexion with a curve of the third order or cubic, a point,
we have

\smallskip
\hspace*{2em}(a) The first or conic polar of the point.

\hspace*{2em}(b) The second or line polar of the point.

\smallskip
The meaning of these terms is well known, and they require
no explanation.

Next, considering, in connexion with the cubic, a line---

\smallskip
\hspace*{2em}(c) The first or conic polars of each point of the line meet
in four points, which are the four poles of the line.

\hspace*{2em}(d) The second or line polars of each point of the line
envelope a conic, which is the lineo-polar envelope of the line.

\smallskip
And reciprocally considering, in connexion with a curve of
the third class, a line, we have

\smallskip
\hspace*{2em}(e) The first or conic pole of the line.

\hspace*{2em}(f) The second or point-pole of the line.

\smallskip
And considering, in connexion with the curve of the third
class, a point---

\smallskip
\hspace*{2em}(g) The first or conic poles of each line through the point
touch four lines, which are the four polars of the point.

\hspace*{2em}(h) The second or point poles of each line through the point
generate a conic which is the point-pole locus of the point.

\smallskip
But I shall not have occasion in the present memoir to speak
of these reciprocal figures, except indeed the first or conic pole
of the line.

The term \textbf{conjugate poles} of a cubic is used to denote two
points, such that the first or conic polar of either of them,
with respect to the cubic, is a pair of lines passing through
the other of them.

Reciprocally, the term \textbf{conjugate polars} of a curve of the
third class denotes two lines, such that the first or conic pole
of either of them, with respect to the curve of the third class,
is a pair of points lying in the other of them.

\medskip
2. The expression, a \textbf{syzygetic cubic}, used in reference to
two cubics, denotes a curve of the third order passing through
the points of intersection of the two cubics; but in the present
memoir the expression is in general used in reference to a single
cubic, to denote a curve of the third order passing through the
points of intersection of the cubic and its Hessian.

As regards curves of the third class, I use in the memoir
the full expression, a curve of the third class syzygetically
connected with two given curves of the third class.

It is a well-known theorem, that if at the points of intersection
of a given line with a given cubic tangents are drawn to the
cubic, these tangents again meet the cubic in three points
which lie in a line; such line is in the present memoir termed
the \textbf{satellite line} of the given line, and the point of intersection
of the two lines is termed the \textbf{satellite point} of the given line;
the given line in reference to its satellite line or point is termed
the \textbf{primary line}.

In particular, if the primary line be a tangent of the cubic,
the satellite line coincides with the primary line, and the
satellite point is the point of simple intersection of the primary
line and the cubic.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Article No.~2. Group of Theorems relating to the Conjugate
Poles of a Cubic.}

\medskip
3. The theorems which I have first to mention relate to or
originate out of the theory of the conjugate poles of a cubic,
and may be conveniently connected together and explained by
means of the accompanying figure.

The point $E$ is a point of the Hessian; this being so, its
first or conic polar, with respect to the cubic, will be a pair
of lines passing through a point $F$ of the Hessian; and not
only so, but the first or conic polar of the point $F$, with respect
to the cubic will be a pair of lines passing through $E$.

The pair of lines through $F$ are represented in the figure by
$FBA$, $FDC$, and the pair of lines through $E$ are represented
by $EGA$, $EDB$, and the lines of the one pair meet the lines
of the other pair in the points $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$.

The point $O$, which is the intersection of the lines $AD$, $BC$,
is a point of the Hessian, and joining $EO$, $FO$, these lines
are tangents to the Hessian at the points $E$, $F$, that is, the
points $E$, $F$ are corresponding points of the Hessian, in the
sense that the tangents to the Hessian at these points meet
in a point of the Hessian.

The two points $E$, $F$ are, according to a preceding definition,
conjugate poles of the cubic.

The line $EF$ meets the Hessian in a third point $G$, and the
points $O$, $G$ are conjugate poles of the cubic.

The first or conic polar of $G$, with respect to the cubic, is
the pair of lines $AOD$, $BOC$ meeting in $O$.

The first or conic polar of $O$, with respect to the cubic, is
the pair of lines $GEF$ and $Ge'e'$ meeting in $G$.

The four poles of the line $EO$, with respect to the cubic, are
the points of intersection of the first or conic polars of the two
points $E$ and $O$, that is, the four poles in question are the
points $F$, $f'$, $e$, $e'$.

Similarly, the four poles of the line $FO$, with respect to the
cubic, are the points $E$, $E'$, $f$, $f'$.

The line $EF$, that is, any line joining two conjugate poles
of the cubic, is a tangent to the Pippian, and the point of
contact $T$ is the harmonic with respect to the points $E$, $F$
(which are points on the Hessian) of $G$, the third point of
intersection with the Hessian.

Conversely, any tangent of the Pippian meets the Hessian
in three points, two of which are conjugate poles of the cubic,
and the point of contact is the harmonic, with respect to these
two points, of the third point of intersection with the Hessian.

The line $GO$ in the figure is of course also a tangent of the
Pippian, and moreover the lines $FBA$, $FDC$ (that is, the pair
of lines which are the first or conic polar of~$E$) and the lines
$EGA$, $EDB$ (that is, the pair of lines which are the first or
conic polar of~$F$) touch the Pippian, the points of contact
being the intersections of these lines with the satellite lines
of the lines $EF$, $EG$ respectively.

It will be presently seen that the analytical theory leads to
the consideration of a line $IJ$ (not represented in the figure):
the line in question is the polar of $E$ (or $F$) with respect to
the conic which is the first or conic polar of $F$ (or $E$) with
respect to any syzygetic cubic.

The line $IJ$ is a tangent of the Pippian, and moreover the
lines $EF$ and $IJ$ are conjugate polars of a curve of the third
class syzygetically connected with the Pippian and Quippian,
and which is moreover such that its Hessian is the Pippian.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Article Nos.~3 to 19. Analytical investigations, comprising
the proof of the theorems, Article No.~2.}

\medskip
4. The analytical theory possesses considerable interest.
Take as the equation of the cubic,
\[
U = x^{3} + y^{3} + z^{3} + 6lxyz = 0;
\]
then the equation of the Hessian is
\[
HU = l^{2}(x^{3} + y^{3} + z^{3}) - (1 + 2l^{3})xyz = 0;
\]
and the equation of the Pippian in line coordinates (that is,
the equation which expresses that $\xi x + \eta y + \zeta z = 0$
is a tangent of the curve) is
\[
PU = -l(\xi^{3} + \eta^{3} + \zeta^{3}) + (-1 + 4l^{3})\xi\eta\zeta = 0.
\]

The equation of the Quippian in line coordinates is
\[
QU = (1 - 10l^{3})(\xi^{3} + \eta^{3} + \zeta^{3}) - 6l^{2}(9 + 4l^{3})\xi\eta\zeta = 0;
\]
and the values of the two invariants of the cubic form are
\[
S = -l + l^{4}, \quad T = 1 - 20l^{3} - 8l^{6},
\]
values which give identically,
\[
T^{2} - 64S^{3} = (1 + 8l^{3})^{3},
\]
the last-mentioned function being in fact the discriminant.

\medskip
5. Suppose that the cubic $U = 0$ is represented in the
canonical form
\[
U = x^{3} + y^{3} + z^{3} + 6lxyz = 0.
\]
The Hessian is
\[
HU = l^{2}(x^{3} + y^{3} + z^{3}) - (1 + 2l^{3})xyz = 0.
\]
A syzygetic cubic is
\[
\Theta U + \Phi HU = 0,
\]
where $\Theta$, $\Phi$ are arbitrary parameters; or substituting,
\[
(\Theta l^{2} + \Phi)(x^{3} + y^{3} + z^{3}) + (6\Theta l - 2\Phi l^{3})xyz = 0.
\]
The condition that this may be a pair of lines is that the
discriminant may vanish, \ie{} that
\[
(\Theta l^{2} + \Phi)^{3} - (6\Theta l - 2\Phi l^{3})^{3} = 0,
\]
or, what is the same thing,
\[
(\Theta l^{2} + \Phi) - (6\Theta l - 2\Phi l^{3}) = 0,
\]
or
\[
\Theta(l^{2} - 6l) + \Phi(1 + 2l^{3}) = 0.
\]

\medskip
6. Let $E = (x_{1}, y_{1}, z_{1})$ be a point of the Hessian; then
\[
l^{2}(x_{1}^{3} + y_{1}^{3} + z_{1}^{3}) - (1 + 2l^{3})x_{1}y_{1}z_{1} = 0.
\]
The first or conic polar of $E$ is
\[
x_{1}(x^{2} + 2lyz) + y_{1}(y^{2} + 2lzx) + z_{1}(z^{2} + 2lxy) = 0.
\]
This is a pair of lines meeting in the point $F = (x_{2}, y_{2}, z_{2})$,
where
\begin{align*}
x_{2} &= y_{1}z_{1} - lx_{1}^{2}, \\
y_{2} &= z_{1}x_{1} - ly_{1}^{2}, \\
z_{2} &= x_{1}y_{1} - lz_{1}^{2}.
\end{align*}

And since $E$ is a point of the Hessian, it follows that $F$ is
also a point of the Hessian, and that the first or conic polar
of $F$ is a pair of lines meeting in~$E$; that is, $E$ and $F$ are
conjugate poles of the cubic.

\medskip
7. The coordinates of the point~$F$ being as above, we have
\begin{align*}
x_{1} &= y_{2}z_{2} - lx_{2}^{2}, \\
y_{1} &= z_{2}x_{2} - ly_{2}^{2}, \\
z_{1} &= x_{2}y_{2} - lz_{2}^{2},
\end{align*}
and the line $EF$ meets the Hessian in a third point $G$, the
coordinates of which are
\[
x_{3} : y_{3} : z_{3} = x_{1}y_{1}z_{2} - x_{2}y_{2}z_{1}
: y_{1}z_{1}x_{2} - y_{2}z_{2}x_{1}
: z_{1}x_{1}y_{2} - z_{2}x_{2}y_{1}.
\]

The first or conic polar of $G$ is the pair of lines $AOD$, $BOC$
meeting in the point $O$, where $O$ is the intersection of the
lines $AD$, $BC$.

\medskip
8. The point $O$ is the pole of the line $EF$ with respect to
the conic which is the first or conic polar of $E$ (or $F$) with
respect to any syzygetic cubic; and the line $GO$ is a tangent
to the Pippian.

The line $IJ$, which is the polar of $E$ (or $F$) with respect
to the conic which is the first or conic polar of $F$ (or $E$)
with respect to any syzygetic cubic, is also a tangent to the
Pippian.

\medskip
9. The points $E$, $F$ being conjugate poles of the cubic,
the line $EF$ is a tangent to the Pippian. The point of contact~$T$
is given by the harmonic condition
\[
(E, F; T, G) = -1,
\]
where $G$ is the third point in which the line $EF$ meets the Hessian.

Conversely, any tangent of the Pippian meets the Hessian
in three points $E$, $F$, $G$; two of these, say $E$, $F$, are
conjugate poles of the cubic, and the point of contact $T$ is
the harmonic of $G$ with respect to $E$, $F$.

\medskip
10. The coordinates of the point of contact $T$ of the tangent
$EF$ of the Pippian are
\begin{align*}
x_{t} &= x_{1}(y_{2}z_{3} + y_{3}z_{2}) + x_{2}(y_{3}z_{1} + y_{1}z_{3})
+ x_{3}(y_{1}z_{2} + y_{2}z_{1}), \\
y_{t} &= y_{1}(z_{2}x_{3} + z_{3}x_{2}) + y_{2}(z_{3}x_{1} + z_{1}x_{3})
+ y_{3}(z_{1}x_{2} + z_{2}x_{1}), \\
z_{t} &= z_{1}(x_{2}y_{3} + x_{3}y_{2}) + z_{2}(x_{3}y_{1} + x_{1}y_{3})
+ z_{3}(x_{1}y_{2} + x_{2}y_{1}).
\end{align*}

These may be written in the more convenient form
\begin{align*}
x_{t} &= \partial_{\xi}PU, \\
y_{t} &= \partial_{\eta}PU, \\
z_{t} &= \partial_{\zeta}PU,
\end{align*}
where $PU = 0$ is the equation of the Pippian in line coordinates,
and $\xi$, $\eta$, $\zeta$ are the coordinates of the line $EF$.

\medskip
11. The Pippian may be defined geometrically as the envelope
of the line $EF$, where $E$, $F$ are conjugate poles of the cubic;
or, what is the same thing, as the locus of the point $T$, the
harmonic of $G$ with respect to $E$, $F$.

\medskip
12. The equation of the Pippian in point coordinates is
obtained by eliminating $\xi$, $\eta$, $\zeta$ between the equations
\[
\xi x + \eta y + \zeta z = 0, \quad PU = 0, \quad \partial_{\xi}PU = 0, \quad
\partial_{\eta}PU = 0, \quad \partial_{\zeta}PU = 0.
\]
This gives
\begin{multline*}
(x^{3} + y^{3} + z^{3})^{2} + (2 + 16l^{3})(x^{3} + y^{3} + z^{3})xyz \\
+ (1 - 20l^{3} - 8l^{6})x^{2}y^{2}z^{2} = 0,
\end{multline*}
which is a curve of the sixth order.

\medskip
13. The Pippian has nine cusps, which are the nine points
of inflection of the cubic. The cuspidal tangent at each cusp
is the harmonic polar of the corresponding point of inflection.

\medskip
14. The Pippian has three double points, which are the
points of the Hessian corresponding to the three points of the
cubic at which the tangent meets the cubic in three consecutive
points (\ie{} the sextactic points).

\medskip
15. The Quippian is the locus of a point $P$ such that the
point-polar locus of $P$ (with respect to the curve of the third
class $PU = 0$) is a conic; or, what is the same thing, it is
the envelope of the line which is the first or conic polar of
the point-polar locus of a variable point.

\medskip
16. The equation of the Quippian in line coordinates is
\[
QU = (1 - 10l^{3})(\xi^{3} + \eta^{3} + \zeta^{3}) - 6l^{2}(9 + 4l^{3})\xi\eta\zeta = 0,
\]
and in point coordinates it is a curve of the sixth order.

\medskip
17. The Quippian has nine cusps and three double points,
similar to the Pippian, but the positions of these singular points
are different.

\medskip
18. The Hessian of the cubic is the locus of a point such
that its first or conic polar is a pair of intersecting lines.
The Steinerian of the cubic is the locus of a point such that
its first or conic polar is a pair of coincident lines. The
Steinerian is the reciprocal of the Pippian.

\medskip
19. The relations between the different curves are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item[$\bullet$] The \textbf{Hessian} is a curve of the 3rd order; it is the
locus of a point whose first or conic polar is a pair of intersecting lines.
\item[$\bullet$] The \textbf{Steinerian} is a curve of the 3rd order; it is the
locus of a point whose first or conic polar is a pair of coincident lines.
\item[$\bullet$] The \textbf{Pippian} is a curve of the 3rd class; it is the
envelope of the line joining two conjugate poles.
\item[$\bullet$] The \textbf{Quippian} is a curve of the 3rd order; it is the
locus of the first or conic polar of the point-polar locus of a variable point.
\item[$\bullet$] The \textbf{Cayleyan} is identical with the Pippian.
\end{itemize}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Article Nos.~20 to 28. Further Developments.}

\medskip
20. The theorems of Hesse relating to the Hessian have
their analogues in relation to the Pippian. Thus, Hesse has
shown that the Hessian of a cubic passes through the nine
points of inflection of the cubic; and in like manner, the Pippian
touches the nine harmonic polars of the nine points of inflection.

\medskip
21. Hesse has shown that the first or conic polar of any
point of the Hessian is a pair of lines; and in like manner,
the first or conic pole of any tangent of the Pippian is a pair
of points.

\medskip
22. Hesse has shown that the second or line polar of any
point of the Hessian is a line touching the Hessian; and in
like manner, the second or line pole of any tangent of the
Pippian is a point of the Pippian.

\medskip
23. Hesse has shown that the Hessian is the locus of a
point whose first or conic polar is a pair of lines meeting in
a point of the Hessian; and in like manner, the Pippian is
the envelope of a line whose first or conic pole is a pair of
points lying on a tangent of the Pippian.

\medskip
24. Hesse has shown that the Hessian of a cubic and the
cubic itself have the same points of inflection; and in like
manner, the Pippian of a cubic and the cubic itself have the
same satellite lines.

\medskip
25. The theory of the Pippian may be extended to curves
of higher order. For a curve of the $n$th order, the Pippian
is a curve of the $3(n-2)$th class; and for a curve of the $n$th
class, the Pippian is a curve of the $3(n-2)$th order.

\medskip
26. The Pippian of a curve of the fourth order is a curve
of the sixth class; it has 24 cusps and 12 double points. The
Pippian of a curve of the fourth class is a curve of the sixth
order; it has 24 cusps and 12 double points.

\medskip
27. The theory of the Pippian is intimately connected with
the theory of covariants and contravariants. The Pippian is
in fact a contravariant of the cubic; and the theory of the
Pippian may be regarded as a chapter in the general theory of
covariants and contravariants.

\medskip
28. The results of the present memoir may be summarized
as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The Pippian is a curve of the third class, which may be
defined geometrically as the envelope of the line joining two
conjugate poles of the cubic.
\item The Quippian is a curve of the third order, which may be
defined geometrically as the locus of the first or conic polar of
the point-pole locus of a variable point.
\item The Pippian and Quippian are contravariants of the cubic;
their equations in the canonical form are
\[PU = -l(\xi^{3} + \eta^{3} + \zeta^{3}) + (-1 + 4l^{3})\xi\eta\zeta = 0,\]
and
\[QU = (1 - 10l^{3})(\xi^{3} + \eta^{3} + \zeta^{3}) - 6l^{2}(9 + 4l^{3})\xi\eta\zeta = 0.\]
\item The Pippian has nine cusps (the points of inflection of the
cubic) and three double points (the sextactic points).
\item The Quippian has nine cusps and three double points, but
these are in general distinct from those of the Pippian.
\item The Hessian and Steinerian are related to the Pippian in
the same way that the Hessian is related to the cubic itself.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Article Nos.~29 to 31. Formulae for the intersection of a
cubic curve and a line.}

\medskip
29. If the cubic $U = x^{3} + y^{3} + z^{3} + 6lxyz = 0$
intersects the line $\xi x + \eta y + \zeta z = 0$ in the three points
$(x_{1}, y_{1}, z_{1})$, $(x_{2}, y_{2}, z_{2})$, $(x_{3}, y_{3}, z_{3})$, then the coordinates
of these points are given by the equations
\begin{align*}
x_{1} + x_{2} + x_{3} &= 0, \\
y_{1} + y_{2} + y_{3} &= 0, \\
z_{1} + z_{2} + z_{3} &= 0, \\
x_{1}y_{1}z_{1} + x_{2}y_{2}z_{2} + x_{3}y_{3}z_{3} &= 0.
\end{align*}

\medskip
30. The equation of the three tangents at the points of
intersection is
\begin{multline*}
\Pi = [(x_{1}^{2} + 2ly_{1}z_{1})x + (y_{1}^{2} + 2lz_{1}x_{1})y + (z_{1}^{2} + 2lx_{1}y_{1})z] \\
\times [(x_{2}^{2} + 2ly_{2}z_{2})x + (y_{2}^{2} + 2lz_{2}x_{2})y + (z_{2}^{2} + 2lx_{2}y_{2})z] \\
\times [(x_{3}^{2} + 2ly_{3}z_{3})x + (y_{3}^{2} + 2lz_{3}x_{3})y + (z_{3}^{2} + 2lx_{3}y_{3})z] = 0.
\end{multline*}

\medskip
31. Now considering the equation
\[
F \cdot U - \Pi = (\xi' x + \eta' y + \zeta' z)(\xi'' x + \eta'' y + \zeta'' z),
\]
in order that this may be identically true, we must have
\begin{align*}
\xi'\xi'' &= F \cdot 1 - (x_{1}^{2} + 2ly_{1}z_{1})(x_{2}^{2} + 2ly_{2}z_{2})(x_{3}^{2} + 2ly_{3}z_{3}), \\
\eta'\eta'' &= F \cdot 1 - (y_{1}^{2} + 2lz_{1}x_{1})(y_{2}^{2} + 2lz_{2}x_{2})(y_{3}^{2} + 2lz_{3}x_{3}), \\
\zeta'\zeta'' &= F \cdot 1 - (z_{1}^{2} + 2lx_{1}y_{1})(z_{2}^{2} + 2lx_{2}y_{2})(z_{3}^{2} + 2lx_{3}y_{3}),
\end{align*}
and similarly for the other coefficients.

The coefficient of $x^{3}$ in $F \cdot U - \Pi$ is simply $F$, which
must therefore be equal to the product of the coefficients of~$x$
in the three tangent lines; that is,
\[
F = (x_{1}^{2} + 2ly_{1}z_{1})(x_{2}^{2} + 2ly_{2}z_{2})(x_{3}^{2} + 2ly_{3}z_{3}).
\]

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Article Nos.~32 to 40. The satellite line and satellite point.}

\medskip
32. The satellite line of a given line with respect to the
cubic is the line which passes through the three points in which
the tangents at the points of intersection of the given line with
the cubic again meet the cubic.

\medskip
33. The satellite point of a given line is the point of
intersection of the given line and its satellite line.

\medskip
34. If the given line is $\xi x + \eta y + \zeta z = 0$, then the
satellite line is given by the equation
\[
\partial_{\xi}PU \cdot x + \partial_{\eta}PU \cdot y + \partial_{\zeta}PU \cdot z = 0,
\]
where $PU = 0$ is the equation of the Pippian.

\medskip
35. The satellite point of the line $\xi x + \eta y + \zeta z = 0$
is the point whose coordinates are
\[
x : y : z = \partial_{\xi}PU : \partial_{\eta}PU : \partial_{\zeta}PU.
\]

\medskip
36. The primary line and its satellite line are conjugate
polars of a curve of the third class syzygetically connected
with the Pippian and Quippian; and the satellite point is the
pole of the primary line with respect to the conic which is the
first or conic polar of the satellite line with respect to any
syzygetic cubic.

\medskip
37. If the primary line is a tangent of the cubic, the satellite
line coincides with the primary line, and the satellite point is
the point of simple intersection of the primary line and the cubic.

\medskip
38. The envelope of the satellite line of a variable line is
the Pippian; and the locus of the satellite point of a variable
line is the Quippian.

\medskip
39. The satellite line of the line joining two conjugate poles
of the cubic is the tangent to the Pippian at the point of contact
of the line joining the two poles.

\medskip
40. The satellite point of the line joining two conjugate
poles is the point of contact of the line with the Pippian.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Article No.~41. Recapitulation of geometrical definitions of the Pippian.}

\medskip
In conclusion, I will recapitulate the different modes of
generation of the Pippian.

\smallskip
\hspace*{2em}(1) The Pippian is the envelope of the first or conic pole
of the lineo-polar envelope of a variable line.

\hspace*{2em}(2) The Pippian is the locus of the first or conic pole of
the lineo-polar envelope of a variable line.

\hspace*{2em}(3) The Pippian is the envelope of the line which is the
first or conic pole of the lineo-polar envelope of a variable line.

\hspace*{2em}(4) The Pippian is the locus of the line which is the first
or conic pole of the lineo-polar envelope of a variable line.

\hspace*{2em}(5) The Pippian is the envelope of the second or line polar
of the first or conic pole of a variable conic.

\hspace*{2em}(6) The Pippian is the locus of the second or line polar of
the first or conic pole of a variable conic.

\hspace*{2em}(7) The Pippian is the envelope of the line which is the
second or line polar of the first or conic pole of a variable conic.

\hspace*{2em}(8) The Pippian is the locus of the line which is the second
or line polar of the first or conic pole of a variable conic.

\hspace*{2em}(9) The Pippian is the envelope of the line which is the
lineo-polar envelope of a variable point.

\smallskip
These nine definitions are all equivalent to each other, and
each of them leads to the same curve, the Pippian of the given cubic.
