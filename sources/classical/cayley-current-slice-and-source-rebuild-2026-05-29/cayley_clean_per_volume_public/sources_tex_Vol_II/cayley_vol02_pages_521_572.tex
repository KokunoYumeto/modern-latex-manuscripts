\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{155. A Fourth Memoir upon Quantics (continued)}

\begin{center}
\Large\textbf{A FOURTH MEMOIR UPON QUANTICS.}
\end{center}

\setcounter{subsection}{80}

\subsection{}

It was noticed in the Introductory Memoir, that, by Joachimsthal's theorem, the discriminant, on putting $a = 0$, becomes divisible by $b^2$, and that throwing out this factor it is to a numerical factor \textit{pr\`es} the discriminant of the quantic of the order $(m-1)$ obtained by putting $a = 0$ and throwing out the factor $x$; and it was also remarked, that this theorem, combined with the general property of invariants, afforded a convenient method for the calculation of the discriminant of a quantic when that of the order immediately preceding is known. Thus let it be proposed to find the discriminant of the cubic
\[
(a, b, c, d\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^3.
\]

Imagine the discriminant expanded in powers of the leading coefficient $a$ in the form
\[
Aa^2 + Ba + C,
\]
then this function \textit{qu\`a} invariant must be reduced to zero by the operation $3b\partial_a + 2c\partial_b + d\partial_c$; or putting for shortness $\nabla = 2c\partial_b + d\partial_c$, the operation is $\nabla + 3b\partial_a$, and we have
\[
\left.\begin{aligned}
a^2 \nabla A &+ a\nabla B + \nabla C\\
&+ a\, 6bA + 3bB
\end{aligned}\right\} = 0,
\]
and consequently
\[
B = -\frac{1}{3b}\nabla C, \quad A = -\frac{1}{6b}\nabla B, \quad \nabla A = 0.
\]

But $C$ is equal to $b^2$ into the discriminant of $(3b, 3c, d\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^2$, that is, its value is $b^2(12bd - 9c^2)$, or throwing out the factor $3$, we may write
\[
C = 4b^2d - 3b^2c^2;
\]
this gives
\[
B = -\frac{1}{3b}(-6b^2cd + 24b^2cd - 12bc^3),
\]
or reducing
\[
B = -6bcd + 4c^3;
\]
and thence
\[
A = -\frac{1}{6b}(-6bd^2 + 12c^2d - 12c^2d),
\]
or reducing
\[
A = d^2,
\]
which verifies the equation $\nabla A = 0$, and the discriminant is, as we know,
\[
a^2d^2 - 6abcd + 4ac^3 + 4b^3d - 3b^2c^2.
\]

\subsection{}

If we consider the quantic $(a, b, \ldots \,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, 1)^m$ as expressed in terms of the roots in the form $a(x - \alpha y)(x - \beta y)\ldots$, then the discriminant ($= a^{2m-2}\alpha^{m-1} + \&c.$ as above) is to a factor \textit{pr\`es} equal to the product of the squares of the differences of the roots, and the factor may be determined as follows: viz. denoting by $\zeta(\alpha, \beta, \ldots)$ the product of the squares of the differences of the roots, we may write
\[
a^{2m-2}\,\zeta(\alpha, \beta, \ldots) = N(a^{m-1}\alpha^{m-1} + \&c.),
\]
where $N$ is a number; and then considering the equation $a^{m-1} = 0$, we have to determine $N$ the equation
\[
\zeta(\alpha, \beta, \ldots) = (-)^{m-1}N\,.
\]

But in general
\[
\zeta(\alpha, \beta, \ldots) = (-)^{\frac{1}{2}m(m-1)}(\alpha - \beta)(\alpha - \gamma)\ldots(\beta - \alpha)(\beta - \gamma)\ldots,
\]
and if
\[
\phi x = (x - \alpha)(x - \beta)\ldots,
\]
then
\[
(\alpha - \beta)(\alpha - \gamma)\ldots = \phi'\alpha, \quad \&c.,
\]
or
\[
\zeta(\alpha, \beta, \ldots) = (-)^{\frac{1}{2}m(m-1)}\phi'\alpha\,\phi'\beta\ldots;
\]
here
\[
\phi'\alpha\,\phi'\beta\ldots = m^2 a^{m-2}(\alpha\beta\gamma\ldots)^{m-1},
\]
but
\[
(-)^m a\,\alpha\beta\gamma\ldots = -1,
\]
or
\[
\alpha\beta\gamma\ldots = (-)^{m-1}\frac{1}{a},
\]
and
\[
\phi'\alpha\,\phi'\beta\ldots = (-)^{m(m-1)}\frac{1}{a^{m-2}}\,m^m = (-)^{m-1}\frac{m^m}{a^{m-2}};
\]
whence
\[
\zeta(\alpha, \beta, \ldots) = (-)^{\frac{1}{2}m(m-1)+m(m-1)}\frac{m^m}{a^{m-2}} = (-)^{\frac{1}{2}m(m-1)}\frac{m^m}{a^{m-2}},
\]
or
\[
a^{m-2}\zeta(\alpha, \beta, \ldots) = (-)^{\frac{1}{2}m(m-1)}m^m,
\]
and consequently
\[
a^{2m-2}\zeta(\alpha, \beta, \ldots) = (-)^{\frac{1}{2}m(m-1)}m^m\,a^{m-1};
\]
so that the discriminant of the quantic is equal to the product of the squares of the differences of the roots multiplied by $(-)^{\frac{1}{2}m(m-1)}m^m\,a^{m-1}$.

It would have been allowable to define the discriminant so as that the leading term should be $(-)^{\frac{1}{2}m(m-1)}m^m\,a^{m-1}$, in which case the discriminant would have constantly the same sign as the product of the squared differences; but I have upon the whole thought it better to make the leading term of the discriminant always positive.

\subsection{}

A quantic of an even order $2p$ has an invariant of peculiar simplicity, viz. the determinant the terms of which are the coefficients of the $p$th differential coefficients, or derived functions of the quantic with respect to the facients; such invariant may also be considered as a multipartite invariant of the $p$th emanants. Thus the sextic
\[
(a, b, c, d, e, f, g\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^6
\]
has for one of its invariants, the determinant
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
a & b & c & d \\
b & c & d & e \\
c & d & e & f \\
d & e & f & g
\end{vmatrix}.
\]

The invariant in question is termed by Professor Sylvester the \textbf{Catalecticant}.

\subsection{}

Professor Sylvester also remarked, that we may from the catalecticant form a function containing an indeterminate quantity $\lambda$, such that the coefficients of the different powers of $\lambda$ are invariants of the quantic; thus for the sextic, the function in question is
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
a & b & c & d - \lambda \\
b & c & d + \lambda & e \\
c & d - \lambda & e & f \\
d + \lambda & e & f & g
\end{vmatrix},
\]
where the law of formation is manifest; the terms in the sinister diagonal are modified by annexing to their numerical submultiples of $\lambda$ with the signs $+$ and $-$ alternately, and in which the multipliers are the reciprocals of the binomial coefficients. The function so obtained is termed the \textbf{Lambdaic}.

\subsection{}

If we consider a quantic of an odd order, and form the catalecticant of the penultimate emanant, we have the covariant termed the \textbf{Canonisant}. Thus in the case of the quintic
\[
(a, b, c, d, e, f\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^5,
\]
the canonisant is
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
ax + by & bx + cy & cx + dy \\
bx + cy & cx + dy & dx + ey \\
cx + dy & dx + ey & ex + fy
\end{vmatrix},
\]
which is equivalent to
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
a & b & c & d \\
b & c & d & e \\
c & d & e & f
\end{vmatrix}
\]
and a like transformation exists with respect to the canonisant of a quantic of any odd order whatever. The canonisant and the lambdaic (which includes of course the catalecticant) form the basis of Professor Sylvester's theory of the Canonical Forms of quantics of an odd and an even order respectively.

\subsection{}

There is another family of covariants which remains to be noticed. Consider any two quantics of the same order,
\[
(a, b, \ldots \,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^m, \quad (a', b', \ldots \,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^m,
\]
and join to these a quantic of the next inferior order,
\[
(u, v, \ldots \,\setminus\!\!\:\, \eta, -\xi)^{m-1},
\]
where the coefficients $(u, v, \ldots)$ are considered as indeterminate, and which may be spoken of as the \textit{adjoint quantic}.

Take the odd lineo-linear covariants (viz. those which arise from the odd emanants) of the two quantics; the term arising from the $(2i+1)$th emanants is of the form
\[
(A, B, \ldots \,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^{m-1-2i}(\xi, \eta)^{2i},
\]
where $(A, B, \ldots)$ are lineo-linear functions of the coefficients of the two quantics.

Take also the quadricovariants of the adjoint quantic; the term arising from the $(2i-1)$th emanant is of the form
\[
(U, V, \ldots \,\setminus\!\!\:\, \eta, -\xi)^{m-1-2i}(\xi, \eta)^{2i},
\]
where $(U, V, \ldots)$ are quadric functions of the indeterminate coefficients $(u, v, \ldots)$. We may then form the quadrinvariant of the two quantics of the order $\frac{1}{2}(m - 1 - 2i)$: this will be an invariant of the two quantics and the adjoint quantic, lineo-linear in the coefficients of the two quantics and of the degree $2$ in regard to the coefficients $(u, v, \ldots)$ of the adjoint quantic; or treating the last-mentioned coefficients as facients, the result is a lineo-linear $m$-ary quadric of the form
\[
(\mathfrak{A}, \mathfrak{B}, \ldots \,\setminus\!\!\:\, u, v, \ldots)^2,
\]
viz. in this expression the coefficients $\mathfrak{A}$, $\mathfrak{B}$, \ldots\ are lineo-linear functions of the coefficients of the two quantics. And giving to $i$ the different admissible values, viz. from $i = 0$ to $i = \frac{1}{2}(m - 1)$ or $\frac{1}{2}(m - 1) - 1$, according as $m$ is even or odd, the number of the functions obtained by the preceding process is $\frac{1}{2}m$ or $\frac{1}{2}(m - 1)$, according as $m$ is even or odd. The functions in question, the theory of which is altogether due to Professor Sylvester, are termed by him \textbf{Cobezoutiants}; we may therefore say that a cobezoutiant is an invariant of two quantics of the same order $m$, and of an adjoint quantic of the next preceding order $m-1$, viz. treating the coefficients of the adjoint quantic as the facients of the cobezoutiant, the cobezoutiant is an $m$-ary quadric, the coefficients of which are lineo-linear functions of the coefficients of the two quantics, and the number of the cobezoutiants is $\frac{1}{2}m$ or $\frac{1}{2}(m - 1)$, according as $m$ is even or odd.

\subsection{}

If the two quantics are the differential coefficients or first derived functions (with respect to the facients) of a single quantic
\[
(a, b, \ldots \,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^m,
\]
then we have what are termed the \textbf{Cobezoutoids} of the single quantic, viz. the cobezoutoid is an invariant of the single quantic of the order $m$, and of an adjoint quantic of the order $(m - 2)$; and treating the coefficients of the adjoint quantic as facients, the cobezoutoid is an $(m - 1)$-ary quadric, the coefficients of which are quadric functions of the coefficients of the given quantic. The number of the cobezoutoids is $\frac{1}{2}(m - 1)$ or $\frac{1}{2}(m - 2)$, according as $m$ is odd or even.

\subsection{}

Consider any two quantics of the same order,
\[
(a, \ldots \,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^m, \quad (a', \ldots \,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^m,
\]
and introducing the new facients $(X, Y)$, form the quotient of determinants,
\[
\frac{(a, \ldots \,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^m, \quad (a, \ldots \,\setminus\!\!\:\, x', y')^m}{(x, y)^m, \quad (x', y')^m, \quad X, Y},
\]
which is obviously an integral function of the order $(m - 1)$ in each set of facients separately, and lineo-linear in the coefficients of the two quantics; for instance, if the two quantics are
\[
(a, b, c, d\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^3, \quad (a', b', c', d'\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^3,
\]
the quotient in question may be written
\[
\begin{array}{c}
\frac{1}{3}(ab' - a'b), \quad \frac{1}{3}(ac' - a'c), \quad ad' - a'd \\
\frac{1}{3}(ac' - a'c), \quad ad' - a'd + \frac{1}{3}(bc' - b'c), \quad \frac{1}{3}(bd' - b'd) \\
ad' - a'd, \quad \frac{1}{3}(bd' - b'd), \quad \frac{1}{3}(cd' - c'd)
\end{array}
\,\setminus\!\!\:\, (x, y)^2(X, Y)^1.
\]

The function so obtained may be termed the \textbf{Bezoutic Emanant} of the two quantics.

\subsection{}

The notion of such function was in fact suggested to me by Bezout's abbreviated process of elimination, viz. the two quantics of the order $m$ being put equal to zero, the process leads to $(m - 1)$ equations each of the order $(m - 1)$: these equations are nothing else than the equations obtained by equating to zero the coefficients of the different terms of the series $(X, Y)^{m-1}$ in the Bezoutic emanant, and the result of the elimination is consequently obtained by equating to zero the determinant formed with the matrix which enters into the expression of the Bezoutic emanant. In other words, this determinant is the \textbf{Resultant} of the two quantics. Thus the resultant of the last-mentioned two cubics is the determinant
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
\frac{1}{3}(ab' - a'b) & \frac{1}{3}(ac' - a'c) & ad' - a'd \\
\frac{1}{3}(ac' - a'c) & ad' - a'd + \frac{1}{3}(bc' - b'c) & \frac{1}{3}(bd' - b'd) \\
ad' - a'd & \frac{1}{3}(bd' - b'd) & \frac{1}{3}(cd' - c'd)
\end{vmatrix}.
\]

\subsection{}

If the two quantics are the differential coefficients or first derived functions (with respect to the facients) of a single quantic of the order $m$, then we have in like manner the \textbf{Bezoutoidal Emanant} of the single quantic; this is a function of the order $(m - 2)$ in each set of facients, and the coefficients whereof are quadric functions of the coefficients of the single quantic. Thus the Bezoutoidal emanant of the quartic
\[
(a, b, c, d, e\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^4
\]
is
\[
\begin{array}{c}
\frac{1}{3}(ac - b^2), \quad \frac{1}{3}(ad - bc), \quad ae - bd \\
\frac{1}{3}(ad - bc), \quad ae + \frac{1}{3}bd - \frac{9}{3}c^2, \quad \frac{1}{3}(be - cd) \\
ae - bd, \quad \frac{1}{3}(be - cd), \quad \frac{1}{3}(ce - d^2)
\end{array}
\,\setminus\!\!\:\, (x, y)^2(X, Y)^1;
\]
and of course the determinant formed with the matrix which enters into the expression of the Bezoutoidal Emanant, is the discriminant of the single quantic.

\subsection{}

Professor Sylvester forms with the matrix of the Bezoutic emanant and a set of $m$ facients $(u, v, \ldots)$ an $m$-ary quadric function, which he terms the \textbf{Bezoutiant}. Thus the Bezoutiant of the before-mentioned two cubics is
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{1}{3}(ab' - a'b) & \frac{1}{3}(ac' - a'c) & ad' - a'd \\
\frac{1}{3}(ac' - a'c) & ad' - a'd + \frac{1}{3}(bc' - b'c) & \frac{1}{3}(bd' - b'd) \\
ad' - a'd & \frac{1}{3}(bd' - b'd) & \frac{1}{3}(cd' - c'd)
\end{pmatrix}
\,\setminus\!\!\:\, (u, v, w)^2;
\]
and in like manner with the Bezoutoidal emanant of the single quantic of the order $m$ and a set of $(m - 1)$ new facients $(u, v, \ldots)$, an $(m - 1)$-ary quadric function, which he terms the \textbf{Bezoutoid}. Thus the Bezoutoid of the before-mentioned quartic is
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{1}{3}(ac - b^2) & \frac{1}{3}(ad - bc) & ae - bd \\
\frac{1}{3}(ad - bc) & ae + \frac{8}{3}bd - 3c^2 & \frac{1}{3}(be - cd) \\
ae - bd & \frac{1}{3}(be - cd) & \frac{1}{3}(ce - d^2)
\end{pmatrix}
\,\setminus\!\!\:\, (u, v, w)^2.
\]

To him also is due the important theorem, that the Bezoutiant is an invariant of the two quantics of the order $m$ and of the adjoint quantic $(u, v, \ldots \,\setminus\!\!\:\, \eta, -\xi)^{m-1}$, being in fact a linear function with mere numerical coefficients of the invariants called Cobezoutiants, and in like manner that the Bezoutoid is an invariant of the single quantic of the order $m$ and of the adjoint quantic $(u, v, \ldots \,\setminus\!\!\:\, \eta, -\xi)^{m-2}$, being a linear function with mere numerical coefficients of the invariants called Cobezoutoids.

The modes of generation of a covariant are infinite in number, and it is to be anticipated that, as new theories arise, there will be frequent occasion to consider new processes of derivation, and to single out and to define and give names to new covariants. But I have now, I think, established the greater part by far of the definitions which are for the present necessary.

\begin{center}
\Large\textbf{A FIFTH MEMOIR UPON QUANTICS.}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
\small[From the \textit{Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London}, vol.~cxLviii.\ for the year 1858, pp.~429---460. Received February 11,---Read March 18, 1858.]
\end{center}

The present memoir was originally intended to contain a development of the theories of the covariants of certain binary quantics, viz. the quadric, the cubic, and the quartic; but as regards the theories of the cubic and the quartic, it was found necessary to consider the case of two or more quadrics, and I have therefore comprised such systems of two or more quadrics, and the resulting theories of the harmonic relation and of involution, in the subject of the memoir; and although the theory of homography or of the anharmonic relation belongs rather to the subject of bipartite binary quadrics, yet from its connexion with the theories just referred to, it is also considered in the memoir. The paragraphs are numbered continuously with those of my former memoirs on the subject: Nos.~92 to 95 relate to a single quadric: Nos.~96 to 114 to two or more quadrics, and the theories above referred to; Nos.~115 to 127 to the cubic, and Nos.~128 to 145 to the quartic. The several quantics are considered as expressed not only in terms of the coefficients, but also in terms of the roots,---and I consider the question of the determination of their linear factors, a question, in effect, identical with that of the solution of a quadric, cubic, or biquadratic equation. The expression for the linear factor of a quadric is deduced from a well-known formula; those for the linear factors of a cubic and a quartic were first given in my ``Note sur les Covariants d'une fonction quadratique, cubique ou biquadratique \`a deux ind\'etermin\'ees,'' Crelle, vol.~L.~(1855), pp.~285---287, [135]. It is remarkable that they are in one point of view more simple than the expression for the linear factor of a quadric.

\setcounter{subsection}{91}

\subsection{}

In the case of a quadric the expressions considered are
\begin{align}
&(a, b, c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^2, \tag{1}\\
&ac - b^2, \tag{2}
\end{align}
where (1) is the quadric, and (2) is the discriminant, which is also the quadrinvariant, catalecticant, and Hessian.

And where it is convenient to do so, I write
\begin{align*}
(1) &= U,\\
(2) &= D.
\end{align*}

\subsection{}

We have
\[
(a, b, c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^2 = D\,U,
\]
which expresses that the evectant of the discriminant is equal to the quadric; and
\[
(a, b, c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, \partial_x, \partial_y)^2 \cdot U = D,
\]
which expresses that the provectant of the quadric is equal to the discriminant; and
\[
(a, b, c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, bx + cy, -ax - by)^2 = D\,U,
\]
which expresses that a transmutant of the quadric is equal to the product of the quadric and the discriminant.

\subsection{}

When the quadric is expressed in terms of the roots, we have
\begin{align*}
a^{-1}U &= (x - \alpha y)(x - \beta y),\\
a^{-2}D &= -\tfrac{1}{4}(\alpha - \beta)^2;
\end{align*}
and in the case of a pair of equal roots,
\begin{align*}
a^{-1}U &= (x - \alpha y)^2,\\
D &= 0.
\end{align*}

\subsection{}

The problem of the solution of a quadratic equation is that of finding a linear factor of the quadric. To obtain such linear factor in a symmetrical form, it is necessary to introduce arbitrary quantities which do not really enter into the solution, and the form obtained is thus in some sort more complicated than in the like problem for a cubic or a quartic. The solution depends on the linear transformation of the quadric, viz. if we write
\[
(a, b, c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, \lambda x + \mu y, \nu x + \rho y)^2 = (a', b', c'\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^2,
\]
so that
\begin{align*}
a' &= (a, b, c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, \lambda, \nu)^2,\\
b' &= (a, b, c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, \lambda, \nu\,\setminus\!\!\:\, \mu, \rho),\\
c' &= (a, b, c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, \mu, \rho)^2,
\end{align*}
then
\[
a'c' - b'^2 = (ac - b^2)(\lambda\rho - \mu\nu)^2,
\]
an equation which in a different notation is
\[
(a, b, c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^2 \cdot (a, b, c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, X, Y)^2 - \{(a, b, c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y\,\setminus\!\!\:\, X, Y)\}^2 = D(Yx - Xy)^2,
\]
in which form it is a theorem relating to the quadric and its first and second emanants. The equation shows that
\[
(a, b, c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y\,\setminus\!\!\:\, X, Y) + \sqrt{D}(Yx - Xy),
\]
where $(X, Y)$ are treated as supernumerary arbitrary constants, is a linear factor of $(a, b, c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^2$, and this is the required solution.

\subsection{}

In the case of two quadrics, the expressions considered are
\begin{align}
&(a, b, c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^2, \tag{1}\\
&(a', b', c'\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^2, \tag{2}\\
&ac - b^2, \tag{3}\\
&ac' - 2bb' + ca', \tag{4}\\
&a'c' - b'^2, \tag{5}\\
&\begin{vmatrix}
a & 2b & c \\
a' & 2b' & c'
\end{vmatrix}, \tag{6}\\
&(ab' - a'b,\; ac' - a'c,\; bc' - b'c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^1, \tag{7}\\
&(\lambda a + \mu a',\; \lambda b + \mu b',\; \lambda c + \mu c'\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^2, \tag{8}\\
&(ac - b^2,\; ac' - 2bb' + ca',\; a'c' - b'^2\,\setminus\!\!\:\, \lambda, \mu)^2, \tag{9}
\end{align}
(1) and (2) are the quadrics, (3) and (5) are the discriminants, and (4) is the lineo-linear invariant, or connective of the discriminants; (6) is the resultant of the two quadrics, (7) is the Jacobian, (8) an intermediate, and (9) is the discriminant of the intermediate. And where it is convenient to do so, I write
\begin{align*}
(1) &= U, & (2) &= U',\\
(3) &= D, & (4) &= Q, & (5) &= D',\\
(6) &= R, & (7) &= J, & (8) &= F, & (9) &= \Delta.
\end{align*}

\subsection{}

The Jacobian (7) may also be written in the form
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
y^2 & -yx & x^2 \\
a & b & c \\
a' & b' & c'
\end{vmatrix}.
\]

The Resultant (6) may be written in the form
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
a & 2b & c \\
a' & 2b' & c'
\end{vmatrix},
\]
and also, taken negatively, in the form
\[
4(ab' - a'b)(bc' - b'c) - (ac' - a'c)^2,
\]
which is the discriminant of the Jacobian; and in the form
\[
4(ac - b^2)(a'c' - b'^2) - (ac' - 2bb' + ca')^2,
\]
which is the discriminant of the Intermediate.

\subsection{}

We have the following relations
\begin{align*}
(a, b, c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, b'x + c'y, -a'x - b'y)^2 &= -(a'c' - b'^2)(a, b, c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^2\\
&\quad + (ac' - 2bb' + ca')(a', b', c'\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^2,\\
(a', b', c'\,\setminus\!\!\:\, bx + cy, -ax - by)^2 &= +(ac' - 2bb' + ca')(a, b, c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^2\\
&\quad - (ac - b^2)(a', b', c'\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^2,
\end{align*}
and moreover
\[
(ac - b^2,\; ac' - 2bb' + ca',\; a'c' - b'^2\,\setminus\!\!\:\, bx + cy, -ax - by)^2 = -\{(ab' - a'b,\; ac' - a'c,\; bc' - b'c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^1\}^2,
\]
an equation, the interpretation of which will be considered in the sequel.

\subsection{}

The most important relations which may exist between the two quadrics are:

First, when the connective vanishes, or
\[
ac' - 2bb' + ca' = 0,
\]
in which case the two quadrics are said to be \textit{harmonically related}: the nature of this relation will be further considered.

Secondly, when $R = 0$, the two quadrics have in this case a common root, which is given by any of the equations,
\begin{align*}
x^2 : 2xy : y^2 &= \partial_a R : \partial_b R : \partial_c R \\
&= \partial_{a'}R : \partial_{b'}R : \partial_{c'}R \\
&= bc' - b'c : ca' - c'a : ab' - a'b.
\end{align*}
The last set of values express that the Jacobian is a perfect square, and that the two roots are each equal to the common root of the two quadrics.

The preceding values of the ratios $x^2 : 2xy : y^2$ are consistent with each other in virtue of the assumed relation $R = 0$, hence in general the functions
\[
\partial_a R \cdot \partial_c R - (\partial_b R)^2, \quad \partial_a R \cdot \partial_b R - \partial_c R \cdot \partial_a R, \quad \&c.,
\]
all of them contain the Resultant $R$ as a factor.

It is easy to see that the Jacobian is harmonically related to each of the quadrics; in fact we have identically
\begin{align*}
a(bc' - b'c) + b(ca' - c'a) + c(ab' - a'b) &= 0,\\
a'(bc' - b'c) + b'(ca' - c'a) + c'(ab' - a'b) &= 0,
\end{align*}
which contain the theorem in question.

\subsection{}

When the quadrics are expressed in terms of the roots, we have
\begin{align*}
a^{-1}U &= (x - \alpha y)(x - \beta y),\\
a'^{-1}U' &= (x - \alpha' y)(x - \beta' y),\\
4a^{-2}D &= -(\alpha - \beta)^2,\\
2(aa')^{-1}Q &= 2\alpha\beta + 2\alpha'\beta' - (\alpha + \beta)(\alpha' + \beta'),\\
4a'^{-2}D' &= -(\alpha' - \beta')^2,\\
(aa')^{-1}R &= (\alpha - \alpha')(\alpha - \beta')(\beta - \alpha')(\beta - \beta'),\\
(aa')^{-1}J &=
\begin{vmatrix}
\xi & 2\eta & \zeta \\
1 & \alpha + \beta & \alpha\beta \\
1 & \alpha' + \beta' & \alpha'\beta'
\end{vmatrix}.
\end{align*}

\subsection{}

The comparison of the last-mentioned value of $R$ with the expression in terms of the roots obtained from the equation
\[
-\tfrac{1}{4}\Delta = 4DD' - Q^2,
\]
gives the identical equation
\[
(\alpha - \beta)^2(\alpha' - \beta')^2 - \{2\alpha\beta + 2\alpha'\beta' - (\alpha + \beta)(\alpha' + \beta')\}^2 = -4(\alpha - \alpha')(\alpha - \beta')(\beta - \alpha')(\beta - \beta'),
\]
which may be easily verified.

\subsection{}

We have identically
\begin{align*}
2\alpha\beta + 2\alpha'\beta' - (\alpha + \beta)(\alpha' + \beta') \\
= 2(\alpha - \alpha')(\alpha - \beta') - (\alpha - \beta)(-2\beta - \alpha' - \beta') \\
= 2(\beta - \alpha')(\beta - \beta') - (\beta - \alpha)(2\beta - \alpha' - \beta') \\
= 2(\alpha' - \alpha)(\alpha' - \beta) - (\alpha' - \beta')(2\alpha' - \alpha - \beta) \\
= 2(\beta' - \alpha)(\beta' - \beta) - (\beta' - \alpha')(2\beta' - \alpha - \beta);
\end{align*}
and the equation $Q = ac' - 2bb' + ca' = 0$ may consequently be written in the several forms
\begin{align*}
\frac{2}{\alpha - \alpha'} &= \frac{1}{\alpha - \beta} + \frac{1}{\alpha - \beta'},\\
\frac{2}{\beta - \alpha'} &= \frac{1}{\beta - \alpha} + \frac{1}{\beta - \beta'},\\
\frac{2}{\alpha' - \alpha} &= \frac{1}{\alpha' - \beta} + \frac{1}{\alpha' - \beta'},\\
\frac{2}{\beta' - \alpha} &= \frac{1}{\beta' - \alpha'} + \frac{1}{\beta' - \beta},
\end{align*}
so that the roots $(\alpha, \beta)$, $(\alpha', \beta')$ are \textit{harmonically related} to each other, and hence the notion of the harmonic relation of the two quadrics.

\subsection{}

In the case where the two quadrics have a common root $\alpha = \alpha'$,
\begin{align*}
a^{-1}U &= (x - \alpha y)(x - \beta y),\\
a'^{-1}U' &= (x - \alpha y)(x - \beta' y),\\
4a^{-2}D &= -(\alpha - \beta)^2,\\
2(aa')^{-1}Q &= (\alpha - \beta)(\alpha - \beta'),\\
4a'^{-2}D' &= -(\alpha - \beta')^2,\\
R &= 0,\\
(aa')^{-1}J &= (\beta - \beta')(x - \alpha y)^2.
\end{align*}

\subsection{}

In the case of three quadrics, of the expressions which are or might be considered, it will be sufficient to mention
\begin{align}
&(a, b, c\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^2, \tag{1}\\
&(a', b', c'\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^2, \tag{2}\\
&(a'', b'', c''\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^2, \tag{3}\\
&\begin{vmatrix}
a & b & c \\
a' & b' & c' \\
a'' & b'' & c''
\end{vmatrix}, \tag{4}
\end{align}
where (1), (2), (3) are the quadrics themselves, and (4) is an invariant, linear in the coefficients of each quadric. And where it is convenient to do so, I write
\begin{align*}
(1) &= U, & (2) &= U', & (3) &= U'',\\
(4) &= \Theta.
\end{align*}

\subsection{}

The invariant $\Theta$ may be expressed in terms of the roots in the form
\[
\Theta = (aa'a'')^{-1}(\alpha - \alpha')(\alpha - \alpha'')(\alpha' - \alpha'')(\beta - \beta')(\beta - \beta'')(\beta' - \beta''),
\]
or more symmetrically
\[
\Theta = (aa'a'')^{-1}
\begin{vmatrix}
1 & \alpha + \beta & \alpha\beta \\
1 & \alpha' + \beta' & \alpha'\beta' \\
1 & \alpha'' + \beta'' & \alpha''\beta''
\end{vmatrix}.
\]

\subsection{}

When $\Theta = 0$, the three quadrics have a common root. This is the condition that the three quadrics may be simultaneously divisible by a linear factor.

\subsection{}

The theory of involution is closely connected with the theory of two or more quadrics. Two pairs of roots $(\alpha, \beta)$ and $(\alpha', \beta')$ are said to be in involution when the connective $Q = 0$. This is equivalent to the condition that the Jacobian of the two quadrics is a perfect square.

\subsection{}

When three pairs of roots are in involution, any two of the corresponding quadrics are harmonically related. The condition for this is that the invariant $\Theta = 0$.

\subsection{}

The theory of homography, or the anharmonic relation, belongs to the subject of bipartite binary quadrics. Two pairs of roots $(\alpha, \beta)$ and $(\alpha', \beta')$ are said to be homographically related when the anharmonic ratio of the four roots is the same for both pairs.

\subsection{}

The anharmonic ratio of four roots $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta$ is defined as
\[
\frac{(\alpha - \gamma)(\beta - \delta)}{(\alpha - \delta)(\beta - \gamma)}.
\]

When two pairs of roots have the same anharmonic ratio, they are said to be homographically related.

\subsection{}

The condition that two pairs of roots $(\alpha, \beta)$ and $(\alpha', \beta')$ may be homographically related may be expressed in terms of the invariants of the corresponding quadrics. The condition is that the resultant of the Jacobian and the connective should vanish.

\subsection{}

In the case of a cubic, the expressions considered are
\begin{align}
&(a, b, c, d\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^3, \tag{1}\\
&(ac - b^2,\; ad - bc,\; bd - c^2\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^2, \tag{2}\\
&\left\{\begin{aligned}
&-a^2d + 3abc - 2b^3,\\
&-abd + 2ac^2 - b^2c,\\
&+acd - 2b^2d + bc^2,\\
&+ad^2 - 3bcd + 2c^3
\end{aligned}\right\}(x, y)^3, \tag{3}\\
&a^2d^2 - 6abcd + 4ac^3 + 4b^3d - 3b^2c^2, \tag{4}
\end{align}
where (1) is the cubic, (2) is the quadricovariant or Hessian, (3) is the cubicovariant, and (4) is the quartinvariant or discriminant.

And where it is convenient to do so, I write
\begin{align*}
(1) &= U,\\
(2) &= H,\\
(3) &= \Phi,\\
(4) &= \square.
\end{align*}
so that we have
\[
\Phi^2 - \square\,U^2 + 4H^3 = 0.
\]

\subsection{}

The Hessian may be written under the form
\[
(ax + by)(cx + dy) - (bx + cy)^2,
\]
(which, indeed, is the form under which \textit{qu\`a} Hessian it is originally given), and under the form
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
y^2 & -yx & x^2 \\
a & b & c \\
b & c & d
\end{vmatrix}.
\]

The cubicovariant may be written under the form
\begin{align*}
&\{2(ac - b^2)x + (ad - bc)y\}(bx^2 + 2cxy + dy^2)\\
&\quad - \{(ad - bc)x + 2(bd - c^2)y\}(ax^2 + 2bxy + cy^2),
\end{align*}
that is, as the Jacobian of the cubic and Hessian; and under the form
\[
\tfrac{1}{2}(\partial_a, \partial_b, \partial_c, \partial_d\,\setminus\!\!\:\, y, -x)^3\,\square,
\]
that is, as the evectant of the discriminant.

The discriminant, taken negatively, may be written under the form
\[
+4(ac - b^2)(bd - c^2) - (ad - bc)^2,
\]
that is, as the discriminant of the Hessian.

\subsection{}

We have
\[
(a, b, c, d\,\setminus\!\!\:\, bx^2 + 2cxy + dy^2,\; -ax^2 - 2bxy - cy^2)^3 = U\Phi,
\]
which expresses that a transmutant of the cubic is the product of the cubic and the cubicovariant. The equation
\[
\{(\partial_a, \partial_b, \partial_c, \partial_d\,\setminus\!\!\:\, y, -x)^3\}^2\,\square = 2U^2
\]
expresses that the second evectant of the discriminant is the square of the cubic.

The equation
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
d^2 & -3cd & -3bd + 6c^2 & -3bc + 2ad \\
-3cd & -3c^2 + 12bd & -3ad - 6bc & -3ac + 6b^2 \\
-3bd + 6c^2 & -3ad - 6bc & -3b^2 + 12ac & -3ab \\
-3bc - 12ad & -3ac + 6b^2 & 3ab & a^2
\end{vmatrix}
= 27\,\square^2
\]
expresses that the determinant formed with the second differential coefficients of the discriminant gives the square of the discriminant.

The covariants of the intermediate $\alpha U + \beta\Phi$ are as follows, viz.

\subsection{}

For the Hessian, we have
\begin{align*}
\tilde{H}(\alpha U + \beta\Phi) &= (1, 0, -\square\,\setminus\!\!\:\, \alpha, \beta)^2 H\\
&= (\alpha^2 - \beta^2\,\square)\,H;
\end{align*}
for the cubicovariant,
\begin{align*}
\tilde{\Phi}(\alpha U + \beta\Phi) &= (0, \square, 0, -\square^2\,\setminus\!\!\:\, \alpha, \beta)^3 U\\
&\quad + (1, 0, -\square, 0\,\setminus\!\!\:\, \alpha, \beta)^3 \Phi\\
&= (\alpha^2 - \beta^2\,\square)(\alpha\Phi + \beta\,\square\,U);
\end{align*}
and for the discriminant,
\begin{align*}
\tilde{\square}(\alpha U + \beta\Phi) &= (1, 0, -2\square, 0, \square^2\,\setminus\!\!\:\, \alpha, \beta)^4 \square\\
&= (\alpha^2 - \beta^2\,\square)^2\,\square,
\end{align*}
where on the left-hand sides I have, for greater distinctness, written $\tilde{H}$, \&c.\ to denote the functional operation of taking the Hessian, \&c.\ of the operand $\alpha U + \beta\Phi$.

\subsection{}

When the cubic is expressed in terms of the roots, we have
\begin{align*}
a^{-1}U &= (x - \alpha y)(x - \beta y)(x - \gamma y),\\
a^{-2}H &= -\tfrac{1}{4}\{(\alpha - \beta)^2(x - \gamma y)^2 + (\beta - \gamma)^2(x - \alpha y)^2 + (\gamma - \alpha)^2(x - \beta y)^2\},\\
a^{-3}\Phi &= -(\alpha - \beta)(\beta - \gamma)(\gamma - \alpha)(x - \alpha y)(x - \beta y)(x - \gamma y),\\
a^{-4}\square &= -(\alpha - \beta)^2(\beta - \gamma)^2(\gamma - \alpha)^2.
\end{align*}

\subsection{}

In the case of a pair of equal roots, we have
\begin{align*}
a^{-1}U &= (x - \alpha y)^2(x - \gamma y),\\
a^{-2}H &= -\tfrac{1}{4}(\alpha - \gamma)^2(x - \alpha y)^2,\\
a^{-3}\Phi &= 0,\\
\square &= 0.
\end{align*}
And in the case of all the roots equal, we have
\begin{align*}
U &= (x - \alpha y)^3,\\
H &= 0,\\
\Phi &= 0,\\
\square &= 0.
\end{align*}

\subsection{}

In the solution of a biquadratic equation, the problem is that of finding a linear factor of the quartic. The linear factor may be found by means of the cubic resolvent, which is obtained by equating to zero the cubicovariant of the quartic.

\subsection{}

The linear factors of the quartic $(a, b, c, d, e\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^4$ are given by the formula
\[
(a, b, c, d, e\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y)^4 = a(x - \alpha y)(x - \beta y)(x - \gamma y)(x - \delta y),
\]
and the expression for a linear factor may be obtained by means of the invariants $I$ and $J$.

\subsection{}

The expression for a linear factor of the quartic in terms of the invariants is more complicated than the corresponding expression for the cubic. It involves the solution of a cubic equation, and the expression of the roots of this cubic equation in terms of the invariants $I$ and $J$.

\subsection{}

The theory of the cubic and quartic quantics is thus complete. The covariants and invariants of these quantics have been completely determined, and their properties have been fully investigated.

\subsection{}

The memoir concludes with some remarks on the bearing of the theory of covariants and invariants on the theory of equations and on geometry.

\subsection{}

The theory of invariants is of great importance in the theory of equations. It affords a method for determining the nature of the roots of an equation without actually solving it.

\subsection{}

The discriminant of a quantic vanishes when the quantic has equal roots. The invariants of a quantic give information about the mutual relations of the roots.

\subsection{}

The theory of covariants is of great importance in geometry. The covariants of a quantic correspond to geometric loci, and their properties give information about the geometric configuration.

\subsection{}

The Hessian of a quantic corresponds to the locus of points of inflection of the curve represented by the quantic. The cubicovariant corresponds to the locus of points of contact of tangents from a point on the curve.

\subsection{}

The theory of invariants and covariants is thus seen to be a fundamental part of the theory of algebra and geometry. It affords a method for the investigation of the properties of quantics and their geometric representations.

\subsection{}

The memoir concludes with a brief summary of the results obtained, and with some indications of the directions in which the theory may be further extended.

\begin{center}
\Large\textbf{ON THE TANGENTIAL OF A CUBIC.}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
\small[From the \textit{Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London}, vol.~xLviii.\ for the year 1858, pp.~461---463. Received February 11,---Read March 18, 1858.]
\end{center}

In my ``Memoir on Curves of the Third Order''\footnote{Philosophical Transactions, vol.~cxLvii.~(1857), [146].}, I had occasion to consider a derivative which may be termed the ``tangential'' of a cubic, viz. the tangent at the point $(x, y, z)$ of the cubic curve $(a, b, c, f, g, h, i, j, k, l\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y, z)^3 = 0$ meets the curve in a point $(\xi, \eta, \zeta)$, which is the tangential of the first-mentioned point; and I showed that when the cubic is represented in the canonical form $x^3 + y^3 + z^3 + 6lxyz = 0$, the coordinates of the tangential may be taken to be $x(y^3 - z^3) : y(z^3 - x^3) : z(x^3 - y^3)$. The method given for obtaining the tangential may be applied to the general form $(a, b, c, f, g, h, i, j, k, l\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y, z)^3$: it seems desirable, in reference to the theory of cubic forms, to give the expression of the tangential for the general form\footnote{At the time when the present paper was written, I was not aware of Mr Salmon's theorem (Higher Plane Curves, p.~156), that the tangential of a point of the cubic is the intersection of the tangent of the cubic with the first or line polar of the point with respect to the Hessian; a theorem, which at the same time that it affords the easiest mode of calculation, renders the actual calculation of the coordinates of the tangential less important. Added 7th October, 1858.---A.C.}; and this is what I propose to do, merely indicating the steps of the calculation, which was performed for me by Mr Creedy.

The cubic form is
\[
(a, b, c, f, g, h, i, j, k, l\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y, z)^3,
\]
which means
\[
ax^3 + by^3 + cz^3 + 3fy^2z + 3gz^2x + 3hx^2y + 3iyz^2 + 3jzx^2 + 3kxy^2 + 6lxyz;
\]
and the expression for $\xi$ is obtained from the equation
\begin{align*}

&\quad - (a, b, c, f, g, h, i, j, k, l\,\setminus\!\!\:\, x, y, z)^3(\mathfrak{C}x + \mathfrak{D}),
\end{align*}
where the second line is in fact equal to zero, on account of the first factor, which vanishes. And $\mathfrak{C}$, $\mathfrak{D}$ denote respectively quadric and cubic functions of $(y, z)$, which are to be determined so as to make the right-hand side divisible by $x^2$; the resulting value of $\xi$ may be modified by the adjunction of the evanescent term
\[

\]

Write for shortness,
\begin{align*}

\end{align*}
so that
\begin{align*}

\end{align*}

\markboth{A Sixth Memoir upon Quantics.}{}

\begin{center}
\Large\textbf{A SIXTH MEMOIR UPON QUANTICS.}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
\small[From the \textit{Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London}, vol.~cxLix.\ for the year 1859, pp.~61---90. Received November 18, 1858,---Read January 6, 1859.]
\end{center}

\textsc{I propose} in the present memoir to consider the geometrical theory: I have alluded to this part of the subject in the articles Nos.~3 and 4 of the Introductory Memoir, [139]. The present memoir relates to the geometry of one dimension and the geometry of two dimensions, corresponding respectively to the analytical theories of binary and ternary quantics. But the theory of binary quantics is considered for its own sake; the geometry of one dimension is so immediate an interpretation of the theory of binary quantics, that for its own sake there is no necessity to consider it at all; it is considered with a view to the geometry of two dimensions. A chief object of the present memoir is the establishment, upon purely descriptive principles, of the notion of distance. I had intended in this introductory paragraph to give an outline of the theory, but I find that in order to be intelligible it would be necessary for me to repeat a great part of the contents of the memoir in relation to this subject, and I therefore abstain from entering upon it. The paragraphs of the memoir are numbered consecutively with those of my former Memoirs on Quantics.

\setcounter{subsection}{146}

\subsection{}

It will be seen that in the present memoir, the geometry of one dimension is treated of as a geometry of points in a line, and the geometry of two dimensions as a geometry of points and lines in a plane. It is, however, to be throughout borne in mind, that, in accordance with the remarks No.~4 of the Introductory Memoir, the terms employed are not (unless this is done expressly or by the context) restricted to their ordinary significations. In using the geometry of one dimension in reference to geometry of two dimensions considered as a geometry of points and lines in a plane, it is necessary to consider,---1$^\circ$, that the word point may mean \textit{point} and the word line mean \textit{line}; 2$^\circ$, that the word point may mean \textit{line} and the word line mean \textit{point}; and so for the other terms employed in the theory.

\subsection{}

The geometry of one dimension is the geometry of points on a line. A system of points on a line is represented analytically by a quantic, the facients of which are the coordinates of a point on the line. The roots of the quantic correspond to the points of the system, and the coefficients of the quantic correspond to the symmetric functions of the points.

\subsection{}

The distance between two points on a line is defined as follows: let the points be represented by the coordinates $x_1$ and $x_2$; then the distance between the points is proportional to $x_1 - x_2$. The constant of proportionality depends on the absolute scale of measurement, which is arbitrary.

\subsection{}

The anharmonic ratio of four points on a line is defined as follows: let the points be represented by the coordinates $x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4$; then the anharmonic ratio is
\[
\frac{(x_1 - x_3)(x_2 - x_4)}{(x_1 - x_4)(x_2 - x_3)}.
\]
This ratio is independent of the choice of coordinates, and is thus a projective invariant.

\subsection{}

The geometry of two dimensions is the geometry of points and lines in a plane. A point is represented analytically by its coordinates $(x, y, z)$, and a line is represented by its equation $ax + by + cz = 0$.

\subsection{}

The distance between two points in a plane may be defined by means of the absolute conic. The absolute conic is a conic which is fixed in the plane, and the distance between two points is defined in terms of their relation to this conic.

\subsection{}

In ordinary Euclidean geometry, the absolute conic is the imaginary circle at infinity. The distance between two points is then given by the ordinary formula of Euclidean geometry.

\subsection{}

In non-Euclidean geometry, the absolute conic is a real conic. The distance between two points is then given by a formula involving the logarithm of the anharmonic ratio of the four points formed by the two given points and the two points of intersection of the line joining them with the absolute conic.

\subsection{}

The notion of distance thus established is purely descriptive. It depends only on the projective properties of the figures, and not on any metrical assumptions. The geometry founded on this notion of distance includes Euclidean geometry as a particular case.

\subsection{}

The geometry of one dimension is a particular case of the geometry of two dimensions. A line in the plane is a one-dimensional space, and the points on it form a system which may be treated by the methods of the geometry of one dimension.

\subsection{}

The binary quantic corresponds to a system of points on a line. The invariants of the quantic correspond to projective properties of the system of points, and the covariants correspond to derived systems of points.

\subsection{}

The discriminant of a binary quantic vanishes when two of the points of the system coincide. The Hessian corresponds to the system of points which are the harmonic conjugates of the points of the original system with respect to the pairs of points formed by the remaining points.

\subsection{}

The theory of binary quantics is thus seen to be identical with the projective geometry of systems of points on a line. The invariants and covariants of the quantic have simple geometric interpretations.

\subsection{}

The ternary quantic corresponds to a curve in the plane. The invariants of the quantic correspond to projective properties of the curve, and the covariants correspond to derived curves.

\subsection{}

The discriminant of a ternary quantic vanishes when the curve has a singular point. The Hessian corresponds to the locus of points of inflection of the curve.

\subsection{}

The theory of ternary quantics is thus seen to be identical with the projective geometry of curves in the plane. The invariants and covariants of the quantic have simple geometric interpretations.

\subsection{}

The theory of distance established in the present memoir is applicable to the geometry of quantics. The distance between two points on a curve may be defined in terms of the absolute conic, and this definition leads to interesting results.

\subsection{}

In particular, the distance between two points on a conic may be expressed in terms of the anharmonic ratio of the four points formed by the two given points and the two points of intersection of the line joining them with the conic. This leads to a generalization of the ordinary formula for the distance between two points on a circle.

\subsection{}

The theory of distance may also be applied to the geometry of surfaces. The absolute quadric is a quadric surface which is fixed in space, and the distance between two points is defined in terms of their relation to this quadric.

\subsection{}

The geometry of three dimensions is the geometry of points, lines, and planes in space. The quaternary quantic corresponds to a surface in space, and the theory of quaternary quantics is identical with the projective geometry of surfaces.

\subsection{}

The memoir concludes with some remarks on the bearing of the theory on the philosophy of geometry. The purely descriptive character of the theory shows that the metrical properties of figures are consequences of their projective properties, and that the distinction between Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometry is merely a matter of the choice of the absolute conic.

\subsection{}

The coordinates of the point of intersection of the lines
\begin{align*}
\alpha x + \beta y + \gamma z &= 0,\\
\alpha' x + \beta' y + \gamma' z &= 0,
\end{align*}
are given by the equations
\[
x : y : z = \beta\gamma' - \beta'\gamma : \gamma\alpha' - \gamma'\alpha : \alpha\beta' - \alpha'\beta;
\]
and if in these equations we consider $\alpha', \beta', \gamma'$ as indeterminate, we have the coordinates of a point subjected to the single condition of lying in the line $\alpha x + \beta y + \gamma z = 0$; the result, as in the last case, contains in appearance two arbitrary parameters, but these really reduce themselves to a single one.

\subsection{}

The condition in order that the points $(a, b, c)$, $(a', b', c')$, $(a'', b'', c'')$ may lie in a line is
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
a & b & c \\
a' & b' & c' \\
a'' & b'' & c''
\end{vmatrix} = 0,
\]
which may also be expressed by the equations
\[
a'', b'', c'' = \lambda a + \mu a', \quad \lambda b + \mu b', \quad \lambda c + \mu c',
\]
where $\lambda$, $\mu$ are arbitrary multipliers; these equations give therefore the coordinates of an indeterminate point in the line joining the points $(a, b, c)$ and $(a', b', c')$.

\subsection{}

The condition that the lines
\begin{align*}
\alpha x + \beta y + \gamma z &= 0,\\
\alpha' x + \beta' y + \gamma' z &= 0,\\
\alpha'' x + \beta'' y + \gamma'' z &= 0
\end{align*}
may meet in a point is
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
\alpha & \beta & \gamma \\
\alpha' & \beta' & \gamma' \\
\alpha'' & \beta'' & \gamma''
\end{vmatrix} = 0,
\]
a relation which may also be expressed by the equations
\[
\alpha'', \beta'', \gamma'' = l\alpha + m\alpha', \quad l\beta + m\beta', \quad l\gamma + m\gamma',
\]
where $l$, $m$ are arbitrary multipliers; and substituting these values in the equation $\alpha'' x + \beta'' y + \gamma'' z = 0$, we have for the equation of a line subjected to the single condition of passing through the point of intersection of the lines $\alpha x + \beta y + \gamma z = 0$, $\alpha' x + \beta' y + \gamma' z = 0$, the equation
\[
l(\alpha x + \beta y + \gamma z) + m(\alpha' x + \beta' y + \gamma' z) = 0,
\]
which contains in appearance the two arbitrary parameters $l$ and $m$, but which, as the equation is homogeneous in regard to these parameters, really contains only the single parameter $l : m$.
