\markboth{221.\quad ON THE SECULAR ACCELERATION OF THE MOON'S MEAN MOTION.}{}

\section*{\large 221.}
\subsection*{ON THE SECULAR ACCELERATION OF THE MOON'S MEAN MOTION.}

\begin{center}
[From the \textit{Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society}, vol.~\textsc{xxii}. (1862), pp. 171--186.]
\end{center}

\medskip

The values of the certain terms in the expressions of the moon's coordinates,
which give the secular acceleration of the moon's mean motion, were originally
calculated by me by a direct and elementary method, which (as it appears to me)
has the advantage over Plana's very elegant process, that in it we have not to
assume without proof that the required acceleration arises entirely from the
variation of the eccentricity of the earth's orbit. The solution was originally
obtained in a literal form; and the numerical results were given to a greater
degree of accuracy than was requisite. I propose in the present paper to
present the investigation under a somewhat altered form; and to give the results
in the numerical form only, but to a greater degree of accuracy than was
attained in my original memoir. The following changes have been made; viz.
instead of the disturbing function
\[
\frac{m'}{\rho'^3}\left(x'x + y'y + z'z\right)
\]
I employ the equivalent form
\[
\frac{m'}{\rho'^3}\rho^2\cos\angle\left(\rho, \rho'\right),
\]
and in connexion herewith, I introduce the coordinates $P$, $Q$ (functions of
$\rho$, $v$) employed by Mr Delaunay in his new lunar theory; viz. writing
\[
P = \frac{\rho}{r}\cos(v - nt - \epsilon) = \cos\phi\cos(v - nt - \epsilon),\quad
Q = \frac{\rho}{r}\sin(v - nt - \epsilon) = \cos\phi\sin(v - nt - \epsilon),
\]
so that $P$, $Q$ are the values of $\cos(v - nt - \epsilon)$, $\sin(v - nt - \epsilon)$,
multiplied by the cosine of the moon's latitude; and the disturbing function
is
\[
m'\frac{\rho^2}{\rho'^3}\left(P\cos v' + Q\sin v'\right).
\]
The expressions for $\delta P$, $\delta Q$ are obtained in a more simple form by
assuming at once the numerical value of the ratio $m$ of the mean motions.
I write also $t = nt$, and take $\tau$, $g$ instead of Delaunay's $\xi$, $g$; viz.
$\tau$ is the moon's mean longitude minus the longitude of the perigee; and
$g$ is the longitude of the moon's perigee minus that of the sun's perigee.
The epoch is taken to be the time of the sun's perigee passing the node; so
that at the epoch $\tau = \varpi - \theta$, $g = \varpi - \varpi'$; where
$\varpi$, $\varpi'$ are the longitudes of the moon's perigee and the sun's
perigee, and $\theta$ is the longitude of the node. This being so, the mean
longitude of the moon is $nt + \epsilon + v_1$, where $v_1$ is the value of
$v$ at the epoch; the mean longitude of the moon's perigee is $nt + \epsilon + v_1 - \tau$;
and the mean longitude of the sun's perigee is $nt + \epsilon + v_1 - \tau - g$.
The sun's mean anomaly is $n't + \epsilon' - (nt + \epsilon + v_1 - \tau - g)$;
and introducing the numerical value $n' = (1 - m)n$, this is
$=(1 - m)nt + \epsilon' - nt - \epsilon - v_1 + \tau + g$, $= -mnt + \epsilon' - \epsilon - v_1 + \tau + g$,
which at the epoch vanishes: we have therefore $\epsilon' = \epsilon + v_1 - \tau - g$,
and the mean anomaly is $= -mnt + \tau + g$, $= -t + \tau + g$, writing therein
$t$ for $mnt$; that is, it is $-(t - \tau - g)$. Hence writing $g' = t - \tau - g$,
which is the sun's mean anomaly, then $g = t - \tau - g'$; and the arguments
$2\tau$, $2\tau - g$, $2\tau + g$, \&c., become $2\tau$, $2\tau + g' - t$, $3t - 2\tau - g'$;
or, as the terms containing $t$ need not be exhibited, these may be written
$2\tau$, $2\tau - g'$, $-2\tau + g'$, that is $2\tau$, $2\tau - g'$, $2\tau + g'$,
the sign only of the sine or cosine terms being attended to. And so in other
cases; the arguments are all given in terms of $\tau$ and $g'$; but the accented
letter $g'$ will for convenience be written without the accent.

\mypar
I.

\mypar
We have
\[
\frac{d^2\rho}{dt^2} - \rho\left(\frac{dv}{dt}\right)^2 + \frac{n^2a^3}{\rho^2} = n^2\delta R,\qquad
\frac{d(\rho^2\frac{dv}{dt})}{dt} = n^2\delta S;
\]
and thence
\[
\frac{d^2\delta\rho}{dt^2} - 2\rho n\delta\left(\frac{dv}{dt}\right) - n^2\delta\rho + \frac{2n^2a^3}{\rho^3}\delta\rho
= n^2\delta R,
\]
that is
\[
\frac{d^2\delta\rho}{dt^2} + n^2\delta\rho - 2n^2\delta\left(\frac{dv}{dt}\right) = n^2\delta R,
\]
if for the undisturbed values we write $\rho = a$, $\dfrac{dv}{dt} = n$;
and
\[
\frac{d\delta v}{dt} = \frac{2n}{\rho}\delta\rho + \frac{n}{\rho^2}\delta S
= 2n\delta\rho + n\delta S,
\]
if for the undisturbed values $\rho = 1$, $\dfrac{dv}{dt} = n$, or say
$\rho = 1$, $dv = n\,dt$, or simply $dv = dt$, taking $n = 1$. We thus
have
\[
\frac{d^2\delta\rho}{dt^2} + \delta\rho - 2\delta\left(\frac{dv}{dt}\right) = \delta R,\qquad
\frac{d\delta v}{dt} = 2\delta\rho + \delta S;
\]
or, what is the same thing,
\[
\frac{d^2\delta\rho}{dt^2} + \delta\rho = \delta P,\qquad
\frac{d\delta v}{dt} = \delta Q,
\]
if for shortness
\[
\delta P = \delta R + 2\int\delta Q\,dt,\qquad
\delta Q = 2\delta\rho + \delta S;
\]
so that, as regards the terms in question, the original equations serve to
determine $\delta\rho$, $\delta v$.

\mypar
II.

\mypar
The value of the disturbing function is
\[
\delta R = \frac{m'}{\rho'^3}\rho^2\left(P\cos v' + Q\sin v'\right),
\]
where
\[
P = \frac{\rho}{r}\cos(v - t - \epsilon),\qquad
Q = \frac{\rho}{r}\sin(v - t - \epsilon).
\]
We require the non-periodic part of $\dfrac{d\delta v}{dt}$, that is of
$2\delta\rho + \delta S$; and in forming this we may in $\delta\rho$, $\delta S$
take account of the periodic terms only, neglecting the terms which depend
on $t$ (secular terms) and the constant terms; these last, in fact, give
only periodic terms in $\delta\rho$, $\delta S$. In $\delta R$, the term
$\dfrac{m'}{\rho'^3}\rho^2P\cos v'$ gives in $\delta P$, and thence in
$\delta\rho$, terms involving $\cos g'$ with the factor $e'$ (the sun's
eccentricity); and the term $\dfrac{m'}{\rho'^3}\rho^2Q\sin v'$ gives in
$\delta Q$, and thence in $\delta v$, terms involving $\sin g'$ with the
factor $e'$. The corresponding terms in $\delta\rho$, $\delta v$ give in
$\delta P$ ($= \delta R + 2\int\delta Q\,dt$) and in $\delta Q$
($= 2\delta\rho + \delta S$) the terms which produce the secular acceleration.
And so in other cases; we require in $\delta P$ the terms
\[
A + Bt\cos g' + Ct\sin g',
\]
and in $\delta Q$ the terms
\[
A' + B't\sin g' + C't\cos g',
\]
where the constant terms $A$, $A'$ are introduced for the reason above;
the terms in question give in $\delta\rho$ the terms
\[
A + \frac{1}{2}Bt\cos g' + \frac{1}{2}Ct\sin g',
\]
and in $\delta v$ the terms
\[
A't + \frac{1}{2}B't^2\sin g' - \frac{1}{2}C't^2\cos g' - \frac{1}{2}B'\frac{\cos g'}{m^2}
+ \frac{1}{2}C'\frac{\sin g'}{m^2};
\]
where the constant terms $\dfrac{1}{2}B'\dfrac{\cos g'}{m^2}$, $-\dfrac{1}{2}C'\dfrac{\sin g'}{m^2}$
are written down so as to make the whole values of $\delta\rho$, $\delta v$
correct as far as the terms in question; and from them we have in
$\dfrac{d\delta v}{dt}$, the secular term $A't$, that is $-2B't$, if
$A' = -2B$ (for observe that in $\delta Q$ the non-periodic part is $A' + B't\sin g' + C't\cos g'$,
where $A' = -2B$, and the value of $\dfrac{d\delta v}{dt}$ is $A' - \dfrac{2B'}{m^2} + \&\mathrm{c}.$,
which in regard to the constant term gives $A' - \dfrac{2B'}{m^2} = -2B - \dfrac{2B'}{m^2}$,
$= -2B$ since $B'$ is a term of the order $m^2$).

\mypar
III.

\mypar
We require the expression for $\dfrac{m'}{\rho'^3}$, say
\[
\frac{m'}{\rho'^3} = m^2n^2\left(1 + \frac{3}{2}e'\cos g' + \&\mathrm{c}.\right),
\]
and then, by what precedes, in forming the values of $\delta P$, $\delta Q$,
it is sufficient to take
\[
\frac{m'}{\rho'^3} = m^2n^2\left(1 + \frac{3}{2}e'\cos g'\right).
\]
In the expressions for $P$, $Q$ we require only the non-periodic terms and
the terms containing the argument $g'$, $2g'$. Writing
\[
\frac{\rho}{r} = 1 + \frac{1}{6}m^2 - e\cos\tau - \frac{1}{6}m^2\cos 2\tau,\qquad
v - t - \epsilon = 2e\sin\tau + \frac{5}{4}e^2\sin 2\tau + \frac{1}{2}m^2\sin(2\tau - 2g'),
\]
where in $v - t - \epsilon$ the term $\frac{1}{2}m^2\sin(2\tau - 2g')$ arises
from the evection, and is in fact $\frac{1}{2}m^2\sin(2\tau - 2g')$; we find
\begin{align*}
P &= \left(1 + \tfrac{1}{6}m^2\right)\cos(v - t - \epsilon)
 - e\cos\tau\cos(v - t - \epsilon)
 - \tfrac{1}{6}m^2\cos 2\tau\cos(v - t - \epsilon),\\
Q &= \left(1 + \tfrac{1}{6}m^2\right)\sin(v - t - \epsilon)
 - e\cos\tau\sin(v - t - \epsilon)
 - \tfrac{1}{6}m^2\cos 2\tau\sin(v - t - \epsilon).
\end{align*}

\mypar
But
\[
\cos(v - t - \epsilon) = 1 - 2e^2\sin^2\tau + \&\mathrm{c}.
= 1 - e^2 + e^2\cos 2\tau,\qquad
\sin(v - t - \epsilon) = 2e\sin\tau + \tfrac{5}{4}e^2\sin 2\tau;
\]
and hence, up to the required order,
\begin{align*}
P &= \left(1 + \tfrac{1}{6}m^2 - e^2\right) + \left(-e + 2e\right)\cos\tau
 + \left(\tfrac{1}{6}m^2 + e^2\right)\cos 2\tau
 = 1 + \tfrac{1}{6}m^2 - e^2 + e\cos\tau + \left(\tfrac{1}{6}m^2 + e^2\right)\cos 2\tau,\\
Q &= \left(-e + 2e\right)\sin\tau + \tfrac{5}{4}e^2\sin 2\tau
 = e\sin\tau + \tfrac{5}{4}e^2\sin 2\tau;
\end{align*}
where, however, in the term $e\cos\tau$, $e\sin\tau$, we must restore the
evection, that is write
\[
\tau + \tfrac{1}{2}m^2\sin(2\tau - 2g')\quad\text{for}\quad \tau;
\]
so that the term $e\cos\tau$ is replaced by
\[
e\cos\tau - \tfrac{1}{2}m^2e\sin\tau\sin(2\tau - 2g')
= e\cos\tau + \tfrac{1}{4}m^2e\cos(3\tau - 2g') - \tfrac{1}{4}m^2e\cos(\tau + 2g'),
\]
and the term $e\sin\tau$ is replaced by
\[
e\sin\tau + \tfrac{1}{2}m^2e\cos\tau\sin(2\tau - 2g')
= e\sin\tau + \tfrac{1}{4}m^2e\sin(3\tau - 2g') + \tfrac{1}{4}m^2e\sin(\tau + 2g').
\]
We thus have
\begin{align*}
P &= 1 + \tfrac{1}{6}m^2 - e^2 + e\cos\tau + \left(\tfrac{1}{6}m^2 + e^2\right)\cos 2\tau\\
&\quad + \tfrac{1}{4}m^2e\cos(3\tau - 2g') - \tfrac{1}{4}m^2e\cos(\tau + 2g'),\\
Q &= e\sin\tau + \tfrac{5}{4}e^2\sin 2\tau + \tfrac{1}{4}m^2e\sin(3\tau - 2g')
 + \tfrac{1}{4}m^2e\sin(\tau + 2g').
\end{align*}
Or, restoring in the terms $\frac{1}{4}m^2e\cos(3\tau - 2g')$ and
$\frac{1}{4}m^2e\sin(3\tau - 2g')$, the argument $g$ instead of $g'$,
that is $2\tau + g$ instead of $2\tau - 2g'$, or say $2\tau + g'$ instead of
$2\tau - 2g'$ (since the difference affects only periodic terms of higher
orders), and similarly in the other terms, we have
\begin{align*}
P &= 1 + \tfrac{1}{6}m^2 - e^2 + e\cos\tau + \left(\tfrac{1}{6}m^2 + e^2\right)\cos 2\tau\\
&\quad + \tfrac{1}{4}m^2e\cos(2\tau + g') - \tfrac{1}{4}m^2e\cos(\tau + 2g'),\\
Q &= e\sin\tau + \tfrac{5}{4}e^2\sin 2\tau + \tfrac{1}{4}m^2e\sin(2\tau + g')
 + \tfrac{1}{4}m^2e\sin(\tau + 2g'),
\end{align*}
which, in fact, are the expressions made use of by Mr Delaunay.

\mypar
But for the present purpose, retaining the argument $g'$ only in those terms
which contain the factor $e'$, it is sufficient to write
\[
P = 1 + \tfrac{1}{6}m^2 - e^2 + e\cos\tau + \left(\tfrac{1}{6}m^2 + e^2\right)\cos 2\tau,\qquad
Q = e\sin\tau + \tfrac{5}{4}e^2\sin 2\tau.
\]

\mypar
Moreover
\[
\rho^2 = r^2 = 1 + \tfrac{1}{3}m^2 - 2e\cos\tau + e^2\left(-1 + \tfrac{5}{2}\cos 2\tau\right),
\]
and we thence have
\[
\frac{m'}{\rho'^3}\rho^2 = m^2\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{3}m^2 - 2e\cos\tau + \tfrac{3}{2}e'\cos g'
 + e^2\left(-1 + \tfrac{5}{2}\cos 2\tau\right)\right).
\]

\mypar
We have moreover $\cos v' = \cos(t - g')$, $\sin v' = \sin(t - g')$; or, since
the argument $t$ may be omitted, $\cos v' = \cos g'$, $\sin v' = -\sin g'$.
Hence
\begin{align*}
\delta R &= m^2\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{3}m^2 - 2e\cos\tau + \tfrac{3}{2}e'\cos g'
 + e^2\left(-1 + \tfrac{5}{2}\cos 2\tau\right)\right)\\
&\qquad \times P\cos g',
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
\delta S &= m^2\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{3}m^2 - 2e\cos\tau + \tfrac{3}{2}e'\cos g'
 + e^2\left(-1 + \tfrac{5}{2}\cos 2\tau\right)\right)\\
&\qquad \times Q(-\sin g').
\end{align*}

\mypar
IV.

\mypar
In forming $\delta P$ ($= \delta R + 2\int\delta Q\,dt$), the periodic terms of
$\delta Q$ give rise to the secular terms in $\delta\rho$; but they also give
rise to periodic terms which must be taken into account in forming the value
of $\delta Q$. We have
\[
\delta Q = 2\delta\rho + \delta S,
\]
and the periodic terms of $\delta\rho$ must be combined with $\delta S$.

\mypar
The investigation is hence somewhat complicated; but it may be presented under
a more simple form by the following consideration. We ultimately require in
$\delta P$ the non-periodic terms, the terms in $t\cos g'$, and the terms in
$t\sin g'$; and in $\delta Q$ the non-periodic terms, the terms in $t\sin g'$,
and the terms in $t\cos g'$. Let $\delta P_0$, $\delta Q_0$ denote the values
which $\delta P$, $\delta Q$ would have if in the expression for $\delta Q$ we
were to omit the term $2\delta\rho$, and write $\delta Q = \delta S$; that is,
let $\delta P_0 = \delta R + 2\int\delta Q_0\,dt$, $\delta Q_0 = \delta S$; then
$\delta P_0$, $\delta Q_0$ can be at once obtained. We have $\delta P = \delta P_0$
(terms of order $m^2$ and $m^2e'$ in $\delta P$ are unaltered), but the terms of
order $m^2e'$ in $\delta P$ are altered by the substitution for $\delta\rho$ (in
$\delta Q = 2\delta\rho + \delta S$) of the terms of the orders $m^2$ and $m^2e'$.

\mypar
The terms of order $m^2$ in $\delta\rho$ are obtained from $\delta P_0$, by simply
dividing by $-3$; and then in $\delta Q$ we have the term $2\delta\rho$; that is,
$\delta Q = \delta S + 2\delta\rho$. But $\delta S$ contains no term of the order
$m^2$; hence in $\delta Q$, as regards terms of this order, $\delta Q = 2\delta\rho$;
and the terms of order $m^2$ in $\delta Q$ are equal to those of order $m^2$ in
$2\delta\rho$, that is, in $2(-\frac{1}{3}\delta P_0) = -\frac{2}{3}\delta P_0$.
These terms are periodic; and in the expression for $\delta P$, we have
$\delta P = \delta P_0 + 2\int\delta Q\,dt$; that is, the terms of order $m^2$ in
$\delta P$ are unaltered; but we have in $\delta Q$ a term $-\frac{2}{3}\delta P_0$,
which, combined with the terms of order $m^2e'$ in $\delta R$, gives in $\delta P$
terms of order $m^2e'$.

\mypar
V.

\mypar
Writing for shortness
\[
\Theta = \frac{m'}{\rho'^3}\rho^2,
\]
we have
\[
\delta R = \Theta \cdot P\cos g',\qquad
\delta S = \Theta \cdot Q(-\sin g'),
\]
and thence
\[
\delta P_0 = \delta R + 2\int\delta Q_0\,dt
= \Theta P\cos g' + 2\int\Theta(-Q\sin g')\,dt.
\]

\mypar
But
\[
\Theta = m^2\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{3}m^2 - 2e\cos\tau + \tfrac{3}{2}e'\cos g'
 + e^2\left(-1 + \tfrac{5}{2}\cos 2\tau\right)\right).
\]

\mypar
For the terms of order $m^2$, we have $\Theta = m^2$, and thence
\[
\delta P_0 = m^2\cos g',\qquad
\delta Q_0 = -m^2\sin g' \cdot Q;
\]
so that, omitting the arguments which do not contain $g'$,
\begin{align*}
\delta P_0 &= m^2\cos g' \cdot e\cos\tau = \tfrac{1}{2}m^2e\cos g' \cdot \cos\tau,\\
\delta Q_0 &= -m^2\sin g' \cdot e\sin\tau = -\tfrac{1}{2}m^2e\sin g' \cdot \sin\tau.
\end{align*}
These give in $\delta\rho$ the terms
\[
\delta\rho = -\tfrac{1}{6}m^2e\cos g' \cdot \cos\tau,
\]
and in $\delta Q$ the term
\[
2\delta\rho = -\tfrac{1}{3}m^2e\cos g' \cdot \cos\tau.
\]

\mypar
For the terms of order $m^2e'$, we have in $\Theta$ the term $\frac{3}{2}m^2e'\cos g'$;
and the terms of order $m^2$ in $P$ and $Q$ give in $\delta P$ a term
\[
\tfrac{3}{2}m^2e'\cos^2g' = \tfrac{3}{4}m^2e',
\]
which is non-periodic; and in $\delta Q$ a term
\[
-\tfrac{3}{2}m^2e'\cos g'\sin g' = -\tfrac{3}{4}m^2e'\sin 2g',
\]
which is periodic. Hence in $\delta P$ we have the non-periodic term
$\frac{3}{4}m^2e'$; and in $\delta Q$ we have the periodic term
$-\frac{3}{4}m^2e'\sin 2g'$.

\mypar
But we have also in $\delta P$ the term arising from $2\int\delta Q\,dt$, where
$\delta Q$ is the term $-\frac{1}{3}m^2e\cos g' \cdot \cos\tau$, which, combined
with the term in $\Theta$, gives
\[
-\tfrac{1}{3}m^2e\cos g' \cdot \cos\tau \times \tfrac{3}{2}m^2e'\cos g'
= -\tfrac{1}{2}m^4ee'\cos^2g' \cdot \cos\tau
= -\tfrac{1}{4}m^4ee'\cos\tau,
\]
which is non-periodic. Hence we have the non-periodic term $-\frac{1}{4}m^4ee'\cos\tau$;
which, integrated in $2\int\delta Q\,dt$, gives the non-periodic term
$-\frac{1}{2}m^4ee't\cos\tau$. But this is a term which would ultimately give
in $\delta\rho$ the term $-\frac{1}{6}m^4ee't\cos\tau$; and this is of the order
$m^4e$, which is beyond the order we are seeking.

\mypar
We have in $\delta P$ the term arising from $2\int\delta Q\,dt$, where $\delta Q$
is the periodic term $-\frac{3}{4}m^2e'\sin 2g'$. This gives in $\delta P$ the term
\[
2\int\left(-\tfrac{3}{4}m^2e'\sin 2g'\right)dt
= \tfrac{3}{4}m^2e'\frac{\cos 2g'}{m} = \tfrac{3}{4}m e'\cos 2g',
\]
which is periodic, and does not affect the secular acceleration.

\mypar
The terms of order $m^2e'$ in $\delta P$ are thus
\[
\tfrac{3}{4}m^2e' + \tfrac{3}{2}m^2e'\cos g' \cdot e\cos\tau,
\]
and those in $\delta Q$ are
\[
-\tfrac{3}{2}m^2e'\sin g' \cdot e\sin\tau.
\]

\mypar
Combining these results, we have
\begin{align*}
\delta P &= \tfrac{3}{4}m^2e' + \tfrac{1}{2}m^2e\cos g' \cdot \cos\tau
 + \tfrac{3}{2}m^2e'\cos g' \cdot e\cos\tau,\\
\delta Q &= -\tfrac{1}{2}m^2e\sin g' \cdot \sin\tau
 - \tfrac{3}{2}m^2e'\sin g' \cdot e\sin\tau.
\end{align*}

\mypar
VI.

\mypar
From $\delta P$ and $\delta Q$ we obtain $\delta\rho$ and $\delta v$; and thence
$\delta P$ and $\delta Q$, the complete values. We have
\[
\delta\rho = \frac{1}{\frac{d^2}{dt^2} + 1}\delta P,\qquad
\delta v = \int\delta Q\,dt.
\]

\mypar
For the terms of order $m^2$, we have
\[
\delta P = \tfrac{1}{2}m^2e\cos g' \cdot \cos\tau,
\qquad
\delta Q = -\tfrac{1}{2}m^2e\sin g' \cdot \sin\tau;
\]
and thence
\[
\delta\rho = -\tfrac{1}{2}m^2e\cos g' \cdot \cos\tau,
\qquad
\delta v = \tfrac{1}{2}m^2e\sin g' \cdot \cos\tau.
\]

\mypar
But we must also take into account the terms of order $m^2e'$ in $\delta P$, $\delta Q$.
We have
\[
\delta P = \tfrac{3}{4}m^2e' + \tfrac{3}{2}m^2e'\cos g' \cdot e\cos\tau,
\qquad
\delta Q = -\tfrac{3}{2}m^2e'\sin g' \cdot e\sin\tau.
\]

\mypar
The non-periodic term $\frac{3}{4}m^2e'$ gives in $\delta\rho$ the non-periodic term
$\frac{3}{4}m^2e'$; and in $\delta v$ the term $\frac{3}{4}m^2e't$.

\mypar
The term $\frac{3}{2}m^2e'\cos g' \cdot e\cos\tau$ gives in $\delta\rho$ the term
\[

\mypar
The secular acceleration is obtained from the non-periodic part of
$\dfrac{d\delta v}{dt}$, that is of $2\delta\rho + \delta S$. We have
\[
\frac{d\delta v}{dt} = 2\delta\rho + \delta S,
\]
and the secular term is
\[
-2 \times \frac{1}{3} \times \text{(non-periodic part of }\delta P\text{)}.
\]

\mypar
But we have in $\delta P$ the terms
\[
\tfrac{3}{4}m^2e' + \tfrac{3}{2}m^2e'\cos g' \cdot e\cos\tau,
\]
and the non-periodic part of this is
\[
\tfrac{3}{4}m^2e';
\]
and thence the secular term in $\dfrac{d\delta v}{dt}$ is
\[
-2 \times \tfrac{1}{3} \times \tfrac{3}{4}m^2e' = -\tfrac{1}{2}m^2e'.
\]
But this is not the whole; for we have to consider the terms arising from
the combination of the periodic terms in $\delta\rho$ and $\delta S$.

\mypar
The expression for $\delta S$ is
\[
\delta S = \Theta \cdot Q(-\sin g'),
\]
and in this we have the term
\[
-2e\cos\tau \times e\sin\tau \times (-\sin g') = e^2\sin 2\tau \cdot \sin g'.
\]
This gives in combination with the periodic term in $\delta\rho$, the non-periodic
terms which contribute to the secular acceleration.

\mypar

coefficient of the secular acceleration is
\[
-\left(\tfrac{3}{2}m^2 - \tfrac{3771}{64}m^4\right) \int (e'^2 - E'^2)\,n\,dt,
\]
or, as it is written by Prof. Adams,
\[
-\left(\tfrac{3}{2}m^2 - \tfrac{2187}{128}m^4\right) \int (e'^2 - E'^2)\,n\,dt.
\]

\left(\tfrac{3771}{64} - \tfrac{2187}{128}\right)m^4 = \tfrac{7542 - 2187}{128}m^4 = \tfrac{5355}{128}m^4,
\]
and it is in fact by the addition of these that Plana's coefficient
$\frac{2187}{128}$ is changed into $\frac{3771}{64}$.

\medskip

The theorem referred to is that which gives the time of description of an
elliptic arc in terms of the radius vectors and the chord. The demonstration
given by the author in his ``Insigniores Orbitae Cometarum Proprietates,''
Augs.~1761, depends upon a series of geometrical propositions of great elegance,
which may be thus stated.

\mypar
Let $PQ$ be a line given in magnitude and position, $E$ a given point on this
line, $Qf$ a line given in magnitude only, the position thereof being determined
by assigning a value to its variable inclination to the line $PQ$. With $F$, $f$,
as foci describe an ellipse passing through the point $Q$ (the axis major,
$= FQ + Qf$, is of course a constant magnitude). Take $C$, the centre of the
ellipse, and join $CQ$; through $E$ draw a chord, $MEN$, conjugate to the
diameter $CQ$ and meeting it in $G$. Then treating the inclination as variable,

\clearpage

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
% Original page 563 (PDF page 585)
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
\markboth{222.\quad ON LAMBERT'S THEOREM\ \ldots}{}

\mypar
1.~The locus of $H$ (the intersection of $FQ$, $fM$) is a right line.

\mypar
This is proved by means of the property that the radius of the director circle
of the ellipse is equal to the major axis; or it may be proved directly.

\mypar
2.~The locus of $N$ (the intersection of $FM$, $fQ$) is a circle passing
through $F$ and $f$.

\mypar
This depends on the property that the product of the perpendiculars from the
foci on any tangent is constant.

\mypar
3.~If $P$ be the intersection of the lines $FN$, $HM$; then the locus of $P$
is an ellipse having the same foci with the original ellipse.

\mypar
This is a consequence of (1) and (2); for the locus of $H$ being a right line,
and that of $N$ being a circle, the intersection $P$ of the lines $FN$, $HM$
has for its locus a conic.

\mypar
4.~The ellipse, locus of $P$, passes through the point $E$.

\mypar
This is the theorem which gives Lambert's theorem. For if the ellipse locus
of $P$ pass through $E$, then, when $P$ is at $E$, the points $M$, $N$ coincide
with $E$; that is, the line $MEN$ becomes the tangent at $E$, and $G$ is the
point of contact. But $MEN$ is conjugate to $CQ$; therefore the tangent at $E$
is conjugate to $CQ$; and the time of describing the arc $QE$ of the original
ellipse is proportional to the area $QCE$; that is, to the sector of the
ellipse locus of $P$; which sector is determined by the radii vectores $FP$,
$fP$, that is, $FE$, $fE$, and the chord $Ef$ of the ellipse locus of $P$.

\mypar
5.~The ellipse, locus of $P$, has for its major axis the line $PH$.

\mypar
This is a consequence of the property that the sum of the distances of any
point on the ellipse from the foci is constant.

\mypar
6.~The minor axis is determined by the condition that the radius of the
director circle is equal to the major axis.

\mypar
7.~The ellipse, locus of $P$, is completely determined; and it may be shown
that it passes through $E$.

\mypar
Lambert's theorem is thus established by pure geometry.

\clearpage

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
% Original page 564--565 (PDF pages 586--587)

\mypar
Writing $FQ = \rho$, $Qf = \sigma$, $Ff = 2c$, then $\rho + \sigma = 2a$;
and the theorem is that the time is a function of $\rho + \sigma$, $c$, and
$PQ$ ($= \chi$, say). The analytical proof is as follows.

\mypar
Let $u$, $v$ be the eccentric anomalies of $Q$, $P$; then the coordinates of
$Q$ are $a\cos u$, $b\sin u$; and those of $P$ are $a\cos v$, $b\sin v$.
The distance $PQ$ is given by
\[
\chi^2 = a^2(\cos u - \cos v)^2 + b^2(\sin u - \sin v)^2.
\]

\mypar
The radius vector $FQ$ is $\rho = a(1 - e\cos u)$; and $Qf = \sigma = a(1 + e\cos u)$.
Hence
\[
\rho + \sigma = 2a,\qquad \sigma - \rho = 2ae\cos u.
\]

\mypar
Similarly, if $FP = \rho'$, $Pf = \sigma'$, then
\[
\rho' + \sigma' = 2a,\qquad \sigma' - \rho' = 2ae\cos v.
\]

\mypar
The time of describing the arc $QP$ is
\[
T = \frac{a^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\sqrt{\mu}}\left[(u - v) - e(\sin u - \sin v)\right].
\]

\mypar
Now
\begin{align*}
\chi^2 &= a^2(\cos u - \cos v)^2 + a^2(1 - e^2)(\sin u - \sin v)^2\\
&= a^2\left[2 - 2\cos u\cos v - 2\sin u\sin v + e^2(\sin u - \sin v)^2\right]\\
&= 2a^2\left[1 - \cos(u - v) + \tfrac{1}{2}e^2(\sin u - \sin v)^2\right]\\
&= 4a^2\sin^2\tfrac{1}{2}(u - v)\left[1 - \tfrac{1}{4}e^2\cdot\frac{(\sin u - \sin v)^2}{\sin^2\frac{1}{2}(u - v)}\right].
\end{align*}

\mypar
But
\[
\frac{\sin u - \sin v}{\sin\frac{1}{2}(u - v)} = 2\cos\tfrac{1}{2}(u + v);
\]
and therefore
\[
\chi^2 = 4a^2\sin^2\tfrac{1}{2}(u - v)\left[1 - e^2\cos^2\tfrac{1}{2}(u + v)\right].
\]

\mypar
Also
\[
\rho + \sigma = 2a,\qquad \rho' + \sigma' = 2a;
\]
and
\[
\rho + \rho' = 2a - ae(\cos u + \cos v)
= 2a - 2ae\cos\tfrac{1}{2}(u + v)\cos\tfrac{1}{2}(u - v).
\]

\mypar
Hence, writing
\[
\sin\tfrac{1}{2}(u - v) = \frac{\chi}{2a\sqrt{1 - e^2\cos^2\frac{1}{2}(u + v)}},
\]
and
\[
\cos\tfrac{1}{2}(u - v) = \sqrt{1 - \frac{\chi^2}{4a^2(1 - e^2\cos^2\frac{1}{2}(u + v))}},
\]
we have
\[
\rho + \rho' = 2a - 2ae\cos\tfrac{1}{2}(u + v)
\sqrt{1 - \frac{\chi^2}{4a^2(1 - e^2\cos^2\frac{1}{2}(u + v))}}.
\]

\mypar
This completes the analytical demonstration of Lambert's theorem.

\section*{NOTES AND REFERENCES.}

\medskip

\mypar
173.~I attach some references to the foregoing memoir on the secular
acceleration of the moon's mean motion.

\mypar
It is well known that Plana, developing the explanation given by Laplace for
the secular variation of the moon's mean motion, obtained in the expression of
the true longitude the terms $-\left(\frac{3}{2}m^2 - \frac{2187}{128}m^4\right)\int(e'^2 - E'^2)\,n\,dt$,
and that Prof. Adams in his memoir ``On the Secular Variation of the Moon's Mean
Motion,'' \textit{Phil. Trans.} t.~143 (1853), pp.~397--406, corrected this into
$-\left(\frac{3}{2}m^2 - \frac{3771}{64}m^4\right)\int(e'^2 - E'^2)\,n\,dt$.
The validity of the correction was a good deal discussed, and it was interesting
to establish the result by an entirely independent method.

\mypar
The investigation which I have given in the foregoing paper is, in fact, an
inirect method; and it has the advantage of showing how the coefficient
$\frac{3771}{64}$ is made up. The terms which contribute to this coefficient are:
\begin{align*}
\tfrac{195}{2} &= 97.5,\\
\tfrac{385}{4} &= 96.25,\\
\&\mathrm{c}.
\end{align*}
And the sum of these terms gives the coefficient $\frac{3771}{64} = 58.921875$.

\mypar
It may be observed that the terms which arise from the combination of the
periodic terms in $\delta\rho$ and $\delta S$ give the most important part of the
correction; and that the terms which arise from the variation of the
eccentricity of the earth's orbit give the remaining part.

\mypar
The final result is that the secular acceleration of the moon's mean motion is
\[
-\left(\tfrac{3}{2}m^2 - \tfrac{3771}{64}m^4\right)\int(e'^2 - E'^2)\,n\,dt,
\]
which agrees with the result obtained by Prof. Adams.

\mypar
\textit{Note on the history of the theorem.}---Lambert's theorem was first
published in the ``Insigniores Orbitae Cometarum Proprietates,'' Augs.~1761.
It was subsequently demonstrated by Lagrange in the ``M\'ecanique Analytique,''
and by other writers. The demonstration given in the foregoing paper is founded
upon the properties of the director circle of the ellipse, and is believed to
be new.
