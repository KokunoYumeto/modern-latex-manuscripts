\section{Paper 213: On the Development of the Disturbing Function in the Lunar Theory}
\label{sec:paper213}

\noindent\textit{[From the \textit{Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society}, vol.~xviii.~(1858), pp.~199--204.]}

\medskip\noindent
The disturbing function is here developed as far as the fifth order inclusive (the eccentricities and inclination being treated as of the same order), for the special case of the moon; the development is in fact that of the function
\[
\Omega = m' \frac{a^2}{a'^3} \left(\frac{3}{2}\eta^2 - \frac{3}{2}\eta^4\right)
\]
multiplied into
\[
\frac{a'^3}{r'^3} \cdot \frac{r^2}{a^2} \left(\frac{r}{a}\right)^n \cos j(U - U'),
\]
where $n$ is = 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4; or say it is the development of
\[
\Omega_n = m' \frac{a^2}{a'^3} \left(\frac{3}{2}\eta^2 - \frac{3}{2}\eta^4\right) \text{ multiplied into } \frac{a'^3}{r'^3} \cdot \frac{r^2}{a^2} \left(\frac{r}{a}\right)^n \cos j(U - U').
\]

\subsection{Note on the Developments (pp.~309--319)}

\noindent
The above developments have been calculated independently by the method of special values of the variables. The general form of the disturbing function is
\[
\Omega = m' \frac{a^2}{a'^3} \sum A_{i,j,k,l} \cdot e^i e'^j \gamma^k \cos(i\varpi + j\varpi' + k\theta + l\Theta'),
\]
where $\gamma = \sin\frac{1}{2}I$, and $I$ is the inclination.

\medskip\noindent
The values have been verified by comparison with the results of Lubbock and Pont\'{e}coulant. The following table shows the comparison of the several terms:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|c|c|c}
\toprule
Term & Lubbock & Pont\'{e}coulant & Cayley \\
\midrule
$\Omega_0$ & $(0)$ & Same & $m' \dfrac{a^2}{a'^3}(\cdots)$ \\
$\Omega_1$ & $(1)$ & Same & $m' \dfrac{a^2}{a'^3}(\cdots)$ \\
$\Omega_2$ & $(2)$ & Same & $m' \dfrac{a^2}{a'^3}(\cdots)$ \\
$\Omega_3$ & $(3)$ & Same & $m' \dfrac{a^2}{a'^3}(\cdots)$ \\
$\Omega_4$ & $(4)$ & Same & $m' \dfrac{a^2}{a'^3}(\cdots)$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip\noindent
The complete expression for $\Omega_0$ is:
\begin{align*}
\Omega_0 = m' \frac{a^2}{a'^3} \Big\{& \frac{1}{4} + \frac{3}{8}e^2 + \frac{15}{64}e^4 - \frac{3}{4}e'^2 + \frac{9}{4}e^2e'^2 + \frac{3}{8}e'^4 + \frac{3}{2}\gamma^2 \\
& - \frac{9}{4}e^2\gamma^2 - \frac{3}{2}e'^2\gamma^2 + \frac{15}{8}\gamma^4 \Big\},
\end{align*}
and similarly for the higher orders.

\medskip\noindent
The developments agree with those of Delaunay as far as the terms which he has calculated.

\section{Paper 214: The First Part of a Memoir on the Development of the Disturbing Function in the Lunar and Planetary Theories}
\label{sec:paper214}

\noindent\textit{[From the \textit{Philosophical Transactions}, vol.~cxlviii.~(1858), pp.~435--471.]}
\medskip\noindent
\textit{Received February 11,---Read March 11, 1858.}

\medskip\noindent
The disturbing function in the lunar and planetary theories requires to be developed in terms of the elements of the two bodies. The object of the present memoir is to establish the general formulae for this development, and to apply them to the calculation of the numerical coefficients as far as a certain order.

\subsection{Preliminary Formulae (pp.~319--323)}

\noindent
The reciprocal of the distance between the two bodies is
\[
\frac{1}{\Delta} = \frac{1}{r'} \left\{1 + \left(\frac{r}{r'}\right) \cos S + \left(\frac{r}{r'}\right)^2 \left(\frac{3}{2}\cos^2 S - \frac{1}{2}\right) + \left(\frac{r}{r'}\right)^3 \left(\frac{5}{2}\cos^3 S - \frac{3}{2}\cos S\right) + \cdots \right\},
\]
or as it may be written,
\[
\frac{1}{\Delta} = \frac{1}{r'} \sum_n \left(\frac{r}{r'}\right)^n P_n(\cos S),
\]
where $S$ is the angle between the radius vectors, and $P_n$ is the Legendre coefficient.

\medskip\noindent
Let the longitude in orbit be denoted by $v$, the mean anomaly by $g$, the mean longitude by $\epsilon$, the longitude of perihelion by $\varpi$, and the eccentricity by $e$. We have
\[
v = nt + \varpi + e\sin g + \frac{1}{2}e^2 \sin 2g + \frac{1}{8}e^3(3\sin 3g - \sin g) + \dots
\]
and
\[
\frac{r}{a} = 1 - e\cos g - \frac{1}{2}e^2(\cos 2g - 1) - \frac{3}{8}e^3(\cos 3g - \cos g) - \dots
\]

\subsection{Formulae for the Development (pp.~323--326)}

\noindent
\textbf{I.} Let $j$, $j'$ be positive or negative integers. We require the development of
\[
\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)^n \cos j(v - nt - \varpi) \quad \text{and} \quad \left(\frac{r}{a}\right)^n \sin j(v - nt - \varpi).
\]
Writing $v - nt - \varpi = f$, the true anomaly, we have
\[
\frac{r}{a} = \frac{1 - e^2}{1 + e\cos f}, \qquad \frac{r^2}{a^2} \frac{df}{dg} = \sqrt{1 - e^2},
\]
and thence
\[
\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)^n \cos jf = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{+\infty} X_k^{n,j} \cos kg, \qquad
\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)^n \sin jf = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{+\infty} X_k^{n,j} \sin kg,
\]
where the coefficients $X_k^{n,j}$ are functions of the eccentricity.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{II.} The development of $\cos j(v - v')$ and $\sin j(v - v')$ is obtained by means of the formulae
\begin{align*}
\cos j(v - v') &= \cos j[(v - nt - \varpi) - (v' - nt - \varpi') + (\varpi - \varpi') + nt - nt'], \\
\sin j(v - v') &= \sin j[(v - nt - \varpi) - (v' - nt - \varpi') + (\varpi - \varpi') + nt - nt'].
\end{align*}

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{III.} The disturbing function may be written
\[
\frac{1}{\Delta} = \frac{1}{r'} \sum_n \left(\frac{r}{r'}\right)^n X_n^{(0)} P_n(\cos S),
\]
where $X_n^{(0)}$ denotes the value of $\left(\dfrac{r'}{a'}\right)^{-n-1}$ developed in terms of the eccentricity $e'$.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{IV.} But it is necessary to express $\cos S$ in terms of the mutual inclination of the orbits. Let $I$ be the inclination, $\Omega$ the longitude of the node; then
\[
\cos S = \cos(v - \Omega)\cos(v' - \Omega) + \cos I \sin(v - \Omega)\sin(v' - \Omega).
\]

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{V.} Considering $P_n(\cos S)$ as a function of $\cos S$, we may write
\[
P_n(\cos S) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} B_k^{(n)} \cos k(v - v') + \sum_{k=1}^{n} C_k^{(n)} \cos k(v + v' - 2\Omega),
\]
where the coefficients $B_k^{(n)}$, $C_k^{(n)}$ are functions of $\cos I$.

\subsection{The Coefficients $X_k^{n,j}$ (pp.~326--335)}

\noindent
The coefficients $X_k^{n,j}$ are calculated by means of the definite integral
\[
X_k^{n,j} = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} \left(\frac{r}{a}\right)^n \cos jf \cos kg \, dg,
\]
and similarly for the sine terms.

\medskip\noindent
For the special case $n = -1$, we have
\[
X_0^{-1,0} = 1, \quad X_1^{-1,0} = -e, \quad X_2^{-1,0} = \frac{1}{2}e^2, \quad X_3^{-1,0} = -\frac{3}{8}e^3, \quad \dots
\]

\medskip\noindent
The general values are given by the formula
\[
X_k^{n,j} = \frac{(n+2)(n+3)\cdots(n+k+1)}{1 \cdot 2 \cdots k} \left(\frac{e}{2}\right)^k F\left(-n-k-1, -k, j-k+1, \frac{e^2}{4}\right),
\]
where $F$ denotes the hypergeometric function.

\medskip\noindent
The following table gives the values of $X_k^{n,j}$ for $n = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$:

\subsection{Development in Terms of the Elements (pp.~335--344)}

The general term of the disturbing function is of the form
\[

\]
where $\gamma = \sin\frac{1}{2}I$, and $\theta$, $\Theta$ are angles depending on the longitudes of the node and the epoch.

2 & 0 & 2 & $-\dfrac{9}{4}$ & $2\varpi - 2\theta$ \\

\medskip\noindent
The development for the planetary theory is similar, but with different numerical values depending on the ratio of the mean distances.

\medskip\noindent
The comparison with Delaunay's results shows complete agreement as far as the fifth order.

\section{Paper 215: A Supplementary Memoir on the Problem of Disturbed Elliptic Motion}
\label{sec:paper215}

\noindent\textit{[From the \textit{Philosophical Transactions}, vol.~cxlviii.~(1858), pp.~471--475.]}
\medskip\noindent
\textit{Received March 11,---Read April 8, 1858.}

\medskip\noindent
The following is a supplementary memoir to the author's former memoir on the Problem of Disturbed Elliptic Motion (see Vol.~III, pp.~210--233). In that memoir the equations were established on the assumption that the disturbing function was expressed in terms of the elliptic elements; the present memoir extends the investigation to the case where the disturbing function is expressed in terms of the coordinates and velocities.

\subsection{Equations of Motion (pp.~344--346)}

\noindent
Let $r$, $p$, $y$ be the coordinates of the moon ($y$ being ultimately put equal to zero); the general equations of motion are
\[
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dT}{d\dot{r}}\right) - \frac{dT}{dr} = \frac{dV}{dr},
\]
\[
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dT}{d\dot{p}}\right) - \frac{dT}{dp} = \frac{dV}{dp},
\]
\[
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dT}{d\dot{y}}\right) - \frac{dT}{dy} = \frac{dV}{dy},
\]
where if for the moment $\dot{r}$, $\dot{p}$, $\dot{y}$ denote the differential coefficients of $r$, $p$, $y$, the expression of the force function $V$ is $V = -\dfrac{n^2a^3}{r} + \Omega$, where $n^2a^3$ denotes an absolute constant, the sum of the masses, and the disturbing function $\Omega$ has a contrary sign to that of the \textit{M\'{e}canique C\'{e}leste}.

\medskip\noindent
We may in the first and second equations write at once $Tdt^2 = \frac{1}{2}(dr^2 + r^2dp^2)$, and these equations thus become
\[
\frac{d}{dt}\frac{dr}{dt} - r\left(\frac{dp}{dt}\right)^2 = \frac{n^2a^3}{r^2} + \frac{d\Omega}{dr},
\]
\[
\frac{d}{dt}\left(r^2\frac{dp}{dt}\right) = \frac{d\Omega}{dp}.
\]

\subsection{Transformation of the Equations (pp.~346--347)}

\noindent
If in the third equation we take the general value of $T$ and after the differentiation with respect to $\dot{y}$, make the substitutions $y = 0$, $Q = 0$, $Rdt = dp$, we find
\[
\frac{dT}{d\dot{y}} = 0,
\]
and the equation thus becomes
\[
-\frac{dT}{dy} = \frac{d\Omega}{dy}.
\]

\medskip\noindent
But $Tdt^2 = \frac{1}{2}\{dr^2 + r^2(Q^2 + R^2)dt^2\}$, consequently
\[
-\frac{d\Omega}{dy}dt = \frac{dT}{dy}dt = r^2\left(Q\frac{dQ}{dy} + R\frac{dR}{dy}\right)dt = r^2dp\frac{dR}{dy},
\]
and from the value of $R$, putting after the differentiation $y = 0$, we find
\begin{align*}
\frac{dR}{dy}dt &= -[\sin(p - \Sigma)\sin\Phi\,d\Theta + \cos(p - \Sigma)\,d\Phi] \\
&\quad - \cos(p - \Sigma)\quad [\sin(\Theta - \sigma')\sin\phi'\,d\theta' + \cos(\Theta - \sigma')\,d\phi'] \\
&\quad - \sin(p - \Sigma)\cos\Phi[\cos(\Theta - \sigma')\sin\phi'\,d\theta - \sin(\Theta - \sigma')\,d\phi'].
\end{align*}

\subsection{Final Form of the Equations (pp.~347--350)}

\noindent
The final form of the equations is as follows. Let $H$ be the disturbing function expressed in terms of the coordinates and velocities; then the variations of the elements are given by
\[
\frac{dD}{dt} = -\frac{dH}{dE}, \qquad \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{dH}{dD},
\]
and similar equations for the other elements, where $D$, $E$ are the Delaunay elements.

\medskip\noindent
The quantity $H$ is given by the formula
\[
H = \Omega - \frac{r^2}{2n^2a^4}\left\{\left(\frac{d\Omega}{dr}\right)^2 + \frac{1}{r^2}\left(\frac{d\Omega}{dp}\right)^2\right\} + \dots
\]

\medskip\noindent
The development of $H$ in terms of the elements is effected by substituting for $r$, $p$, $\dot{r}$, $\dot{p}$ their elliptic values, and expanding in powers of the eccentricity and inclination.

\medskip\noindent
The results agree with those of the former memoir, and with the formulae of Hansen and Delaunay.

\medskip\noindent
The following formulae are obtained for the variations of the elements in terms of $H$:
\begin{align*}
\frac{da}{dt} &= \frac{2a^2}{n} \frac{dH}{d\epsilon}, \\
\frac{d\epsilon}{dt} &= -\frac{2a^2}{n} \frac{dH}{da} + \frac{a\sqrt{1-e^2}}{ne} \left(1 - \sqrt{1-e^2}\right) \frac{dH}{de}, \\
\frac{d\varpi}{dt} &= \frac{a\sqrt{1-e^2}}{ne} \left(1 - \sqrt{1-e^2}\right) \frac{dH}{de} + \frac{\tan\frac{1}{2}I}{na^2\sqrt{1-e^2}} \frac{dH}{dI}, \\
\frac{de}{dt} &= -\frac{a\sqrt{1-e^2}}{ne} \frac{dH}{d\varpi} - \frac{a\sqrt{1-e^2}}{ne} \left(1 - \sqrt{1-e^2}\right) \frac{dH}{d\epsilon}, \\
\frac{dI}{dt} &= \frac{1}{na^2\sqrt{1-e^2}\sin I} \frac{dH}{d\theta} - \frac{\tan\frac{1}{2}I}{na^2\sqrt{1-e^2}} \left(\frac{dH}{d\varpi} + \frac{dH}{d\epsilon}\right), \\
\frac{d\theta}{dt} &= -\frac{1}{na^2\sqrt{1-e^2}\sin I} \frac{dH}{dI}.
\end{align*}
