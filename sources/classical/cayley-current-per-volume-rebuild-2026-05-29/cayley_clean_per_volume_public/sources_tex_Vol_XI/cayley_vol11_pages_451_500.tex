The equation of the pair of tangent planes through the line $(x, y, z)$ to the
cone $q''r''X^2 + r''p''Y^2 + p''q''Z^2 = 0$ is
\[
(q''r''xX + r''p''yY + p''q''zZ)^2

\]
viz.\ omitting a factor $p''q''r''$, this equation is
\[
(p'', q'', r'', 0, 0, 0 \mid yZ-zY,\, zX-xZ,\, xY-yX)^2 = 0.
\]
And it is to be shown that this is equivalent to the former equation;
viz.\ writing
\[
yZ - zY = \lambda,\quad zX - xZ = \mu,\quad xY - yX = \nu,
\]
then that the two equations
\[
(q'z^2 + r'y^2,\, r'x^2 + p'z^2,\, p'y^2 + q'x^2,\, -p'yz,\, -q'zx,\, -r'xy \mid \lambda, \mu, \nu)^2 = 0,
\]
\[
p''\lambda^2 + q''\mu^2 + r''\nu^2 = 0,
\]
are equivalent to each other.

We have identically $\lambda x + \mu y + \nu z = 0$, and thence also
\[
(\lambda x + \mu y + \nu z)[(-p'+q'+r')\lambda x + (p'-q'+r')\mu y + (p'+q'-r')\nu z] = 0,
\]
where, on the left-hand side, the terms in $\mu\nu$, $\nu\lambda$, and $\lambda\mu$ are
\[
= -2p'yz\mu\nu - 2q'zx\nu\lambda - 2r'xy\lambda\mu.
\]
Hence the first equation may be written
\begin{multline*}
[q'z^2 + r'y^2 + (-p'+q'-r')x^2]\,\lambda^2
+ [r'x^2 + p'z^2 + (p'-q'-r')y^2]\,\mu^2 \\
+ [p'y^2 + q'x^2 + (p'+q'-r')z^2]\,\nu^2 = 0,
\end{multline*}
and it is to be shown that this is equivalent to
\[
p''\lambda^2 + q''\mu^2 + r''\nu^2 = 0;
\]
viz.\ that we have $p'':q'':r'' = A:B:C$, where
\begin{align*}
A &= q'z^2 + r'y^2 - (p'-q'-r')x^2,\\
B &= r'x^2 + p'z^2 - (-p'+q'-r')y^2,\\
C &= p'y^2 + q'x^2 - (-p'-q'+r')z^2,
\end{align*}
and $px^2 + qy^2 + rz^2 = 0$.

Writing the equation in the form $p'':q'':r'' = A:B:C$,
we have
\begin{align*}
A &= q'z^2 + r'y^2 - p'x^2 + q'x^2 + r'x^2\\
&= -p'x^2 + q'(x^2+z^2) + r'(x^2+y^2)\\
&= -p'x^2 + (q'+r')(x^2+y^2+z^2) - q'y^2 - r'z^2.
\end{align*}

By what precedes, we have an identity of the form
\[
x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = \alpha(p'x^2 + q'y^2 + r'z^2) + \beta(px^2 + qy^2 + rz^2),
\]
where, determining $\alpha$ from the equations
\[
1 = q'\alpha + q\beta,\quad 1 = r'\alpha + r\beta,
\]
we find $\alpha = (q-r) \div (qr' - q'r)$; but $px^2 + qy^2 + rz^2 = 0$, and the
relation thus is
\[
x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = \alpha(p'x^2 + q'y^2 + r'z^2);
\]
hence
\[
A = \{(q'+r')\alpha - 1\}(p'x^2 + q'y^2 + r'z^2),
\]
or, substituting for $\alpha$ its value, this is
\[
A = \frac{q'z^2 + r'y^2 - (p'-q'-r')x^2}{qr'-q'r};
\]
and, forming the like values of $B$ and $C$, the relations to be verified become
\[
\frac{p''}{qr'-q'r} = \frac{q''}{rp'-r'p} = \frac{r''}{pq'-p'q},
\]
which are, in fact, the values of the ratios $p'':q'':r''$ obtained above; and
the theorem is thus seen to be true.

\section*{784. Presidential Address to the British Association, September 1883.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{784. Presidential Address to the British Association, September 1883.}

\begin{flushright}
\small[From the Report of the British Association for the Advancement of Science, (1883), pp.~3---37.]
\end{flushright}

Since our last meeting we have been deprived of three of our most
distinguished members. The loss by the death of Professor Henry John
Stephen Smith is a very grievous one to those who knew and admired and
loved him, to his University, and to mathematical science, which he
cultivated with such ardour and success. I need hardly recall that the
branch of mathematics to which he had specially devoted himself was that
most interesting and difficult one, the Theory of Numbers. The immense
range of this subject, connected with and ramifying into so many others,
is nowhere so well seen as in the series of reports on the progress
thereof, brought up unfortunately only to the year 1865, contributed by
him to the Reports of the Association; but it will still better appear
when to these are united (as will be done in the collected works in
course of publication by the Clarendon Press) his other mathematical
writings, many of them containing his own further developments of
theories referred to in the reports. There have been recently or are
being published many such collected editions---Abel, Cauchy, Clifford,
Gauss, Green, Jacobi, Lagrange, Maxwell, Riemann, Steiner. Among these
the works of Henry Smith will occupy a worthy position.

More recently, General Sir Edward Sabine, K.C.B., for twenty-one years
general secretary of the Association, and a trustee, President of the
meeting at Belfast in the year 1852, and for many years treasurer and
afterwards President of the Royal Society, has been taken from us, at an
age exceeding the ordinary age of man. Born October 1788, he entered the
Royal Artillery in 1803, and commanded batteries at the siege of Fort
Erie in 1814; made magnetic and other observations in Ross and Parry's
North Polar exploration in 1818--19, and in a series of other voyages. He
contributed to the Association reports on Magnetic Forces in 1836--7--8,
and about forty papers to the Philosophical Transactions; originated the
system of Magnetic Observatories, and otherwise signally promoted the
science of Terrestrial Magnetism.

There is yet a very great loss: another late President and trustee of the
Association, one who has done for it so much, and has so often attended
the meetings, whose presence among us at this meeting we might have
hoped for---the President of the Royal Society, William Spottiswoode. It
is unnecessary to say anything of his various merits: the place of his
burial, the crowd of sorrowing friends who were present in the Abbey,
bear witness to the esteem in which he was held.

I take the opportunity of mentioning the completion of a work promoted
by the Association: the determination by Mr James Glaisher of the least
factors of the missing three out of the first nine million numbers: the
volume containing the sixth million is now published.

I wish to speak to you to-night upon Mathematics. I am quite aware of
the difficulty arising from the abstract nature of my subject; and if, as
I fear, many or some of you, recalling the Presidential Addresses at
former meetings---for instance, the r\'{e}sum\'{e} and survey which we had
at York of the progress, during the half century of the lifetime of the
Association, of a whole circle of sciences---Biology, Palaeontology,
Geology, Astronomy, Chemistry---so much more familiar to you, and in
which there was so much to tell of the fairy-tales of science; or at
Southampton, the discourse of my friend who has in such kind terms
introduced me to you, on the wondrous practical applications of science
to electric lighting, telegraphy, the St Gothard Tunnel and the Suez
Canal, gun-cotton, and a host of other purposes, and with the grand
concluding speculation on the conservation of solar energy: if, I say,
recalling these or any earlier Addresses, you should wish that you were
now about to have, from a different President, a discourse on a different
subject, I can very well sympathise with you in the feeling. But be this
as it may, I think it is more respectful to you that I should speak to
you upon and do my best to interest you in the subject which has
occupied me, and in which I am myself most interested. And in another
point of view, I think it is right that the Address of a President
should be on his own subject, and that different subjects should be thus
brought in turn before the meetings. So much the worse, it may be, for a
particular meeting; but the meeting is the individual, which on
evolution principles must be sacrificed for the development of the race.

Mathematics connect themselves on the one side with common life and the
physical sciences; on the other side with philosophy, in regard to our
notions of space and time, and in the questions which have arisen as to
the universality and necessity of the truths of mathematics, and the
foundation of our knowledge of them. I would remark here that the
connexion (if it exists) of arithmetic and algebra with the notion of
time is far less obvious than that of geometry with the notion of space.
As to the former side, I am not making before you a defence of
mathematics, but if I were I should desire to do it---in such manner as
in the Republic Socrates was required to defend justice, quite
irrespectively of the worldly advantages which may accompany a life of
virtue and justice, and to show that, independently of all these,
justice was a thing desirable in itself and for its own sake---not by
speaking to you of the utility of mathematics in any of the questions
of common life or of physical science. Still less would I speak of this
utility before, I trust, a friendly audience, interested or willing to
appreciate an interest in mathematics in itself and for its own sake. I
would, on the contrary, rather consider the obligations of mathematics
to these different subjects as the sources of mathematical theories now
as remote from them, and in as different a region of thought---for
instance, geometry from the measurement of land, or the Theory of
Numbers from arithmetic---as a river at its mouth is from its mountain
source.

On the other side, the general opinion has been and is that it is indeed
by experience that we arrive at the truths of mathematics, but that
experience is not their proper foundation: the mind itself contributes
something. This is involved in the Platonic doctrine that the ideas of
mathematics exist in the mind independently of experience; and in a more
developed form it is the Kantian doctrine that the axioms of geometry
and the principles of mathematics generally are \textit{a priori}
intuitions. I would rather, without entering into any discussion of
these philosophical doctrines, speak of the foundation of mathematics in
a more special and practical point of view. And here I would refer to
the valuable work of Whewell, ``The Philosophy of the Inductive
Sciences,'' 1847, and more especially to the last edition, 1860; also to
the account of his views given by himself in the work ``On the
Philosophy of Discovery,'' 1860.


Whewell's view is that mathematical truths are necessary truths; that
is, that the contrary supposition is impossible in the nature of things.
He distinguishes between the necessary truth of a proposition and the
necessity of assenting to it; the latter depending on the constitution of
the human mind, the former on the constitution of things. He holds that
the truths of mathematics are necessary, not in the sense that we cannot
conceive the contrary, but in the sense that the contrary cannot be
true. And he further maintains that the necessity of mathematical
truths is of the same kind as that of the laws of thought; that the
principles of mathematics are involved in the very conditions of
thinking. He would, I think, say that the axioms of geometry are not
merely generalisations from experience, but that they are conditions
without which we cannot think of space at all. I do not know that he
anywhere expressly says this; but it seems to be involved in his general
doctrine. And he would, I think, further say that the axioms of
geometry are not only conditions of thinking, but that they are true of
space; that they are not merely subjective laws of thought, but that
they are objective truths; that they are not only necessary to our
thinking of space, but that they are necessary in the nature of space
itself.

I would speak of the foundation of mathematics in regard to space. We
have the notion of a solid body; and we form the notion of a solid body
of infinite extent, or say of all space. The notion of space is thus a
notion of reality; it is the notion of a solid body of infinite extent.
But in forming this notion we abstract from all the properties of the
solid body except its extension; and the notion of space is thus the
notion of extension alone. It is a notion of reality, but of a reality
which is only extension. The properties of space are thus the
properties of extension; and the axioms of geometry are the general
truths which hold of extension. They are necessary truths, in the sense
that they are involved in the notion of extension; they are necessary in
the nature of extension. And they are known to us \textit{a priori}, in
the sense that we do not learn them from experience, but that they are
involved in the notion of extension which we form independently of
experience.

I would speak of the foundation of mathematics in regard to number. We
have the notion of a plurality of things; and we form the notion of a
plurality of things of the same kind. The notion of number is thus the
notion of a plurality of things of the same kind. But in forming this
notion we abstract from all the properties of the things except their
being things; and the notion of number is thus the notion of a plurality
of units. The properties of number are thus the properties of a
plurality of units; and the laws of arithmetic are the general truths
which hold of a plurality of units. They are necessary truths, in the
sense that they are involved in the notion of a plurality of units;
they are necessary in the nature of a plurality of units. And they are
known to us \textit{a priori}, in the sense that we do not learn them from
experience, but that they are involved in the notion of a plurality of
units which we form independently of experience.

I would speak of the foundation of mathematics in regard to algebra. We
have the notion of magnitude; and we form the notion of magnitude
without reference to any particular kind of magnitude. The notion of
algebraical magnitude is thus the notion of magnitude in general. But
in forming this notion we abstract from all the properties of particular
magnitudes except their being magnitudes; and the notion of algebraical
magnitude is thus the notion of magnitude in general. The properties of
algebraical magnitude are thus the properties of magnitude in general;
and the laws of algebra are the general truths which hold of magnitude
in general. They are necessary truths, in the sense that they are
involved in the notion of magnitude in general; they are necessary in
the nature of magnitude in general. And they are known to us
\textit{a priori}, in the sense that we do not learn them from experience,
but that they are involved in the notion of magnitude in general which
we form independently of experience.

I would speak of the foundation of mathematics in regard to analysis.
We have the notion of a variable magnitude; and we form the notion of a
variable magnitude without reference to any particular kind of magnitude.
The notion of a variable magnitude is thus the notion of a magnitude
which varies. But in forming this notion we abstract from all the
properties of particular variable magnitudes except their being variable
magnitudes; and the notion of a variable magnitude is thus the notion of
a magnitude which varies in general. The properties of a variable
magnitude are thus the properties of a magnitude which varies in general;
and the principles of analysis are the general truths which hold of a
magnitude which varies in general. They are necessary truths, in the
sense that they are involved in the notion of a magnitude which varies
in general; they are necessary in the nature of a magnitude which varies
in general. And they are known to us \textit{a priori}, in the sense
that we do not learn them from experience, but that they are involved in
the notion of a magnitude which varies in general which we form
independently of experience.

I have been speaking of the foundation of mathematics in a somewhat
abstract manner. I would now speak of it in a more concrete and
practical manner. And I would begin with geometry. The foundation of
geometry is the notion of space; and the notion of space is the notion
of a solid body of infinite extent. The properties of space are the
properties of extension; and the axioms of geometry are the general
truths which hold of extension. They are necessary truths, in the sense
that they are involved in the notion of extension; and they are known to
us \textit{a priori}, in the sense that we do not learn them from
experience.

But the notion of space is not a simple notion; it is a complex notion,
involving many simpler notions. And the axioms of geometry are not all
the necessary truths which hold of extension; they are only the most
general and fundamental of such truths. The simpler notions involved in
the notion of space are the notions of point, line, surface, solid,
angle, distance, direction, and the like. And the axioms of geometry
are the necessary truths which hold of these notions. They are
necessary truths, in the sense that they are involved in the notions of
point, line, surface, solid, angle, distance, direction, and the like;
and they are known to us \textit{a priori}, in the sense that we do not
learn them from experience.

I would now speak of the foundation of arithmetic. The foundation of
arithmetic is the notion of number; and the notion of number is the
notion of a plurality of units. The properties of number are the
properties of a plurality of units; and the laws of arithmetic are the
general truths which hold of a plurality of units. They are necessary
truths, in the sense that they are involved in the notion of a plurality
of units; and they are known to us \textit{a priori}, in the sense that
we do not learn them from experience.

But the notion of number is not a simple notion; it is a complex notion,
involving many simpler notions. And the laws of arithmetic are not all
the necessary truths which hold of a plurality of units; they are only
the most general and fundamental of such truths. The simpler notions
involved in the notion of number are the notions of one, two, three,
and the like; and the operations of addition, subtraction,
multiplication, division, and the like. And the laws of arithmetic are
the necessary truths which hold of these notions. They are necessary
truths, in the sense that they are involved in the notions of one, two,
three, and the like, and in the operations of addition, subtraction,
multiplication, division, and the like; and they are known to us
\textit{a priori}, in the sense that we do not learn them from
experience.


I would now speak of the foundation of algebra. The foundation of
algebra is the notion of algebraical magnitude; and the notion of
algebraical magnitude is the notion of magnitude in general. The
properties of algebraical magnitude are the properties of magnitude in
general; and the laws of algebra are the general truths which hold of
magnitude in general. They are necessary truths, in the sense that they
are involved in the notion of magnitude in general; and they are known
to us \textit{a priori}, in the sense that we do not learn them from
experience.

But the notion of algebraical magnitude is not a simple notion; it is a
complex notion, involving many simpler notions. And the laws of algebra
are not all the necessary truths which hold of magnitude in general;
they are only the most general and fundamental of such truths. The
simpler notions involved in the notion of algebraical magnitude are the
notions of equality, addition, subtraction, multiplication, division,
and the like. And the laws of algebra are the necessary truths which
hold of these notions. They are necessary truths, in the sense that
they are involved in the notions of equality, addition, subtraction,
multiplication, division, and the like; and they are known to us
\textit{a priori}, in the sense that we do not learn them from
experience.

I would now speak of the foundation of analysis. The foundation of
analysis is the notion of a variable magnitude; and the notion of a
variable magnitude is the notion of a magnitude which varies in general.
The properties of a variable magnitude are the properties of a magnitude
which varies in general; and the principles of analysis are the general
truths which hold of a magnitude which varies in general. They are
necessary truths, in the sense that they are involved in the notion of a
magnitude which varies in general; and they are known to us
\textit{a priori}, in the sense that we do not learn them from
experience.

But the notion of a variable magnitude is not a simple notion; it is a
complex notion, involving many simpler notions. And the principles of
analysis are not all the necessary truths which hold of a magnitude
which varies in general; they are only the most general and fundamental
of such truths. The simpler notions involved in the notion of a
variable magnitude are the notions of limit, continuity, infinity, and
the like. And the principles of analysis are the necessary truths which
hold of these notions. They are necessary truths, in the sense that
they are involved in the notions of limit, continuity, infinity, and the
like; and they are known to us \textit{a priori}, in the sense that we
do not learn them from experience.

I have been speaking of the foundation of mathematics in a somewhat
abstract manner. I would now speak of it in a more concrete and
practical manner. And I would begin with the geometry of the ancients.
The geometry of the ancients was founded on the notion of space; and
the notion of space was the notion of a solid body of infinite extent.
The properties of space were the properties of extension; and the axioms
of geometry were the general truths which held of extension. They were
necessary truths, in the sense that they were involved in the notion of
extension; and they were known to the ancients \textit{a priori}, in the
sense that they did not learn them from experience.

The ancients did not distinguish between the notion of space and the
notion of a solid body of infinite extent. They did not distinguish
between the properties of space and the properties of extension. They
did not distinguish between the axioms of geometry and the general
truths which held of extension. They did not distinguish between the
necessary truths which were involved in the notion of extension and the
necessary truths which were involved in the notion of space. They did
not distinguish between the \textit{a priori} knowledge of the axioms of

The geometry of the ancients was thus founded on the notion of space;
and the notion of space was the notion of a solid body of infinite
extent. The properties of space were the properties of extension; and
the axioms of geometry were the general truths which held of extension.
They were necessary truths, in the sense that they were involved in the

The geometry of the ancients was thus a geometry of space; and it was
founded on the notion of space. But the notion of space is not the

The geometry of the ancients was thus a geometry of space; and it was

also the geometry of lines; and there is also the geometry of points.
And the geometry of surfaces is founded on the notion of a surface;
and the geometry of lines is founded on the notion of a line; and the
geometry of points is founded on the notion of a point.

The geometry of the ancients was thus a geometry of space; and it was

only geometry. There is also the geometry of surfaces; and there is
also the geometry of lines; and there is also the geometry of points.
And the geometry of surfaces is founded on the notion of a surface;
and the geometry of lines is founded on the notion of a line; and the
geometry of points is founded on the notion of a point. And each of
these geometries has its own axioms; and each of these geometries has
its own theorems; and each of these geometries has its own methods.

The geometry of the ancients was thus a geometry of space; and it was

\section*{785. Curve.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{785. Curve.}

\begin{flushright}
\small[From the Encyclop\ae{}dia Britannica, Ninth Edition, vol.~vi.\ (1877), pp.~716---728.]
\end{flushright}

This subject is treated here from an historical point of view, for the
purpose of showing how the different leading ideas in the theory were
successively arrived at and developed.

A curve is a line, or continuous singly infinite system of points. We
consider in the first instance, and chiefly, a plane curve described
according to a law. Such a curve may be regarded geometrically as
actually described, or kinematically as in course of description by the
motion of a point; in the former point of view, it is the locus of all
the points which satisfy a given condition; in the latter, it is the
locus of a point moving subject to a given condition. Thus the most
simple and earliest known curve, the circle, is the locus of all the
points at a given distance from a fixed centre, or else the locus of a
point moving so as to be always at a given distance from a fixed
centre. (The straight line and the point are not for the moment
regarded as curves.)

Next to the circle we have the conic sections, the invention of them
attributed to Plato (who lived 430 to 347 B.C.); the original
definition of them as the sections of a cone was by the Greek geometers
who studied them soon replaced by a proper definition in piano like
that for the circle, viz.\ a conic section (or as we now say a
``conic'') is the locus of a point such that its distance from a given
point, the focus, is in a given ratio to its distance from a given
line, the directrix. The Greek geometers studied the conics in great
detail, and their work was continued by the Arabian mathematicians and
by the European geometers of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

Besides the conics, the Greek geometers studied various other curves,
such as the cissoid of Diocles, the conchoid of Nicomedes, the
quadratrix of Dinostratus, the spiral of Archimedes, and the
lemniscate of Bernoulli. These curves were all defined by geometrical
properties, and their equations were not known until the invention of
the method of coordinates by Descartes.

The principle of classification first presented itself in the
G\'{e}om\'{e}trie of Descartes (1637). The idea was to represent any
curve whatever by means of a relation between the coordinates $(x, y)$
of a point of the curve, or say to represent the curve by means of its
equation. Descartes takes two lines $xx'$, $yy'$, called axes of
coordinates, intersecting at a point called the origin (the axes are
usually at right angles to each other, and for the present they are
considered as being so); and he determines the position of a point $P$
by means of its distances $OM$ (or $NP$) $= x$, and $MP$ (or $ON$) $= y$,
from these two axes respectively; where $x$ is regarded as positive or
negative according as it is in the direction $Ox$ or the opposite
direction $Ox'$, and similarly $y$ is regarded as positive or negative
according as it is in the direction $Oy$ or the opposite direction
$Oy'$.

Observe that the distinctive feature is in the exclusive use of such
determination of a curve by means of its equation. The Greek geometers
were perfectly familiar with the property of an ellipse which in the
Cartesian notation is $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$, the
equation of the curve; but it was as one of a number of properties, and
in no wise selected out of the others for the characteristic property of
the curve.

We obtain from the equation the notion of an algebraical or geometrical
as opposed to a transcendental curve. The notion is that an
algebraical curve is one which is represented by an algebraical
equation, or say an equation of the form $f(x, y) = 0$, where $f(x, y)$
is a rational and integral function of $x$, $y$; and a transcendental
curve is one which is not so represented. The distinction is
important, for it will appear that the properties of algebraical curves
are in many respects different from those of transcendental curves.

We obtain from the equation the notion of the order of a curve. An
algebraical curve is said to be of the order $m$ when the equation is
of the degree $m$ in the coordinates $(x, y)$: thus a line is a curve
of the first order; a conic is a curve of the second order; and so on.
A curve of the order $m$ has for its equation $(x, y, 1)^m = 0$; and
when the coefficients of the function are arbitrary, the curve is said
to be the general curve of the order $m$. The number of coefficients
is $\frac{1}{2}(m+1)(m+2)$; but there is no loss of generality if the
coefficients be divided by any one of them which is not zero; and thus
the number of arbitrary constants is $\frac{1}{2}(m+1)(m+2) - 1 =
\frac{1}{2}m(m+3)$.

A curve of the order $m$ is met by any line whatever in precisely $m$
points. This is the fundamental theorem of the theory of curves. It
was first proved by B\'{e}zout in 1779, but the proof was not
satisfactory. The first satisfactory proof was given by Poncelet in
1822. The theorem is easily proved by means of the equation of the
curve. For if the equation be $f(x, y) = 0$, and we substitute in
this equation for $y$ the value $mx + n$, we obtain an equation of the
degree $m$ in $x$, which has $m$ roots; and thus the line meets the
curve in $m$ points.

The theorem is also true for curves of a special form; for instance, a
curve of the order $m$ which breaks up into a curve of the order $p$
and a curve of the order $q$, where $p+q=m$, is met by any line in $m$
points, viz.\ in $p$ points on the first curve, and $q$ points on the
second curve. And the theorem is true for curves which have multiple
points; for instance, a curve of the order $m$ which has a double point
is met by any line in $m$ points, provided that the line does not pass
through the double point.

The theorem is also true for curves which have points at infinity. A
curve of the order $m$ is met by the line at infinity in $m$ points;
and these points are the points at infinity on the curve. For
instance, a conic is met by the line at infinity in two points; and
these points are the circular points at infinity, if the conic be a
circle.

We have thus the notion of the class of a curve. The class of a curve
is the number of tangents which can be drawn to the curve from an
arbitrary point. It was introduced by Poncelet, and is one of the
most important notions in the theory of curves. The class of a curve
is in general different from the order; for instance, the class of a
conic is 2, and the order of a conic is 2; but the class of a cubic is
6, and the order of a cubic is 3.

The relation between the order and class of a curve is given by
Pl\"{u}cker's equations. These equations were discovered by Pl\"{u}cker
in 1834, and they are the foundation of the modern theory of curves.
They connect the order $m$, the class $n$, and the singularities of the
curve, viz.\ the number of nodes $\delta$, the number of cusps $\kappa$,
the number of double tangents $\tau$, and the number of points of
inflexion $\iota$. The equations are:
\begin{align*}
n &= m(m-1) - 2\delta - 3\kappa,\\
m &= n(n-1) - 2\tau - 3\iota.
\end{align*}

From these equations we can deduce a great number of properties of
curves. For instance, we can deduce that the number of points of
inflexion on a curve of the order $m$ without singularities is
$3m(m-2)$. And we can deduce that the number of double tangents to a
curve of the order $m$ without singularities is
$\frac{1}{2}m(m-2)(m^2-9)$.

We have also the notion of the deficiency of a curve. The deficiency
is a number which measures the difference between the curve and a curve
of the same order without singularities. It was introduced by Clebsch
in 1863, and is one of the most important notions in the theory of
curves. The deficiency $D$ of a curve of the order $m$ with $\delta$
nodes and $\kappa$ cusps is given by the formula:
\[
D = \frac{1}{2}(m-1)(m-2) - \delta - \kappa.
\]

The deficiency is always a positive number or zero. When the
deficiency is zero, the curve is said to be a rational curve; and when
the deficiency is greater than zero, the curve is said to be an
irrational curve. The rational curves are the simplest curves; and
they include the conics, the cubics with a double point, and the
quartics with three double points.


The notion of the envelope of a variable curve is very probably older,
but it is at any rate to be found in Lagrange's Th\'{e}orie des fonctions
analytiques (1798); it is there remarked that the equation obtained by
the elimination of the parameter $a$ from an equation $f(x, y, a) = 0$
and the derived equation in respect to $a$ is a curve, the envelope of
the series of curves represented by the variable equation $f(x,y,a)=0$.
The notion of the envelope is a very important one in the theory of
curves; and it has been much studied by mathematicians.

We have in like manner, as derivatives of a given curve, the evolute,
which is at once the locus of the centre of curvature and the envelope
of the normal. It may be added that the given curve is one of a
series of curves, each cutting the several normals at right angles. Any
one of these is a ``parallel'' of the given curve; and it can be
obtained as the envelope of a circle of constant radius having its
centre on the given curve.

We have in like manner, as derivatives of a given curve, the caustic,
catacaustic, or diacaustic, as the case may be, and the secondary
caustic, or curve cutting at right angles the reflected or refracted
rays.

We have in much that precedes disregarded, or at least been indifferent
to, reality; it is only thus that the conception of a curve of the
$m$th order, as one which is met by every right line in $m$ points, is
arrived at; and the curve itself, and the line which cuts it, although
both are tacitly assumed to be real, may perfectly well be imaginary.
For real figures we have the general theorem that imaginary
intersections, \&c., present themselves in conjugate pairs: hence, in
particular, that a curve of an even order is met by a line in an even
number (which may be $=0$) of points; a curve of an odd order in an
odd number of points, hence in one point at least; it will be seen
further on that the theorem may be generalized in a remarkable manner.

Again, when there is in question only one pair of points or lines,
these, if coincident, must be real; thus, a line meets a cubic curve
in three points, one of them real, the other two real or imaginary;
but if two of the intersections coincide they must be real, and we have
a line cutting a cubic in one real point and touching it in another
real point. It may be remarked that this is a limit separating the
two cases where the intersections are all real, and where they are one
real, two imaginary.

Considering always real curves, we obtain the notion of a branch; any
portion capable of description by the continuous motion of a point is a
branch; and a curve consists of one or more branches. Thus the curve
of the first order or right line consists of one branch; but in curves
of the second order, or conics, the ellipse and the parabola consist
each of one branch, the hyperbola of two branches. A branch is either
re-entrant, or it extends both ways to infinity, and in this case, we
may regard it as consisting of two legs (\textit{crura}, Newton), each
extending one way to infinity, but without any definite separation.

The branch, whether re-entrant or infinite, may have a cusp or cusps,
or it may cut itself or another branch, thus having or giving rise to
crunodes; an acnode is a branch by itself---it may be considered as an
indefinitely small re-entrant branch. A branch may have inflexions and
double tangents, or there may be double tangents which touch two
distinct branches; there are also double tangents with imaginary points
of contact, which are thus lines having no visible connexion with the
curve.

A re-entrant branch not cutting itself may be everywhere convex, and it
is then properly said to be an oval; but the term oval may be used
more generally for any re-entrant branch not cutting itself; and we may
thus speak of a once indented, twice indented oval, \&c., or even of a
cuspidate oval. Other descriptive names for ovals and re-entrant
branches cutting themselves may be used when required; thus, in the
last-mentioned case a simple form is that of a figure of eight; such a
form may break up into two ovals, or into a doubly indented oval or
hour-glass. A form which presents itself is when two ovals, one inside
the other, unite, so as to give rise to a crunode---in default of a
better name this may be called a 	extit{binode}.
