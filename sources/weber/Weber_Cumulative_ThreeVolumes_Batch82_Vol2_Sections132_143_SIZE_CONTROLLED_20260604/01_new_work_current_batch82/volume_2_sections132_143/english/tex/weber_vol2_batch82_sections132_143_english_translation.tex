\section*{Sixteenth Section. Numbers and functionals of an algebraic field.}

\section*{\S\,132. Definition of algebraic numbers.}

An algebraic equation
\begin{equation}
F(x)=x^n+a_1x^{n-1}+\cdots +a_{n-1}x+a_n=0
\tag{1}
\end{equation}
whose coefficients are rational numbers will, for brevity, be called
a rational equation. It has, as was shown earlier, at least one root
and at most \(n\) distinct roots. The numerical approximation of such
roots by rational numbers, decimals, continued fractions, and, where
necessary, by adjoining the imaginary unit \(\ii=\sqrt{-1}\), belongs
to the analytic side already treated. Here the concern is not their
numerical values but the arithmetical laws which follow from the
defining equations.

A number \(\theta\) satisfying a rational equation
\[
F(\theta)=0
\]
is called an algebraic number. Rational equations thus provide
algebraic numbers in arbitrary abundance. The question whether there
are also non-algebraic numbers is postponed.

\section*{\S\,133. Integral algebraic numbers.}

An algebraic number \(\theta\) is called an integral algebraic number
if it satisfies an equation
\begin{equation}
\theta^m+A_1\theta^{m-1}+A_2\theta^{m-2}+\cdots +A_m=0
\tag{1}
\end{equation}
whose coefficients \(A_1,\ldots,A_m\) are rational integers. It is
enough that among the rational equations satisfied by \(\theta\) there
is one of this kind.

The ordinary rational integers occur as special cases. Positive
rational integers are also called natural numbers. The word
``integer'', without qualification, will mean an integral algebraic
number, rational or irrational.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item An integral algebraic number which is rational is necessarily a
rational integer. If \(\theta=P/Q\) with \(P,Q\) coprime rational
integers, then equation (1) forces every prime divisor of \(Q\) to
divide \(P\). Hence \(Q=1\).

\item The sum, difference, and product of two integers are again
integers. If \(\alpha,\beta\) satisfy
\[
\alpha^\mu+a_1\alpha^{\mu-1}+\cdots+a_\mu=0,\qquad
\beta^\nu+b_1\beta^{\nu-1}+\cdots+b_\nu=0,
\]
and if
\[
\omega=\alpha+\beta,\qquad \omega=\alpha-\beta,\qquad
\omega=\alpha\beta,
\]
form the \(\mu\nu\) quantities
\[
\alpha^r\beta^s,\qquad r=0,1,\ldots,\mu-1,\quad
s=0,1,\ldots,\nu-1,
\]
and denote them by \(\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_m\). The products
\(\omega\omega_i\) can be expressed linearly:
\begin{equation}
\omega\omega_r=c_{r,1}\omega_1+c_{r,2}\omega_2+\cdots+c_{r,m}\omega_m,
\qquad r=1,\ldots,m,
\tag{2}
\end{equation}
with rational integral coefficients. Eliminating
\(\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_m\) gives
\begin{equation}
\det(c_{r,s}-\omega\delta_{r,s})=0,
\tag{3}
\end{equation}
an equation for \(\omega\) with rational integral coefficients.

\item If \(f(x)\) is irreducible over the rational field and one root
of \(f(x)=0\) is integral, then all roots of \(f(x)=0\) are integral.
Consequently the equation of least degree
\begin{equation}
f(x)=x^n+a_1x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_n
\tag{4}
\end{equation}
for an integral algebraic number has rational integral coefficients.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{\S\,134. Algebraic fields.}

Let \(f(x)\) be the irreducible equation of least degree satisfied by
\(\theta\), and let
\[
\theta,\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_{n-1}
\]
be its roots. These numbers are conjugate. The totality of rational
functions of \(\theta\),
\[
\omega=\varphi(\theta),
\]
with rational coefficients is a field. If \(R\) denotes the field of
rational numbers, this field \(R(\theta)\) is called an algebraic
number field of degree \(n\).

Every number of such a field has, in each conjugate field, a
corresponding conjugate number. Symmetric functions of the conjugates
are rational. Among them the most important are the sum and the
product: the first is the trace and the second the norm,
\[
S(\omega),\qquad N(\omega).
\]
Equal conjugates are counted with their multiplicities.

Since the four arithmetical operations can be carried out in an
algebraic number field as in the rational field, the question arises
how far the laws of arithmetic in the rational integers continue to
hold there. The first issue is the decomposition into prime factors.
This decomposition usually fails if one uses only the numbers of the
field. The material of calculation must therefore be enlarged. Kummer
did this for cyclotomic numbers by ideal prime factors, Dedekind by
ideals, and Kronecker by a different general method. The following
paragraphs connect these theories and develop, from Kronecker's
starting point, what is needed for the later algebraic applications.

\section*{\S\,135. Integral functions in an algebraic field.}

Let \(\Omega\) be an algebraic number field. A whole expression
\begin{equation}
\varphi(x,y,z,\ldots)=\sum \alpha x^r y^s z^t\cdots
\tag{1}
\end{equation}
with non-negative integral exponents and coefficients \(\alpha\) in
\(\Omega\) is called an integral function in \(\Omega\). It is
understood that equal monomials have been collected. Two such
functions are equal only when corresponding coefficients are equal;
one of them is zero only when all its coefficients are zero.

The degree of a term \(x^r y^s z^t\cdots\) is
\(r+s+t+\cdots\), and the degree of a function is the highest degree
of a term with non-zero coefficient. The degree of a product is the
sum of the degrees of the factors.

The following facts from the first volume are used.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item Integral functions in \(\Omega\) are reducible or irreducible.
Every reducible integral function is a finite product of irreducible
integral functions, and the irreducible factors are determined by the
function up to constant factors from \(\Omega\).

\item The product of two primitive integral functions in \(R\) is
primitive. If
\[
\Phi(x,y,z,\ldots)=\sum a x^r y^s z^t\cdots
\tag{2}
\]
has rational integral coefficients with greatest common divisor \(m\),
write
\[
a_i=m e_i,\qquad
E(x,y,z,\ldots)=\sum e_i x^r y^s z^t\cdots.
\tag{3}
\]
Then
\[
\Phi=mE
\tag{4}
\]
and \(E\) is primitive.

\item The divisor-content of a product of integral functions is the
product of the divisor-contents of its factors.
\end{enumerate}

Replacing \(\theta\) successively by its conjugates gives the
conjugate functions
\begin{equation}
\varphi(\theta,x,y,\ldots),\quad
\varphi(\theta_1,x,y,\ldots),\quad
\varphi(\theta_2,x,y,\ldots),\ldots .
\tag{5}
\end{equation}
Their product
\begin{equation}
N(\varphi)=\varphi\varphi_1\varphi_2\cdots
\tag{6}
\end{equation}
is an integral function in \(R\), and
\begin{equation}
N(\varphi\psi)=N(\varphi)N(\psi).
\tag{7}
\end{equation}

\section*{\S\,136. Functionals of an algebraic field \(\Omega\).}

The variables used here are symbols of calculation rather than
independent number-variables. We introduce integral and fractional
functions in any number of variables with coefficients in
\(\Omega\). Every such function can be written as
\[
\omega=\frac{\varphi}{\psi},\qquad \psi\neq 0,
\]
where \(\varphi,\psi\) are integral functions. Equality of two such
fractions means
\[
\varphi\psi_1=\varphi_1\psi.
\]
A fraction is irreducible when numerator and denominator have no
common divisor. Such a function \(\omega\) is called a functional of
the field \(\Omega\). The functionals form a field, the functional
field of \(\Omega\).

A functional with rational coefficients is rational. Every rational
functional can be written as
\[
A=a\frac{E_1}{E_2},
\]
where \(a\) is a positive rational number and \(E_1,E_2\) are
primitive integral functions in \(R\). Thus:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item Every rational functional can be made an integral function in
\(R\) by multiplication by a primitive function; several rational
functionals can be written with a common primitive denominator.

\item The positive rational number \(a\) is the absolute value of
\(A\). For ordinary numbers this is the usual absolute value; the
factor \(E_1/E_2\) generalizes the sign.

\item The absolute value of a product of rational functionals is the
product of their absolute values.
\end{enumerate}

For a functional \(\omega\) in \(\Omega\), \(N(\omega)\) is a rational
functional. The absolute value of \(N(\omega)\) is the absolute norm:
\begin{equation}
N(\omega)=N_a(\omega)E(\omega).
\tag{3}
\end{equation}
Hence
\begin{equation}
N_a(\alpha\beta)=N_a(\alpha)N_a(\beta).
\tag{4}
\end{equation}

Replacing all coefficients by the corresponding numbers in a
conjugate field gives a conjugate functional, and every equation
between functionals remains true after this simultaneous replacement.
If \(t\) is a new variable, then
\begin{equation}
N(t-\omega)=t^n+A_1t^{n-1}+A_2t^{n-2}+\cdots+A_n
\tag{5}
\end{equation}
has rational functionals as coefficients and vanishes for
\(t=\omega\). Among all such functions vanishing at \(\omega\), with
leading coefficient \(1\), there is a unique one \(F(t)\) of least
degree. It is irreducible over \(R\), every other such function is
divisible by it, and
\[
N(t-\omega)=F(t)^e.
\]
Thus every functional is the root of a unique irreducible equation
over \(R\).

\section*{\S\,137. Integral functionals.}

A rational functional is integral when its absolute value is an
integer. In general, a functional \(\omega\) is integral when it
satisfies
\begin{equation}
\omega^m+A_1\omega^{m-1}+A_2\omega^{m-2}+\cdots+A_m=0
\tag{1}
\end{equation}
with integral rational functionals as coefficients. If such a
functional is rational, this definition agrees with the preceding
one.

Let
\[
\Phi(t)=t^m+A_1t^{m-1}+\cdots+A_m
\]
have rational functionals as coefficients. Multiplication by a common
denominator produces a primitive integral function
\[
aE(x,y,\ldots)\Phi(t)=P(t,x,y,\ldots).
\tag{2}
\]
The primitive-factor theorem then gives: if an integral functional
\(\omega\) is a root of \(\Phi\), every rational factor of \(\Phi\)
has integral coefficients. In particular the irreducible equation of
\(\omega\) has integral coefficients.

Every functional becomes integral after multiplication by a rational
integer, and therefore every functional is a quotient of two integral
functionals.

A useful criterion is this. If there are quantities
\(\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_m\), not all zero, such that
\begin{equation}
\omega\omega_i=A_{1,i}\omega_1+A_{2,i}\omega_2+\cdots+A_{m,i}\omega_m,
\qquad i=1,\ldots,m,
\tag{5}
\end{equation}
with integral rational functionals \(A_{r,i}\), then \(\omega\) is
integral. Indeed,
\[
\det(A_{r,i}-\omega\delta_{r,i})=0
\tag{6}
\]
gives
\[
\omega^m+A_1\omega^{m-1}+\cdots+A_m=0.
\tag{7}
\]
The last coefficient is, up to sign,
\[
(-1)^mA_m=\sum \pm A_{1,i_1}A_{2,i_2}\cdots A_{m,i_m}.
\tag{8}
\]

It follows that an integral function of integral functionals is an
integral functional; in particular sums, differences, and products of
integral functionals are integral. Conjugates of an integral
functional are integral, and the absolute norm of an integral
functional is a natural integer.

\section*{\S\,138. Divisibility. Associated functionals. Units.}

An integral functional \(\alpha\) is divisible by another non-zero
integral functional \(\beta\) if \(\alpha:\beta\) is integral. Thus
\(\alpha=\beta\gamma\). The number \(0\) is divisible by every
integral functional. The usual elementary rules follow:
\[
\alpha,\alpha_1\equiv 0\pmod{\beta}\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\alpha\pm\alpha_1\equiv 0\pmod{\beta},
\]
and transitivity of divisibility is immediate.

Two integral functionals divisible by one another are associated. A
functional associated with \(1\) is a unit. Products and quotients of
units are units. If \(\alpha\) is divisible by \(\beta\), then
\begin{equation}
N_a(\alpha)=N_a(\beta)N_a(\gamma).
\tag{1}
\end{equation}
The absolute norm of a unit is \(1\). Conversely an integral
functional of absolute norm \(1\) is a unit, since
\[
\frac1{\varepsilon}=\frac{\varepsilon'}{N(\varepsilon)}.
\]

Moreover \(N(\alpha)=\alpha\alpha'\); hence the natural integer
\(N_a(\alpha)\) is divisible by \(\alpha\). There are therefore
infinitely many natural integers divisible by a given integral
functional, and if \(a\) is the least positive rational integer
divisible by \(\alpha\), every rational integer divisible by
\(\alpha\) is divisible by \(a\).

\section*{\S\,139. Greatest common divisor.}

Let \(\alpha,\beta,\ldots\) be finitely many non-zero integral
functionals and let \(x,y,\ldots\) be new variables. Then
\begin{equation}
\delta=\alpha x+\beta y+\cdots
\tag{1}
\end{equation}
is integral. If \(D\) is the absolute norm of \(\delta\), then
\begin{equation}
N(\delta)=D\,E(x,y,\ldots)
\tag{2}
\end{equation}
with \(E\) primitive.

Substituting rational integers \(x_0,y_0,\ldots\) gives
\[
\delta_0=\alpha x_0+\beta y_0+\cdots.
\]
The norm \(N(\delta t-\delta_0)\), divided by the norm of \(\delta\),
gives an integral equation for
\[
\eta=\frac{\delta_0}{\delta}.
\]
Thus \(\eta\) is integral and \(\delta_0\) is divisible by
\(\delta\). In particular \(\alpha,\beta,\ldots\) are divisible by
\(\delta\); conversely \(\delta\) is divisible by every common
divisor.

Every functional associated with
\[
\delta=\alpha x+\beta y+\cdots
\]
is therefore called a greatest common divisor of
\(\alpha,\beta,\ldots\). If the greatest common divisor is a unit, the
functionals are relatively prime.

Consequences:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item If integral functionals \(\xi,\eta,\ldots\) exist such that
\[
\alpha\xi+\beta\eta+\cdots=\varepsilon
\]
is a unit, then \(\alpha,\beta,\ldots\) have no common divisor.

\item If \(\alpha\) is relatively prime to \(\beta\) and to \(\gamma\),
then it is relatively prime to \(\beta\gamma\).

\item If \(\alpha\) is relatively prime to \(\beta\) and
\(\alpha\mu\) is divisible by \(\beta\), then \(\mu\) is divisible by
\(\beta\).
\end{enumerate}

\section*{\S\,140. Prime functionals.}

An integral functional \(\pi\) of \(\Omega\), not a unit, is called a
prime functional if its only divisors are units and functionals
associated with \(\pi\). Otherwise it is composite.

For an integral \(\omega\) and a prime \(\pi\), either \(\omega\) is
relatively prime to \(\pi\), or \(\omega\) is divisible by \(\pi\).
Therefore:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item If the product \(\alpha\beta\) of two integral functionals is
divisible by a prime functional \(\pi\), then one of the factors is
divisible by \(\pi\).
\end{enumerate}
By repeated application, if a product of several factors is divisible
by \(\pi\), at least one factor is divisible by \(\pi\).

Let \(p\) be the least natural integer divisible by \(\pi\). Then
\(p\) is a rational prime, and the absolute norm of \(\pi\) is a power
of \(p\). Writing
\[
p=\pi\omega
\tag{1}
\]
and taking norms gives
\[
p^n=N_a(\pi)N_a(\omega).
\tag{2}
\]
Thus
\[
N_a(\pi)=p^f,
\tag{3}
\]
where \(1\leq f\leq n\). The integer \(f\) is the degree of the prime
functional \(\pi\).

\section*{\S\,141. Decomposition of integral and fractional functionals into prime factors.}

Every non-zero integral functional which is not a unit is divisible
by a prime functional. If it is not prime, write
\[
\omega=\omega_1\alpha,
\tag{1}
\]
where neither factor is a unit. Then
\[
N_a(\omega_1)<N_a(\omega).
\tag{2}
\]
Repeating the process, the absolute norms strictly decrease, and the
process must stop. Hence every such functional has a finite prime
factorization:
\[
\omega=\pi_1\pi_2\cdots\pi_\nu.
\tag{5}
\]

The factorization is unique up to associated prime functionals. If
\[
\pi_1\pi_2\cdots\pi_\nu,\qquad
\chi_1\chi_2\cdots\chi_\mu
\tag{6}
\]
are associated products of primes, then \(\chi_1\) divides the first
product, hence is associated with one of the \(\pi_i\); after
cancelling, the argument repeats.

If a prime \(\pi\) occurs several times, the associated factors are
collected into powers. We say that \(\pi\) occurs \(h\) times in
\(\omega\) if \(\omega\) is divisible by \(\pi^h\) but not by
\(\pi^{h+1}\). Thus \(\alpha\) is divisible by \(\beta\) precisely
when every prime factor of \(\beta\) occurs in \(\alpha\) at least as
often.

For several functionals one may write
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_1\alpha=\pi_1^{a_1}\pi_2^{a_2}\cdots,\qquad
\varepsilon_2\beta=\pi_1^{b_1}\pi_2^{b_2}\cdots.
\tag{7}
\end{equation}
The greatest common divisor is obtained by taking the smaller
exponents; the least common multiple by taking the larger exponents.
Fractional functionals are reduced by factorizing numerator and
denominator and cancelling the greatest common divisor.

\section*{\S\,142. Integral functions in the field \(\Omega\).}

The preceding laws allow the proof of Gauss's lemma to be repeated
for functionals. Let
\[
\varphi=a_0t^h+a_1t^{h-1}+\cdots+a_h,\qquad
\psi=b_0t^k+b_1t^{k-1}+\cdots+b_k
\]
be integral functions of one variable with integral functional
coefficients. If their product
\[
\chi=\gamma_0t^{h+k}+\gamma_1t^{h+k-1}+\cdots+\gamma_{h+k}
\]
has all coefficients divisible by a prime functional \(\pi\), then
\(\pi\) divides all coefficients of \(\varphi\) or all coefficients
of \(\psi\).

Consequently, for
\[
\varphi=\varphi_0t^h+\varphi_1t^{h-1}+\cdots+\varphi_h
\tag{1}
\]
the functional \(\varphi\) is integral only if all coefficients
\(\varphi_0,\ldots,\varphi_h\) are integral. If not, introduce a
principal denominator \(\mu\):
\[
\chi=\mu\varphi=\alpha_0t^h+\alpha_1t^{h-1}+\cdots+\alpha_h.
\tag{2}
\]
A prime factor of \(\mu\) which does not divide all \(\alpha_i\)
would contradict the preceding lemma when one uses an integrality
equation
\[
\varphi^m=c_1\varphi^{m-1}+c_2\varphi^{m-2}+\cdots+c_m
\tag{3}
\]
and multiplies through by powers of \(\mu\):
\[
E\chi^m=\mu(a_1E_1\chi^{m-1}+a_2E_2\mu\chi^{m-2}+\cdots+a_mE_m\mu^{m-1}).
\tag{4}
\]

Repeated application gives: an integral rational function of several
variables, whose coefficients are numbers or functionals involving
other variables, is integral only when all its coefficients are
integral.

Every integral functional is associated with an integral function
whose coefficients are integers of \(\Omega\); multiplication by a
rational functional which is a unit transforms it into such a
function.

\section*{\S\,143. Prime factors of the numbers of \(\Omega\).}

The numbers of \(\Omega\) are among the functionals, so their
factorization into primes exists, but the prime factors need not be
numbers; in general they are functionals.

Equations between functionals are ultimately identities between
integral rational functions. They remain true when variables are
renamed. Therefore:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item The prime factors of an integral functional \(\omega\) may be
represented so that they do not contain the variables on which
\(\omega\) depends.
\end{enumerate}
For \(\omega\) divides a number, for instance its absolute norm, and
its prime factors are among the prime factors of that number.

If
\[
E\omega=\varphi(x,y,\ldots)
\tag{1}
\]
with \(E\) a unit and \(\varphi\) an integral function in \(\Omega\),
then by replacing prime factors with variable-free representatives one
gets a number associated with \(\omega\). Hence:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*,resume]
\item An integral function \(\varphi\) with integral number
coefficients is the greatest common divisor of all its coefficients.
\end{enumerate}

Now:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*,resume]
\item Given any integral functional \(\omega\), one may choose a number
\(\alpha\) divisible by \(\omega\) such that \(\alpha:\omega\) is
relatively prime to any prescribed functional \(\rho\).
\end{enumerate}
First, for each prime functional \(\pi\) there is a number divisible
by \(\omega\) but not by \(\omega\pi\). For finitely many distinct
prime functionals \(\pi_1,\pi_2,\ldots\) one chooses such numbers and
forms a sum divisible by \(\omega\) but by none of the
\(\omega\pi_i\). Taking the prime functionals occurring in \(\rho\)
gives the assertion.

It follows that:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*,resume]
\item Every integral functional \(\omega\) of \(\Omega\) is the
greatest common divisor of two integral numbers and is associated with
a binary linear form
\[
\alpha x+\beta y
\]
with integral numbers \(\alpha,\beta\).
\end{enumerate}

Thus the main result of the section is that the integral algebraic
numbers of a fixed field factor uniquely when the field is enlarged to
its functional field by adjoining variables. The construction uses
only the presence of integers in the ground field and their unique
prime factorization. Hence the same reasoning applies to any ground
field with these two properties, for example to the field of rational
functions of one variable. This is the starting point for the theory
of algebraic functions of one variable.
