\section*{\S\,39. Algorithm for the calculation of the roots.}

The proof given in the preceding paragraph for the existence of a root of an algebraic equation, although it leaves nothing to be desired in conclusiveness, nevertheless still has the defect that it does not make clear the steps by which a root can be calculated by a convergent computational procedure. We therefore add the following considerations, intended to remedy this defect, even if only theoretically. They also give a second proof of the fundamental theorem, due in its essentials to Lipschitz (\emph{Lehrbuch der Analysis}, vol. I, \S\,61 ff.; I owe a simplification to a communication of Dedekind).

If the two integral rational functions $f(x)$ and $f'(x)$ have a common divisor, then this can be found and removed by rational operations according to \S\,6. We may therefore assume that $f(x)$ and $f'(x)$ have no common divisor, that is, that they do not vanish simultaneously for any value of $x$.

We now assume that the calculation of the roots has already succeeded for a function of degree $n-1$, and accordingly suppose $f'(x)$ resolved into linear factors. Thus, if
\begin{equation}
 f(x)=x^n+a_1x^{n-1}+a_2x^{n-2}+\cdots,
\tag{1}
\end{equation}
then
\begin{equation}
 f'(x)=nx^{n-1}+(n-1)a_1x^{n-2}+\cdots
      =n(x-\beta_1)(x-\beta_2)\cdots(x-\beta_{n-1}),
\tag{2}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_1,\beta_2,\ldots,\beta_{n-1}$ are to be regarded as known numbers, some of which may also be identical. By our assumption, the absolute values
\begin{equation}
 |f(\beta_1)|,\\ |f(\beta_2)|,\ldots, |f(\beta_{n-1})|,
\tag{3}
\end{equation}
which we denote by
\begin{equation}
 b_1,b_2,\ldots,b_{n-1},
\tag{4}
\end{equation}
are all different from zero; let the notation be so chosen that $b_1$ is the smallest among them, or at least exceeds none of the others. By theorem 5 of \S\,38 a value $\alpha$ of $x$ can then be determined so that the absolute value $a$ of $f(\alpha)$ is smaller than $b_1$, hence
\begin{equation}
 a<b_1,b_2,\ldots,b_{n-1}.
\tag{5}
\end{equation}

We now bound a domain $G$ for the variable $x$ in such a way that outside this domain the absolute value of $f(x)$ is always greater than $a$, so that $G$ contains all points $x$ for which $|f(x)|<a$ (but also other points). By \S\,31, IV this is possible in the following way. About the origin as centre draw a circle $(R)$ of sufficiently large radius $R$ containing the points $\alpha,\beta_1,\beta_2,
\ldots,\beta_{n-1}$, and cut out of this circle the points $\beta_1,\beta_2,
\ldots,\beta_{n-1}$, at which $|f(x)|$ is certainly greater than $a$, by circular envelopes of sufficiently small but non-zero radii $\rho_1,\rho_2,
\ldots,\rho_{n-1}$, so that within all these circles $(\rho_1),(\rho_2),\ldots,(\rho_{n-1})$ the absolute value $|f(x)|$ remains greater than $a$ (\S\,31, V). None of these circles then encloses the point $\alpha$, for at that point $|f(x)|=a$. The connected piece of surface bounded by the circles $(R),(\rho_1),\ldots,(\rho_{n-1})$ is the domain $G$.

We now determine a sequence of numbers
\[
 \alpha,\alpha',\alpha'',\alpha''',\ldots
\]
in such a way that the absolute values of $f(\alpha),f(\alpha'),f(\alpha''),\ldots$, which we denote by
\[
 a,a',a'',a''',\ldots,
\]
always decrease. The points so determined all remain in the interior of the domain $G$, since for them $|f(x)|<a$.

For this we use a procedure quite similar to that used for the proof of theorem 5 in \S\,38, but simplified by the fact that $f'(\alpha)$ is already different from zero. If $\delta$ denotes a positive proper fraction, still to be determined more precisely, we put in the expansion
\begin{equation}
 f(\alpha+h)=f(\alpha)+h f'(\alpha)+\frac{h^2}{1\cdot2}f''(\alpha)+\cdots
\tag{6}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 h=-\delta\frac{f(\alpha)}{f'(\alpha)},
\tag{7}
\end{equation}
and obtain
\begin{align}
 f(\alpha+h)={}&(1-\delta)f(\alpha)
 +\frac{\delta^2 f(\alpha)^2 f''(\alpha)}{1\cdot2\,f'(\alpha)^2}
 -\frac{\delta^3 f(\alpha)^3 f'''(\alpha)}{1\cdot2\cdot3\,f'(\alpha)^3}+\cdots .
\tag{8}
\end{align}

Now by (2)
\[
 f'(\alpha)=n(\alpha-\beta_1)(\alpha-\beta_2)\cdots(\alpha-\beta_{n-1});
\]
therefore, if the absolute values of $\alpha-\beta_1,\alpha-\beta_2,
\ldots,\alpha-\beta_{n-1}$ are denoted by $r_1,r_2,
\ldots,r_{n-1}$,
\[
 |f'(\alpha)|=nr_1r_2\cdots r_{n-1}.
\]
Since $\alpha$ lies outside the circle of radius $\rho_i$ drawn about $\beta_i$, we have $r_1>\rho_1,r_2>\rho_2,\ldots$, and consequently
\[
 |f'(\alpha)|>n\rho_1\rho_2\cdots\rho_{n-1}.
\]
Thus $|f'(\alpha)|$ is greater than a positive number $k$ independent of the position of $\alpha$ in $G$. It follows that one can choose a sufficiently large positive number $Q$, likewise independent of the position of $\alpha$ and of the proper fraction $\delta$, so that
\begin{equation}
 \left|\frac{f(\alpha)^2f''(\alpha)}{1\cdot2\,f'(\alpha)^2}
 -\delta\frac{f(\alpha)^3f'''(\alpha)}{1\cdot2\cdot3\,f'(\alpha)^3}+\cdots\right|<Q .
\tag{9}
\end{equation}

One obtains $Q$, for example, by replacing in the denominators on the left of (9) $f'(\alpha)$ by $k$, replacing $\delta$ by $1$, replacing all the other factors by their absolute values, replacing finally the absolute value of $\alpha$ by the greater value $R$, and then making the number so obtained arbitrarily larger. From (8) one obtains
\begin{equation}
 |f(\alpha+h)|<(1-\delta)|f(\alpha)|+\delta^2Q.
\tag{10}
\end{equation}
Assume now $Q$ so chosen that, for every $\alpha$ in $G$,
\[
 2Q>|f(\alpha)|=a,
\]
which is plainly compatible with the above determination. We may then put
\begin{equation}
 \delta=\frac{a}{2Q},\qquad
 h=-\frac{a f(\alpha)}{2Q f'(\alpha)},
\tag{11}
\end{equation}
and, denoting $\alpha+h$ by $\alpha'$, and denoting $|f(\alpha')|$ by $a'$, obtain from (10)
\begin{equation}
 a'<a\left(1-\frac{a}{4Q}\right).
\tag{12}
\end{equation}

By the same procedure we derive from $\alpha',a'$ a second system $\alpha'',a''$, then from $\alpha'',a''$ a third system $\alpha''',a'''$, and so on; for the sequence of decreasing positive numbers $a,a',a'',\ldots$ we obtain the inequalities
\begin{equation}
 a'<a\left(1-\frac{a}{4Q}\right),\quad
 a''<a'\left(1-\frac{a'}{4Q}\right),\quad
 a'''<a''\left(1-\frac{a''}{4Q}\right),\ldots .
\tag{13}
\end{equation}
We prove that this sequence approaches the limit zero. If this were not the case, a positive number $\omega$ could be given such that all $a^{(\nu)}$ remain above $\omega$; the differences
\[
 \frac{a^{(\nu)}}{4Q}
\]
would then all be greater than the positive proper fraction $\omega/(4Q)$, and from (13) it would follow that
\[
 a'<a\theta,
 \quad a''<a'\theta,
 \quad a'''<a''\theta,
 \quad\ldots,
\]
where $0<\theta<1$. By multiplication one gets
\begin{equation}
 a^{(\nu)}<a\theta^\nu.
\tag{14}
\end{equation}
This is a contradiction, since $\theta^\nu$ becomes infinitely small as $\nu$ increases without bound. The result so obtained is expressed by
\begin{equation}
 \lim a^{(\nu)}=0.
\tag{15}
\end{equation}

If the inequalities (13) are written in the form
\[
 a-a'>\frac{a^2}{4Q},\qquad
 a'-a''>\frac{a'^2}{4Q},\qquad\ldots,
\]
and added from the $(\mu+1)$-st to the $(\nu+1)$-st, one obtains
\[
 (a^{(\mu)})^2+(a^{(\mu+1)})^2+\cdots+(a^{(\nu)})^2
 <4Q\bigl(a^{(\mu)}-a^{(\nu+1)}\bigr),
\]
and therefore, when $\mu$ and $\nu$ both tend to infinity,
\begin{equation}
 \lim\bigl((a^{(\mu)})^2+(a^{(\mu+1)})^2+\cdots+(a^{(\nu)})^2\bigr)=0.
\tag{16}
\end{equation}

For the sequence of numbers $\alpha,\alpha',\alpha'',\ldots$ the law of formation resulting from (11) is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \alpha'&=\alpha-\frac{a f(\alpha)}{2Q f'(\alpha)},\\
 \alpha''&=\alpha'-\frac{a' f(\alpha')}{2Q f'(\alpha')},\\
 &\hspace{1.5em}\vdots\\
 \alpha^{(\nu)}&=\alpha^{(\nu-1)}-
 \frac{a^{(\nu-1)} f(\alpha^{(\nu-1)})}{2Q f'(\alpha^{(\nu-1)})}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{17}
\end{equation}
If an arbitrary number of these equations are added, from the $(\mu+1)$-st to the $(\nu+1)$-st, then
\begin{equation}
 \alpha^{(\mu)}-\alpha^{(\nu+1)}=
 \sum_{r=\mu}^{\nu}\frac{a^{(r)}f(\alpha^{(r)})}{2Q f'(\alpha^{(r)})}.
\tag{18}
\end{equation}
Since all the $|f'(\alpha^{(r)})|$ are at least equal to $k$, it follows for the absolute value of this difference that
\begin{equation}
 |\alpha^{(\mu)}-\alpha^{(\nu+1)}|<
 \frac{(a^{(\mu)})^2+(a^{(\mu+1)})^2+\cdots+(a^{(\nu)})^2}{2Qk},
\tag{19}
\end{equation}
and hence, by (16),
\begin{equation}
 \lim |\alpha^{(\mu)}-\alpha^{(\nu+1)}|=0.
\tag{20}
\end{equation}

This says (according to the concept of a sequence of numbers explained in the Introduction, p. 15) that the numbers $\alpha,\alpha',\alpha'',\ldots$ approach a definite limit, which we shall denote by $\xi$. From the continuity of the function $f(x)$ it then follows easily that $f(\xi)=0$; for if $|f(\xi)|>0$, then, since the $\alpha^{(\nu)}$ come arbitrarily close to $\xi$, the absolute values $a^{(\nu)}$ of $f(\alpha^{(\nu)})$ could not sink below every positive value, which is precisely what we have proved for them.
