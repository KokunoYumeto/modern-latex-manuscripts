\section*{Eleventh Section. Ideals in quadratic fields.}

\section*{§ 90. Discriminant of the quadratic field.}

A quadratic field arises when one adjoins a square root $\sqrt d$ to the field of rational numbers; here $d$ may be assumed to be an integer without a square divisor. The field is real or imaginary according as $d$ is positive or negative. If one denotes the field of rational numbers, the absolute domain of rationality, by $\mathfrak R$, and by $\mathfrak R(x,x',\ldots)$ the field which arises by adjoining $x,x',\ldots$ to $\mathfrak R$, then the quadratic field $\Omega$ is to be denoted by
\begin{equation}
 \Omega=\mathfrak R(\sqrt d).
\tag{1}
\end{equation}
Every number of the field $\Omega$ can be put in the form
\begin{equation}
 \omega=\frac{x+y\sqrt d}{2},
\tag{2}
\end{equation}
where $x,y$ are rational integral or fractional numbers. The number conjugate to $\omega$,
\begin{equation}
 \omega'=\frac{x-y\sqrt d}{2},
\tag{3}
\end{equation}
is likewise contained in the field $\Omega$, and consequently $\Omega$ is a normal field.

In order that $\omega$ be an integer it is necessary that
\[
 \omega+\omega'=x,
 \qquad (\omega-\omega')^2=y^2d
\]
be rational integers; and since $d$ is to have no square divisor, $x$ and $y$ must be integers. But in order that $\omega$ really be an integer it is also still necessary that the norm $\frac14(x^2-y^2d)$ be a rational integer, that is, it must be
\begin{equation}
 x^2-y^2d\equiv0\pmod4 .
\tag{4}
\end{equation}
If $d\equiv2$ or $\equiv3\pmod4$, then this condition can be fulfilled only when $x$ and $y$ are even, and if therefore we replace $x,y$ by $2x,2y$, there follows:

\medskip
\noindent 1. If $d\equiv2,3\pmod4$, then all and only those numbers of the field $\Omega$ are integral which are contained in the form
\[
 x+y\sqrt d
\]
with rational integers $x,y$.
\medskip

If, however, $d\equiv1\pmod4$, then (3) is satisfied when $x$ and $y$ are both even or both odd, hence when $x$ has the form $2x_1-y$. If one then again replaces $x_1$ by $x$, one obtains:

\medskip
\noindent 2. If $d\equiv1\pmod4$, then all and only those numbers in $\Omega$ are integral which are contained in the form
\[
 x+y\frac{-1+\sqrt d}{2}
\]
with rational integers $x,y$.
\medskip

Thus in case 1, $(1,\sqrt d)$, and in case 2, $\left(1,\frac{-1+\sqrt d}{2}\right)$, is a minimal basis of the field $\Omega$. The fundamental number, or discriminant, of the field $\Omega$ is therefore, in case 1,
\begin{equation}
 \Delta=\begin{vmatrix}
 1&\sqrt d\\
 1&-\sqrt d
 \end{vmatrix}^{2}=4d,
\tag{5}
\end{equation}
and in case 2,
\begin{equation}
 \Delta=\begin{vmatrix}
 1&\dfrac{-1+\sqrt d}{2}\\[0.35em]
 1&\dfrac{-1-\sqrt d}{2}
 \end{vmatrix}^{2}=d.
\tag{6}
\end{equation}
In both cases, therefore, the integral number $\omega$ can be put in the form
\begin{equation}
 \omega=\frac{x+y\sqrt\Delta}{2},
\tag{7}
\end{equation}
with the condition that
\begin{equation}
 4N(\omega)=x^2-\Delta y^2\equiv0\pmod4
\tag{8}
\end{equation}
be satisfied.

The two forms of the minimal basis which we have obtained, namely $(1,\sqrt d)$ and $\left(1,\frac{-1+\sqrt d}{2}\right)$, are therefore
\begin{equation}
 (1,\theta)=\left(1,\frac12\sqrt\Delta\right),\quad \Delta\equiv0,\qquad
 =\left(1,\frac{-1+\sqrt\Delta}{2}\right),\quad \Delta\equiv1\pmod4.
\tag{9}
\end{equation}
Here the sign of $\sqrt\Delta$ may be determined in any arbitrary way, but in the same consideration it is then to be held fixed. We shall assume once for all:

\medskip
\noindent 3. If $\Delta$ is positive, then $\sqrt\Delta$ is likewise to be positive; and if $\Delta$ is negative, then $-i\sqrt\Delta$ is to be positive, or $\sqrt\Delta$ is to be positive imaginary.
\medskip

From (1), (2) there still follows:

\medskip
\noindent 4. The fundamental number of a quadratic field is always $\equiv0$ or $\equiv1$ modulo $4$ and, apart from $4$, has no square divisor. It is therefore a discriminant, indeed a fundamental discriminant.
