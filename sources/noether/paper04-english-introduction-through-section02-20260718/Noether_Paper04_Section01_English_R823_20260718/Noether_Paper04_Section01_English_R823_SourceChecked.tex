\section*{\S\ 1. Notation and Definitions. Summary of Known Results.}

As usual, we call $x$, the row of elements $x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n$, a variable row. Analogously, $p_\rho$ ($\rho=1,2,\ldots,n-1$) denotes the variable row of the $\binom n\rho$ elements $p_{i_1i_2\ldots i_\rho}$, where the $p_{i_1i_2\ldots i_\rho}$ are the $\binom n\rho$ determinants
\[
\left|\begin{array}{cccc}
 x_{i_1}^{(1)}&x_{i_2}^{(1)}&\cdots&x_{i_\rho}^{(1)}\\
 x_{i_1}^{(2)}&x_{i_2}^{(2)}&\cdots&x_{i_\rho}^{(2)}\\
 \vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
 x_{i_1}^{(\rho)}&x_{i_2}^{(\rho)}&\cdots&x_{i_\rho}^{(\rho)}
\end{array}\right|,
\qquad (i_1<i_2<\cdots<i_\rho;\quad i_1,i_2,\ldots,i_\rho\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}),
\]
formed from the matrix of the $\rho$ rows $x^{(1)},x^{(2)},\ldots,x^{(\rho)}$. By the theorem on corresponding matrices,\footnote{Cf., for example, Gordan--Kerschensteiner, \emph{Vorlesungen \"uber Invariantentheorie}, vol. I, p. 99.} to every row $p_\rho$ there corresponds uniquely a second row $q_{n-\rho}$, through the relation valid for each element:
\begin{equation}
 p_{i_1i_2\ldots i_\rho}=(-1)^{\frac{\rho(\rho+1)}{2}+i_1+i_2+\cdots+i_\rho}q_{j_1j_2\ldots j_{n-\rho}},
\tag{1}
\end{equation}
where the $i$'s and the $j$'s are each ordered and together form a complete permutation of the numbers $1$ through $n$. The row $q_{n-\rho}$ consists of the $\binom n\rho$ determinants of degree $n-\rho$,
$q_{j_1j_2\ldots j_{n-\rho}}$, formed from the matrix of $n-\rho$ rows $u^{(1)},u^{(2)},\ldots,u^{(n-\rho)}$ contragredient to the $x$-rows. These rows satisfy the $\rho(n-\rho)$ conditions
\begin{equation}
 (u^{(k)}\mid x^{(l)})=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}u_\alpha^{(k)}x_\alpha^{(l)}=0;
 \qquad (k=1,2,\ldots,n-\rho;\ l=1,2,\ldots,\rho).
\tag{2}
\end{equation}
Following Clebsch,\footnote{Loc. cit., \S\ 5.} we assume them normalized so that the proportionality factor that would otherwise occur in (1) is equal to one.

According to Clebsch\footnote{Loc. cit., \S\ 12.}---and likewise according to the later investigations of F. Deruyts and A. Capelli; cf. the Introduction---the most general forms can be reduced to forms containing at most one of each of the $n-1$ rows $p_\rho$. They can therefore be represented symbolically as
\begin{equation}
 (S^1\mid p_1)^{m_1}(S^2\mid p_2)^{m_2}\cdots(S^{n-1}\mid p_{n-1})^{m_{n-1}},
\tag{3}
\end{equation}
where, in analogy with (2), we have set
\[
 (S^\rho\mid p_\rho)=\sum S^{i_1i_2\ldots i_\rho}p_{i_1i_2\ldots i_\rho}.
\]
Because of the nonlinear identities among the elements of a row $p_\rho$, the symbol rows $S^\rho$ can be normalized so that they satisfy exactly the same identities. They then behave like the determinants of a matrix with $\rho$ rows $s^{(1)},s^{(2)},\ldots,s^{(\rho)}$ contragredient to the $x$-rows.\footnote{Clebsch, loc. cit., \S\ 4.} Thus to each row $S^\rho$ there corresponds uniquely another row $S_{n-\rho}$ through the relation
\begin{equation}
 S_{i_1i_2\ldots i_{n-\rho}}=(-1)^{\frac{(n-\rho)(n-\rho+1)}2+i_1+i_2+\cdots+i_{n-\rho}}S^{j_1j_2\ldots j_\rho},
\tag{4}
\end{equation}
where again the $i$'s and $j$'s are each ordered and together exhaust the numbers $1$ through $n$. The elements $S_{i_1i_2\ldots i_{n-\rho}}$ of the row $S_{n-\rho}$ behave like the determinants formed from $n-\rho$ rows $\sigma^{(1)},\sigma^{(2)},\ldots,\sigma^{(n-\rho)}$ cogredient to the $x$-rows, and the rows $s$ and $\sigma$ are linked by the $\rho(n-\rho)$ conditions
\begin{equation}
 (s^{(k)}\mid \sigma^{(l)})=0,\qquad(k=1,2,\ldots,\rho;\ l=1,2,\ldots,n-\rho).
\tag{5}
\end{equation}
By (1) and (4), every factor of (3) admits the four equivalent symbolic representations
\begin{equation}
 (S^\rho\mid p_\rho)=(S^\rho q_{n-\rho})=(S_{n-\rho}p_\rho)=(-1)^{\rho(n-\rho)}(q_{n-\rho}\mid S_{n-\rho}),\footnotemark
\tag{6}
\end{equation}
\footnotetext{Cf. Clebsch, loc. cit., \S\ 5.}
Here and throughout what follows, $(S^\rho q_{n-\rho})$ and $(S_{n-\rho}p_\rho)$ abbreviate the determinants
$(s^{(1)}\cdots s^{(\rho)}u^{(1)}\cdots u^{(n-\rho)})$ and
$(\sigma^{(1)}\cdots\sigma^{(n-\rho)}x^{(1)}\cdots x^{(\rho)})$.
Laplace expansion shows that these depend only on the rows $S^\rho,q_{n-\rho}$ and $S_{n-\rho},p_\rho$, respectively, as the notation is intended to indicate. In accordance with the preceding discussion, $(S^\rho\mid p_\rho)$ and $(q_{n-\rho}\mid S_{n-\rho})$ denote the products of the matrices of the rows $s$ and $x$, and of the rows $u$ and $\sigma$, respectively. By a matrix product we mean the sum of the products of corresponding determinants, that is, the determinant of the product matrix.

Following Clebsch, we call the characteristic number $\rho(n-\rho)$ belonging to a symbol row (or variable row) its \emph{weight};\footnote{Loc. cit., \S\ 11.} the number $\rho$, which gives the number of rows $s$, is its upper weight index, and the number $n-\rho$, which gives the number of rows $\sigma$, is its lower weight index. It follows from (4) that a symbol row can occur in the same relation once with an upper and once with a lower weight index; the same holds for the associated rows $p_\rho$ and $q_{n-\rho}$. We further use the following general notation. Higher-order variable rows whose associated matrix consists of $x$-rows are denoted by $p$, and those whose matrix consists of $u$-rows by $q$. Symbol rows cogredient to the $x$-rows are denoted by lowercase Greek letters $\sigma,\tau,\alpha,\beta$; symbol rows cogredient to the $u$-rows by lowercase Latin letters $s,t,a,b$; and all other symbol rows by uppercase Latin letters carrying a weight index: $S^\rho$, $T^\tau$, $A^\rho$, or $S_{n-\sigma}$, $T_{n-\tau}$, $A_{n-\rho}$. Correspondingly, the individual elements of a row are written with upper or lower indices according as the weight index is upper or lower, as in (4).
