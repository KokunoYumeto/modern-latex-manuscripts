    § 9. Form Rows. Reduction Theorems[152 : ∗] .
    We now define the form row 1 introduced at the end of § 6 somewhat more precisely as “the totality
    of invariant formations, linear in the coefficients, of a given initial form; that is, the totality of forms
    obtained from the initial form by folding it in itself, in the ordering by higher forms.”
       To explain the higher forms, let the initial form, by Theorem VII, be given as the product of two
    normal forms S · T . By a higher form we shall mean a form with a higher folding in itself, or, among
    forms with the same folding in themselves, one specially normalized. More precisely, suppose that
    the form A has arisen through αρ fundamental foldings of type ρ, and the form B through βρ such
    foldings. Then B is higher than A:

        1) if, in the coordinatewise system of inequalities βρ ≥ αρ , ρ = 1, 2, . . . , n − 1, strict inequality
           holds for at least one value of ρ[152 : ∗∗] ;

        2) if equality holds for every value of ρ, but fewer symbol rows have been combined into a single
           matrix product. This normalization is necessary because of the reductions in § 8. For example,
           (S n−1 | Tn−1 ) is higher than (S n−1 | [S 1 T ρ ]n−ρ−1 pρ ).

        3) if equality holds for every value of ρ and the number of symbol rows combined into a single
           matrix product is the same, then B is ranked higher than A by a normalization that is arbitrary
           in itself but fixed once and for all. Such a normalization is always possible because only finitely
           many forms belong to a value system α; it may, for example, be achieved by giving priority to
           one form S (a larger number of symbol rows S, a larger sum of the upper weight indices of the
           rows S, and so on).

       It is useful to picture the form row as an (n − 1)-dimensional manifold of lattice points, with the
    n − 1 directions corresponding to the n − 1 fundamental foldings. Each form is then assigned one
    and only one value system α, that is, a positive integral lattice point.2 The ordering of the different
    forms belonging to a value system α is uniquely fixed by the normalization above. Any form in the
    form row is the initial form of a new form row contained as a part of the total form row: namely, the
    part consisting of the forms obtained by proceeding from the new initial form in the n − 1 positive
    directions.
       By Theorem VIII and the general theory of series expansions, precisely the reduction theorems
    of the binary and ternary domains (cf. dissertation, § 3) now hold for form rows. We shall briefly
    derive the principal theorem, first giving the definition:
       “In a given system of forms, let some finite number of forms be combined into a module. We call
    a form reducible if it can be expressed in terms of forms having invariants as factors or in terms

  Then:
      Theorem IX: If the initial form of a form row is reducible because it has arisen by folding with a
  reducer, then the entire form row arising from that initial form is reducible.
      For the proof, observe that a form h obtained by folding a form f with a second form g may, by
  (45), be regarded as a form f containing several cogredient variable rows pρ , p′ρ .3 By the general
  theory of series expansions, such forms can be represented as polars of the elementary covariants of f ,
  by applying the series expansion successively to each pair of cogredient variable rows pρ , p′ρ . By (38),
  the elementary covariants that occur are the forms generated by cogredient folding. Since the order
  in which the series expansion is applied remains arbitrary, we may in particular choose an order
  corresponding to the generation of general foldings from fundamental foldings. The resulting system
  of elementary covariants is then identical with the form row f ; the forms generated by cogredient
  folding of higher defect take their place among the forms generated by fundamental foldings.
      Polars of the form row f are, however, also obtained with any order of series expansion, to which
  a second system of elementary covariants may correspond. Since the form row exhausts the totality
  of invariant formations, every form in the second system can be expressed linearly in terms of polars
  of the form row, specifically polars of those forms that exhibit the same or a greater weight decrease.
  This last assertion follows because (cf. § 7) the polars may be regarded as simultaneous invariants
  of a form row H in (52), with degrees corresponding to the form h, and of the form row f . Every
  folding of H in itself corresponds to a weight decrease that, after substitution (11) is performed, is
  transferred to the symbol rows and hence to the forms of f [154 : ∗] .
      An arbitrary form of the form row h has arisen by folding h in itself; that is, by a higher folding
  of g with f on the one hand, and by folding f or g in itself on the other. In both cases the series
  expansion leads to polars of the form row f , just as shown above for the initial form h. In particular,
  if f is a reducer, an expansion in polars of the reducer results; hence the form row h is reducible.
      Applications of the theorem to the reduction of complete systems of forms follow analogously to
  the binary and ternary domains.
