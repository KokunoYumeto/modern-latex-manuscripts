\section*{\S\ 9. Form Rows. Reduction Theorems\NoetherSrcNote{152:*}{Cf. the corresponding considerations in the ternary domain, dissertation, \S\S\ 1--3.}.}

We now define the \emph{form row}\footnote{\textbf{R823 source defects
N04-S09-SRCDEF-001--012:} R823 has six emphasis-defect classes comprising
nine printed runs at lines 4478, 4480, 4483, 4490, 4492, and 4494; it also loses two punctuation
marks and a page-local note identity, moves two note calls past their printed
punctuation, and replaces the printed list labels 1), 2), 3) by 1., 2., 3.
The English follows the print; the German authority remains unchanged.}
introduced at the end of \S\ 6 somewhat more precisely as ``the totality of
\emph{invariant} formations, linear in the coefficients, of a given initial
form; that is, the totality of forms obtained from the initial form by
folding it in itself, \emph{in the ordering by higher forms}.''

To explain the higher forms, let the initial form, by Theorem VII, be given
as the product of two normal forms $S\cdot T$. By a \emph{higher form} we
shall mean a form with a higher folding in itself, or, among forms with the
same folding in themselves, one specially normalized. More precisely,
suppose that the form $A$ has arisen through $\alpha_\rho$ fundamental
foldings of type $\rho$, and the form $B$ through $\beta_\rho$ such
foldings. Then $B$ is higher than $A$:
\begin{enumerate}
\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{\arabic{enumi})}
\item if, in the coordinatewise system of inequalities
$\beta_\rho\geq\alpha_\rho$, $\rho=1,2,\ldots,n-1$, strict inequality
holds for at least one value of $\rho$\NoetherSrcNote{152:**}{If some of
the comparisons have the sign $>$ and others the sign $<$, the forms are
not comparable, since a form obtained from another by folding always has a
positive value system of fundamental foldings; hence, in our case, the
values $\beta_\rho-\alpha_\rho$ are positive or zero.};
\item if equality holds for every value of $\rho$, but \emph{fewer} symbol
rows have been combined into a single matrix product. This normalization is
necessary because of the reductions in \S\ 8. For example,
$\pair{S^{n-1}}{T_{n-1}}$ is higher than
$\pair{S^{n-1}}{[S^1T^\rho]_{n-\rho-1}p_\rho}$.
\item if equality holds for every value of $\rho$ and the number of symbol
rows combined into a single matrix product is the same, then $B$ is ranked
higher than $A$ by a normalization that is arbitrary in itself but fixed
once and for all. Such a normalization is always possible because only
finitely many forms belong to a value system $\alpha$; it may, for example,
be achieved by giving priority to one form $S$ (a larger number of symbol
rows $S$, a larger sum of the upper weight indices of the rows $S$, and so
on).
\end{enumerate}

It is useful to picture the form row as an $(n-1)$-dimensional manifold of
lattice points, with the $n-1$ directions corresponding to the $n-1$
fundamental foldings. Each form is then assigned one and only one value
system $\alpha$, that is, a positive integral lattice
point.\footnote{\textbf{Source ambiguity N04-S09-AMB-001:} ``positive
integral'' is retained literally, although the initial form has the zero
value system. The source does not resolve whether zero coordinates are
included under this wording.}
The ordering of the different forms belonging to a value system $\alpha$
is uniquely fixed by the normalization above. Any form in the form row is
the initial form of a new form row contained as a part of the total form
row: namely, the part consisting of the forms obtained by proceeding from
the new initial form in the $n-1$ positive directions.

By Theorem VIII and the general theory of series expansions, precisely the
reduction theorems of the binary and ternary domains (cf. dissertation,
\S\ 3) now hold for form rows. We shall briefly derive the principal
theorem, first giving the definition:

``In a given system of forms, let some finite number of forms be combined
into a module. We call a form \emph{reducible} if it can be expressed in
terms of forms having invariants as factors or in terms of `higher forms';
that is, higher forms in the total form row to which the form belongs, or
forms containing the symbols of the module to a higher order. We call a
reducible form row a \emph{reducer}.'' Then:

\emph{Theorem IX: If the initial form of a form row is reducible because it
has arisen by folding with a reducer, then the entire form row arising from
that initial form is reducible.}

For the proof, observe that a form $h$ obtained by folding a form $f$ with a
second form $g$ may, by (45), be regarded as a form $f$ containing several
cogredient variable rows $p_\rho,p'_\rho$.\footnote{\textbf{Source
ambiguity N04-S09-AMB-002:} the print and R823 line 4494 reuse $f$ while
describing $h$. The target preserves that symbol rather than silently
renaming it.} By the general theory of series
expansions, such forms can be
represented as polars of the elementary covariants of $f$, by applying the
series expansion successively to each pair of cogredient variable rows
$p_\rho,p'_\rho$. By (38), the elementary covariants that occur are the
forms generated by cogredient folding. Since the order in which the series
expansion is applied remains arbitrary, we may in particular choose an
order corresponding to the generation of general foldings from fundamental
foldings. The resulting system of elementary covariants is then identical
with the form row $f$; the forms generated by cogredient folding of higher
defect take their place among the forms generated by fundamental foldings.

Polars of the form row $f$ are, however, also obtained with any order of
series expansion, to which a second system of elementary covariants may
correspond. Since the form row exhausts the totality of invariant
formations, every form in the second system can be expressed linearly in
terms of polars of the form row, specifically polars of those forms that
exhibit the same or a greater weight decrease. This last assertion follows
because (cf. \S\ 7) the polars may be regarded as simultaneous invariants
of a form row $H$ in (52), with degrees corresponding to the form $h$, and
of the form row $f$. Every folding of $H$ in itself corresponds to a weight
decrease that, after substitution (11) is performed, is transferred to the
symbol rows and hence to the forms of $f$\NoetherSrcNote{154:*}{This
consideration also underlies the second formulation of the concept of
equivalence at the end of \S\ 8. Further discussion of the different
systems of elementary covariants in the ternary domain is found in
\emph{Study}, loc. cit., II, \S\ 7, Theorems 7) and 8). By our Theorem VII,
the same results also hold for $n$ variables.}.

An arbitrary form of the form row $h$ has arisen by folding $h$ in itself;
that is, by a higher folding of $g$ with $f$ on the one hand, and by folding
$f$ or $g$ in itself on the other. In both cases the series expansion leads
to polars of the form row $f$, just as shown above for the initial form
$h$. In particular, if $f$ is a reducer, an expansion in polars of the
reducer results; hence the form row $h$ is reducible.

Applications of the theorem to the reduction of complete systems of forms
follow analogously to the binary and ternary domains.
