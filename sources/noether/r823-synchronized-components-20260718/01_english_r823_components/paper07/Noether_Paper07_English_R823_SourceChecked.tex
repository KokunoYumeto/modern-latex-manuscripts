\section*{7. The finiteness theorem for the invariants of finite groups}
\begin{center}
Math. Ann. 77 (1916), pp. 89--92
\end{center}

\begin{center}
{\Large The finiteness theorem for the invariants of finite groups.}\par
\vspace{1.2em}
By\par
\vspace{0.8em}
{\scshape Emmy Noether} in Erlangen.
\end{center}

In what follows an entirely elementary proof of finiteness for the invariants of finite groups is to be given --- one based only on the theory of symmetric functions --- and at the same time it gives an actual specification of the full system; whereas the usual proof, based on Hilbert's theorem on module bases (Ann. 36), is only an existence proof.\NoetherSrcNote{*)}{Cf., for example, Weber, \emph{Lehrbuch der Algebra} (2nd ed.), vol. 2, \S\ 57.}

Let the finite group $\Hgrp$ consist of the $h$ linear transformations (with non-vanishing determinant) $A_1,\ldots,A_h$, where $A_k$ denotes the linear transformation
\[
  x_1^{(k)}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{n}a_{1\nu}^{(k)}x_\nu,\ldots,
  x_n^{(k)}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{n}a_{n\nu}^{(k)}x_\nu,
\]
or, in abbreviated form, $(x^{(k)})=A_k(x)$. The group $\Hgrp$ therefore carries the row $(x)$ with elements $x_1,
\ldots,x_n$ into the rows $(x^{(k)})$ with elements $x_1^{(k)},
\ldots,x_n^{(k)}$. Since the identity must be contained among $A_1,
\ldots,A_h$, the row $(x)$ is also contained among the rows $(x^{(k)})$. By an integral rational (absolute) invariant of the group is meant an integral rational function of $x_1,
\ldots,x_n$ which remains identically unchanged under the application of $A_1,
\ldots,A_h$; for such an invariant $f(x)$ one therefore has
\begin{equation}
  f(x)=f(x^{(1)})=\cdots=f(x^{(h)})=
  \frac1h\sum_{k=1}^{h}f(x^{(k)}).
\tag{1}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{1.}
Formula (1) says that $f(x)$ is an integral rational symmetric function of the rows of quantities $(x^{(1)}),
\ldots,(x^{(h)})$ --- and indeed, since each summand $f(x^{(k)})$ contains only the one row $(x^{(k)})$, this is the simplest case, usually called the \emph{one-form} case. By the known theorem on symmetric functions of rows of quantities,\NoetherSrcNote{*)}{Cf. the note to 2.} $f(x)$ is therefore integrally and rationally representable by the elementary symmetric functions of these rows, that is, by the coefficients $G_{\alpha\alpha_1\ldots\alpha_n}(x)$ of the ``Galois resolvent''
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \Phi(z,u)
  &=\prod_{k=1}^{h}\bigl(z+u_1x_1^{(k)}+\cdots+u_nx_n^{(k)}\bigr) \\
  &=z^h+
    \sum G_{\alpha\alpha_1\ldots\alpha_n}(x)
    z^{\alpha}u_1^{\alpha_1}\cdots u_n^{\alpha_n},
\end{aligned}
\qquad
\left(\begin{array}{c}
\alpha+\alpha_1+
\cdots+
\alpha_n=h\\
\alpha\ne h
\end{array}\right),
\]
where the $G_{\alpha\alpha_1\ldots\alpha_n}(x)$ are invariants of degree $\alpha_1+\cdots+\alpha_n$ in the $x$'s. Thus it is proved:

\emph{The coefficients $G_{\alpha\alpha_1\ldots\alpha_n}(x)$ of the Galois resolvent form a full system of invariants of the group, in such a way that every invariant can be represented integrally and rationally by these finitely many invariants.}

\subsection*{2.}
Starting from (1), one may also make the following still more elementary consideration,\NoetherSrcNote{**)}{This amounts to a proof of the theorem on symmetric functions of rows of quantities in the above-mentioned one-form case.} which at the same time leads to a second full system. Put
\[
  f(x)=a+b x_1^{\mu_1}\cdots x_n^{\mu_n}
       +\cdots+c x_1^{\nu_1}\cdots x_n^{\nu_n},
\]
where $a,b,\ldots,c$ denote constants. Then by (1) one has
\[
\begin{aligned}
 h\cdot f(x)
 &=h\cdot a+b\sum_{k=1}^{h}(x_1^{(k)})^{\mu_1}\cdots(x_n^{(k)})^{\mu_n}
     +\cdots
     +c\sum_{k=1}^{h}(x_1^{(k)})^{\nu_1}\cdots(x_n^{(k)})^{\nu_n} \\
 &=h\cdot a+b\cdot J_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_n}+\cdots+c\cdot J_{\nu_1\ldots\nu_n}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Every invariant can therefore be composed integrally and linearly from the special invariants
\[
  J_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_n}
  =\sum_{k=1}^{h}(x_1^{(k)})^{\mu_1}\cdots(x_n^{(k)})^{\mu_n},
\]
and it is enough to prove the assertion for these special ones. But, apart from a numerical factor, $J_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_n}$ is the coefficient of $u_1^{\mu_1}
\cdots u_n^{\mu_n}$ in the expression
\[
  S_\mu=\sum_{k=1}^{h}
  \bigl(u_1x_1^{(k)}+\cdots+u_nx_n^{(k)}\bigr)^{\mu},
\]
where $\mu=\mu_1+\cdots+\mu_n$, and this expression is the $\mu$-th power sum of the $h$ linear forms
\[
  \xi_1=u_1x_1^{(1)}+\cdots+u_nx_n^{(1)},\ldots,
  \xi_h=u_1x_1^{(h)}+\cdots+u_nx_n^{(h)}.
\]
It is known that the infinitely many power sums $S_\mu$ are integral rational combinations of
\[
  S_1,S_2,\ldots,S_h,
\]
whose coefficients are given by the invariants
\[
  J_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_n}\qquad(\mu_1+\cdots+\mu_n\le h).
\]
Thus a second full system has been obtained:

\emph{A full system of invariants of the group is given by all invariants $J_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_n}$ for which $\mu_1+\cdots+\mu_n\le h$, where $h$ denotes the order of the group.}

The connection with 1. is established by observing that the power sums $S_1,
\ldots,S_h$ can be replaced by the elementary symmetric functions of $\xi_1,
\ldots,
\xi_h$, which leads to the full system of the $G_{\alpha\alpha_1\ldots\alpha_n}(x)$ given there. Both results show that all invariants can be expressed integrally and rationally by those whose degree in the $x$'s does not exceed the order $h$ of the group.

\subsection*{3.}
Consequences for rational representation can be drawn from what has just been proved. Every rational absolute invariant can be represented --- as is seen immediately by multiplying numerator and denominator by the conjugates of the denominator with respect to the group --- as the quotient of two integral rational absolute invariants, not necessarily relatively prime. It follows that every rational absolute invariant can be expressed rationally by the coefficients $G_{\alpha\alpha_1\ldots\alpha_n}(x)$ of the Galois resolvent $\Phi(z,u)$. This theorem can also be proved easily in various ways without passing through the finiteness theorem; it is already formulated in Weber II, \S\ 58.\NoetherSrcNote{*)}{The proof given there, however, is not sound. It is only shown that the function $\Psi(t)$ in formula (7) has invariants as coefficients, but not that these invariants are rationally expressible by the coefficients of $\Phi(t)$. This gap is avoided if, in representing the $x_i^{(k)}$ by $\xi_k$, one uses a known differentiation procedure instead of Lagrange's interpolation formula. This is the relation obtained by differentiating the identity $\Phi(-\xi_k,u)=0$ with respect to all $u$:
\[
 \left[\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial u_i}
      -x_i^{(k)}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z}\right]_{z=-\xi_k}=0.
\]
Accordingly, for each rational function $\omega(x)$ the following formula must replace formulas (7) and (8) in Weber:
\[
 \omega(x_1^{(k)},\ldots,x_n^{(k)})=
 \left.
 \omega\!\left(
   \frac{\partial\Phi/\partial u_1}{\partial\Phi/\partial z},\ldots,
   \frac{\partial\Phi/\partial u_n}{\partial\Phi/\partial z}
 \right)\right|_{z=-\xi_k},
\]
which contains only coefficients of $\Phi(z,u)$, and whose summation over all $k$, for invariants $\omega(x)$, gives the desired representation.}

\subsection*{4.}
Finally, it should be mentioned that the results derived here are implicitly contained in my paper ``Fields and systems of rational functions'';\NoetherSrcNote{*)}{Math. Ann. 76, p. 161 (1915).} more precisely, the full system given in 1. is contained in Theorems VI and VII, and a proof of rational representation independent of this finiteness theorem is contained in Theorem III.\NoetherSrcNote{**)}{For relative invariants, Theorems VIII and IX contain a proof of finiteness which is likewise based on a different foundation from the usual one, but is also only an existence proof; the reason is that the relative invariants do not form a field.} In the present special case, however, the course of the proof and the result simplify considerably in comparison with the general theory. I was led to apply the general investigations to the invariants of finite groups by conversations with E. Fischer, from whom, in substance, the proof given under 2 --- in the general case the full system that appears there is not rationally definable --- and the note to 3 also originate.

Erlangen, May 1915.
