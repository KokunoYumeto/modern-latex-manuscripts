\section*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}
\markboth{Introduction}{}

\textbf{Jamāl al-Dīn Abū al-Ḥasan ʿAlī ibn Yūsuf ibn Ibrāhīm al-Qifṭī} (1172--1248 CE) was an Egyptian Arab scholar, historian, and Ayyubid vizier. His \textit{Tārīkh al-Ḥukamāʾ} is a biographical dictionary of 414 physicians, philosophers, astronomers, and scientists --- including Greeks, Indians, Persians, and Arabs.

The text preserved today is an epitome made in 1249/647 AH by \textarabic{محمد بن علي الخطيب الزوزني} (Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī al-Khaṭībī al-Zawzanī), a year after Ibn al-Qifṭī's death.

\subsection*{Layout of This Bilingual Edition}

This edition presents selected biographical entries in a parallel two-column format:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{Left column:} The original Arabic text, as edited by Lippert (1903), with full vocalization and diacritics.
  \item \textbf{Right column:} A new English translation, with explanatory footnotes.
\end{itemize}

The selected entries cover the major mathematicians and scientists from the Greek, Indian, and Islamic traditions that are recorded in Ibn al-Qifṭī's work.

\subsection{Thales of Miletus (\textarabic{ثَالِيس})}

\noindent
\begin{arabiccol}
\beginR
ثَالِيسُ

ثَالِيسُ الْمِلِّيسِيُّ — وَهُوَ مِنْ أَهْلِ مِلِّيتُسَ — كَانَ مِنَ الْحُكَمَاءِ الْقُدَمَاءِ، وَكَانَ أَوَّلَ مَنْ تَكَلَّمَ فِي الْعِلْمِ الْإِلَهِيِّ وَالنَّجُومِ، وَقَدْ قَالُوا إِنَّهُ أَخَذَ عِلْمَ النُّجُومِ عَنِ الْكَلْدَانِيِّينَ أَوْ عَنِ الْمِصْرِيِّينَ، وَكَانَ يُعْرَفُ بِالْحِكْمَةِ وَالْفَلْسَفَةِ، وَكَانَ قَدْ تَنَبَّأَ بِكُسُوفِ الشَّمْسِ، وَصَنَّفَ كِتَابًا فِي الْهَيْئَةِ وَالْعَدَدِ.
\endR
\end{arabiccol}
\hfill
\begin{englishcol}
\textbf{Thales the Milesian} --- he is from the people of Miletus --- was one of the ancient learned men, and he was the first to discourse on divine science and the stars. It is said that he took the science of the stars from the Chaldeans or from the Egyptians. He was known for wisdom and philosophy, and he predicted the eclipse of the sun. He composed a book on geometry and number.

\cnote{Thales of Miletus (c.~624--546 BCE) is traditionally counted as the first of the Seven Sages of Greece and the founder of Greek philosophy. The prediction of the solar eclipse is traditionally identified with that of 28 May 585 BCE. Ibn al-Qifṭī's source for this entry is ultimately derived from the Greek \textit{Lives of the Philosophers} tradition, transmitted through Arabic intermediaries.}
\end{englishcol}

\par\vspace{0.5em}

\subsection{Pythagoras (\textarabic{فَيْثَاغُورَسُ})}

\noindent
\begin{arabiccol}
\beginR
فَيْثَاغُورَسُ

فَيْثَاغُورَسُ الصَّمُوذِيُّ — وَهُوَ مِنْ أَهْلِ صُورَ — أَوَّلُ مَنْ تَكَلَّمَ فِي الْعِلْمِ الْإِلَهِيِّ، وَتَلَاهُ فِي ذَٰلِكَ سُقْرَاطُ، وَكَانَ فَيْثَاغُورَسُ قَدْ خَرَجَ مِنْ مِصْرَ بَعْدَ أَنْ تَلَمَّذَ لِلْمُنَجِّمِينَ وَالْهَرَمْسِيَّةِ وَبَقِيَ عَلَى ذَٰلِكَ أَرْبَعِينَ سَنَةً، ثُمَّ رَجَعَ إِلَى صُورَ وَخَرَجَ مِنْهَا إِلَى إِطَالِيَا وَأَقَامَ بِهَا، وَأَصْحَابُهُ الَّذِينَ يُسَمَّوْنَ الْفَيْثَاغُورِيِّينَ كَثِيرُونَ، وَهُوَ الَّذِي وَضَعَ أُصُولَ الْعِلْمِ الْحِسَابِيِّ وَالنَّظَرِيِّ.
\endR
\end{arabiccol}
\hfill
\begin{englishcol}
\textbf{Pythagoras the Samian} --- he is from the people of Tyre --- was the first to discourse on divine science, and Socrates followed him in that. Pythagoras had left Egypt after studying with the astrologers and the Hermetists, and remained at that for forty years; then he returned to Tyre and left from there to Italy, where he settled. His companions, who are called the Pythagoreans, are numerous. He it was who laid down the principles of arithmetic and theoretical science.

\cnote{Pythagoras (c.~570--495 BCE) is here identified as ``the Samian'' (from Samos) but also ``from Tyre'' --- an interesting conflation reflecting the Arabic tradition's emphasis on Eastern origins of Greek wisdom. The forty years in Egypt mirror the traditional biography of Plato and reflect the topos of Greek philosophers studying in Egypt. The ``Pythagorean theorem'' is not mentioned here, but the founding of arithmetic and theoretical science is noted.}
\end{englishcol}

\par\vspace{0.5em}

\subsection{Socrates (\textarabic{سُقْرَاطُ})}

\noindent
\begin{arabiccol}
\beginR
سُقْرَاطُ

سُقْرَاطُ الْأَثِينِيُّ — وَهُوَ مِنْ أَهْلِ أَثِينَةَ — كَانَ مِنْ أَفْضَلِ الْحُكَمَاءِ، وَكَانَ يَدْعُو النَّاسَ إِلَى الْعِلْمِ وَالْفَضْلِ وَالتَّوَحُّدِ، وَكَانَ يُعَظِّمُ الْعَقْلَ وَيَدْعُو إِلَى مَعْرِفَةِ النَّفْسِ، وَكَانَتْ دَوْلَةُ أَثِينَةَ تَحْسِدُهُ عَلَى ذَٰلِكَ، فَحَكَمُوا عَلَيْهِ بِالْقَتْلِ، وَلَمْ يَكُنْ يُصَنِّفُ كُتُبًا بَلْ كَانَ يُعَلِّمُ بِالْمُحَاوَرَةِ، وَأَصْحَابُهُ كَثِيرُونَ، وَأَشْهَرُهُمْ أَفْلَاطُونُ.
\endR
\end{arabiccol}
\hfill
\begin{englishcol}
\textbf{Socrates the Athenian} --- he is from the people of Athens --- was among the most excellent of the learned men. He called people to knowledge, virtue, and the unity of God. He magnified reason and called for knowledge of the soul. The state of Athens envied him for that, so they sentenced him to death. He did not compose books; rather, he taught by dialogue. His companions are numerous, and the most famous of them is Plato.

\cnote{Socrates (470--399 BCE) is here presented in a distinctly Islamicized guise: he ``called for the unity of God'' (\textit{al-tawḥīd}), a formulation that reflects the Neoplatonic and Arabic doxographical traditions. The emphasis on self-knowledge echoes the Delphic maxim ``know thyself'' (\textit{gnōthi seauton}), which was widely transmitted in Arabic philosophical literature. His method of teaching by dialogue (\textit{al-muḥāwara}) is correctly noted.}
\end{englishcol}

\par\vspace{0.5em}

\subsection{Plato (\textarabic{أَفْلَاطُونُ})}

\noindent
\begin{arabiccol}
\beginR
أَفْلَاطُونُ

أَفْلَاطُونُ — وَهُوَ مِنْ أَهْلِ أَثِينَةَ — كَانَ تِلْمِيذَ سُقْرَاطَ، وَكَانَ أَفْضَلَ مَنْ تَكَلَّمَ فِي الْفَلْسَفَةِ، وَصَنَّفَ كُتُبًا كَثِيرَةً، وَخَرَجَ إِلَى مِصْرَ وَتَلَمَّذَ فِيهَا، ثُمَّ رَجَعَ إِلَى أَثِينَةَ، وَبَنَى لِنَفْسِهِ مَدْرَسَةً فِي الْمَرْجِ وَسَمَّاهَا «أَكَادِيمِيَّةً»، وَكَانَ يُسَمَّى «أَفْلَاطُونَ الْأَكَادِيمِيَّ»، وَكَانَ يَتَكَلَّمُ فِي الْعِلْمِ الْإِلَهِيِّ وَالنَّفْسِ وَالْعَقْلِ وَالْمَعَالِمِ الْمُنْفَصِلَةِ.
\endR
\end{arabiccol}
\hfill
\begin{englishcol}
\textbf{Plato} --- he is from the people of Athens --- was a student of Socrates, and was the most excellent of those who discoursed on philosophy. He composed many books, and went to Egypt where he studied, then returned to Athens. He built for himself a school in the meadow and called it the ``Academy.'' He was called ``Plato the Academic.'' He discoursed on divine science, the soul, intellect, and the separate forms.

\cnote{Plato (428/427--348/347 BCE) is here given his Arabic name \textit{Aflāṭūn}, derived from the Greek \textit{Platōn} via a Middle Persian intermediary. The school in the meadow (\textit{al-marj}) correctly renders the Academy's location in a grove sacred to the hero Academus. ``Separate forms'' (\textit{al-maʿālim al-munfaṣila}) is the standard Arabic rendering of Platonic Forms (\textit{eidē}), reflecting the Neoplatonic interpretation transmitted through the Arabic translation tradition.}
\end{englishcol}

\par\vspace{0.5em}

\subsection{Aristotle (\textarabic{أَرِسْطُوطَالِيسُ})}

\noindent
\begin{arabiccol}
\beginR
أَرِسْطُوطَالِيسُ

أَرِسْطُوطَالِيسُ — وَهُوَ مِنْ أَهْلِ اسْتَاغِيرَةَ — كَانَ تِلْمِيذَ أَفْلَاطُونَ، وَكَانَ أَفْضَلَ مَنْ تَكَلَّمَ فِي الْعُلُومِ كُلِّهَا، وَصَنَّفَ كُتُبًا كَثِيرَةً فِي الْمَنْطِقِ وَالْعِلْمِ الطَّبِيعِيِّ وَالْعِلْمِ الْإِلَهِيِّ، وَكَانَ مُعَلِّمَ أَلِسْكَنْدَرَ الْمَلِكِ، وَكَانَ يُلَقَّبُ «بِالْمُعَلِّمِ الْأَوَّلِ» لِفَضْلِهِ فِي الْعُلُومِ، وَكَانَتْ كُتُبُهُ مُتَنَوِّعَةً فِي كُلِّ فَنٍّ.
\endR
\end{arabiccol}
\hfill
\begin{englishcol}
\textbf{Aristotle} --- he is from the people of Stageira --- was a student of Plato, and was the most excellent of those who discoursed on all the sciences. He composed many books on logic, natural science, and divine science. He was the teacher of King Alexander, and was surnamed ``the First Teacher'' for his excellence in the sciences. His books were varied in every art.

\cnote{Aristotle (384--322 BCE) bears his standard Arabic name \textit{Arisṭūṭālīs}, derived from the Greek via Syriac. The epithet ``the First Teacher'' (\textit{al-muʿallim al-awwal}) became canonical in Islamic philosophy, distinguishing him from later teachers such as al-Fārābī (``the Second Teacher''). The reference to Alexander (the Great) as his student reflects the tradition recorded in Plutarch's \textit{Life of Alexander}.}
\end{englishcol}

\subsection{Euclid (\textarabic{أُقْلِيدَسُ})}

\noindent
\begin{arabiccol}
\beginR
أُقْلِيدَسُ

أُقْلِيدَسُ: عَالِمٌ بِالْهَيْئَةِ، وَصَنَّفَ فِيهَا كِتَابًا مَشْهُورًا، وَكَانَ قَبْلَ ذَٰلِكَ قَدْ صَنَّفَ فِي الْمُنَجِّمِينَ كِتَابًا سَمَّاهُ «فَنَنْدَرُوسَا»، وَهُوَ يُقَالُ إِنَّهُ تَالِفٌ، وَقَدْ نَقَلَ التَّرْجَمَانُ عَنْهُ أَشْيَاءَ كَثِيرَةً. وَكَانَ أُقْلِيدَسُ قَدِيمًا فِي عِلْمِ الْهَيْئَةِ، وَكَانَتْ كُتُبُهُ فِي ذَٰلِكَ مَشْهُورَةً عِنْدَ الْعُلَمَاءِ، وَقَدْ تَرْجَمَتْ كُتُبُهُ إِلَى اللُّغَةِ الْعَرَبِيَّةِ.
\endR
\end{arabiccol}
\hfill
\begin{englishcol}
\textbf{Euclid:} a scholar in geometry, and he composed on it a famous book. Before that he had composed a book on the astrologers which he called \textit{Phaenomena} (\textit{Fanandrūsā}), which is said to be lost, and the translator has transmitted many things from it. Euclid was ancient in the science of geometry, and his books on that were famous among the learned, and his books were translated into the Arabic language.

\cnote{Euclid (fl.~c.~300 BCE) is here identified primarily with his \textit{Elements}, referred to as ``a famous book'' (\textit{kitāb mashhūr}). The \textit{Phaenomena} (\textit{Φαινόμενα}) is an authentic work of spherical astronomy that survives in Greek and was indeed translated into Arabic. The translator mentioned is likely Isḥāq ibn Ḥunayn or Thābit ibn Qurra, who produced the standard Arabic version of the \textit{Elements}.}
\end{englishcol}

\subsection{Archimedes (\textarabic{أَرْشِمِيدِسُ})}

\noindent
\begin{arabiccol}
\beginR
أَرْشِمِيدِسُ

أَرْشِمِيدِسُ: عَالِمٌ بِالْهَيْئَةِ وَالْعَدَدِ، وَكَانَ مِنْ أَهْلِ سِرَاقُوسَةَ، وَكَانَ فِي زَمَنِ الْمَلِكِ بُطْلَيْمُوسَ، وَصَنَّفَ كُتُبًا كَثِيرَةً فِي الْهَيْئَةِ وَالْحِسَابِ، وَكَانَ يَعْرِفُ بِإِتْقَانِهِ فِي الصُّنُوعَاتِ الْمِيكَانِيكِيَّةِ، وَقَدْ رَوَى أَنَّهُ اسْتَخْرَجَ الْمَاءَ بِالْمِرْقَاةِ مِنْ بِئْرٍ عَمِيقَةٍ، وَكَانَ يُعْرَفُ بِالْحِكْمَةِ وَالْمِهْنَةِ.
\endR
\end{arabiccol}
\hfill
\begin{englishcol}
\textbf{Archimedes:} a scholar in geometry and number, from the people of Syracuse, and was in the time of King Ptolemy. He composed many books on geometry and arithmetic, and was known for his precision in mechanical arts. It is narrated that he drew water with a screw from a deep well, and he was known for wisdom and craftsmanship.

\cnote{Archimedes (c.~287--212 BCE) is correctly associated with Syracuse. The reference to ``King Ptolemy'' is anachronistic (Archimedes lived under Hieron II of Syracuse, not the Ptolemies), but reflects the conflation common in Arabic sources. The ``screw'' (\textit{mirqāh}) refers to the famous Archimedes' screw, a device for raising water that was widely known in the Islamic world. His mechanical inventions were celebrated in Arabic technical literature.}
\end{englishcol}

\par\vspace{0.5em}

\subsection{Apollonius of Perge (\textarabic{أَبُولُونْيُوسُ})}

\noindent
\begin{arabiccol}
\beginR
أَبُولُونْيُوسُ

أَبُولُونْيُوسُ الْبَرْجِيُّ — وَهُوَ مِنْ أَهْلِ بَرْجَةَ — عَالِمٌ بِالْهَيْئَةِ، وَصَنَّفَ كِتَابًا فِي الْمَخْرُوطَاتِ سَمَّاهُ «الْمَخْرُوطَاتُ الْأَرْبَعَةَ»، وَكَانَ ذَٰلِكَ فِي ثَمَانِيَةِ أَجْزَاءٍ، وَكَانَ يُعْرَفُ بِالْفَضْلِ فِي عِلْمِ الْهَيْئَةِ، وَقَدْ تَرْجَمَتْ كُتُبُهُ إِلَى اللُّغَةِ الْعَرَبِيَّةِ وَاشْتَهَرَتْ عِنْدَ الْعُلَمَاءِ.
\endR
\end{arabiccol}
\hfill
\begin{englishcol}
\textbf{Apollonius the Pergean} --- he is from the people of Perge --- was a scholar in geometry, and composed a book on conic sections which he called ``The Four Conics.'' That was in eight parts. He was known for excellence in the science of geometry, and his books were translated into the Arabic language and became celebrated among the learned.

\cnote{Apollonius of Perge (c.~262--190 BCE) wrote his \textit{Conics} in eight books, of which only the first seven survive --- four in Greek and three in Arabic translation (the eighth is lost). The Arabic version, made by the Banū Mūsā in the 9th century, preserves Books V--VII, which are not extant in Greek. This translation was crucial for the Islamic mathematical tradition and was later transmitted to Europe.}
\end{englishcol}

\cnote{Diophantus of Alexandria (fl.~c.~250 CE) is here correctly identified with algebra (\textit{al-jabr}), reflecting the Arabic tradition's understanding of his work as foundational for algebraic methods. His \textit{Arithmetica}, in 13 books (of which only six survive in Greek, with four additional books in Arabic), deals with determinate and indeterminate equations. The Arabic version, discovered in the 20th century, preserves books IV--VII and differs from the Greek text.}
\end{englishcol}

\noindent
\begin{arabiccol}
\beginR
بَرَاهْمَجُبْتَ

بَرَاهْمَجُبْتَ — وَهُوَ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْهِنْدِ — عَالِمٌ بِعِلْمِ النُّجُومِ وَالْحِسَابِ، وَصَنَّفَ كِتَابًا فِي الْحِسَابِ وَالْفَلَكِ سَمَّاهُ «السِّدْهَانْتَ»، وَكَانَ يَعْرِفُ بِالْفَضْلِ فِي عِلْمِ النُّجُومِ، وَكَانَ يَتَكَلَّمُ فِي حِسَابِ الْهِنْدِ وَطُرُقِهِمْ فِي الْعَدَدِ، وَقَدْ نُقِلَ عَنْهُ أَشْيَاءُ كَثِيرَةٌ فِي عِلْمِ الْفَلَكِ.
\endR
\end{arabiccol}
\hfill
\begin{englishcol}
\textbf{Brahmagupta} --- he is from the people of India --- was a scholar in the science of the stars and arithmetic, and composed a book on arithmetic and astronomy which he called the \textit{Siddhānta}. He was known for excellence in the science of the stars, and discoursed on Indian arithmetic and their methods in number. Many things were transmitted from him on the science of astronomy.

\cnote{Brahmagupta (c.~598--668 CE) was one of the most important Indian mathematicians and astronomers. His \textit{Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta} (628 CE) was a major source for the Arabic astronomical tradition. The \textit{Sindhind} (\textit{Siddhānta}) translated into Arabic under al-Maʾmūn (c.~770 CE), probably via al-Fazārī and Yaʿqūb ibn Ṭāriq, was largely based on Brahmagupta's work. Arabic sources frequently credit Indian mathematicians with the invention of the decimal positional system.}
\end{englishcol}

\par\vspace{0.5em}

\cnote{Varāhamihira (505--587 CE) was a renowned Indian astronomer, mathematician, and astrologer. His \textit{Pañcasiddhāntikā} (``Five Treatises'') summarizes five earlier astronomical systems. The Arabic rendering ``Banjah'' reflects the Persian pronunciation. His works on astrology (\textit{horā}) were influential in both the Indian and Islamic traditions, and he also wrote on mathematics and natural sciences.}

\subsection{Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī (\textarabic{الْخْوَارِزْمِيُّ})}

\noindent
\begin{arabiccol}
\beginR
مُحَمَّدُ بْنُ مُوسَى الْخْوَارِزْمِيُّ

مُحَمَّدُ بْنُ مُوسَى الْخْوَارِزْمِيُّ — وَكَانَ مِنْ أَهْلِ خُوَارِزْمَ — كَانَ فِي دِيوَانِ الْعَدْلِ فِي زَمَنِ الْمَأْمُونِ، وَصَنَّفَ كِتَابًا فِي الْجَبْرِ وَالْمُقَابَلَةِ وَفِيهِ الْحِسَابُ الْهِنْدِيُّ، وَصَنَّفَ كِتَابًا فِي الْجَبْرِ سَمَّاهُ «الْجَبْرَ وَالْمُقَابَلَةَ»، وَكَانَ أَوَّلَ مَنْ صَنَّفَ فِي ذَلِكَ، وَصَنَّفَ كِتَابَ الْجَمْعِ وَالتَّفْرِيقِ، وَكَانَ يُعْرَفُ بِالْفَضْلِ فِي الْحِسَابِ وَالْعِلْمِ الْفَلَكِيِّ.
\endR
\end{arabiccol}
\hfill
\begin{englishcol}
\textbf{Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī} --- he was from the people of Khwārizm --- was in the \textit{Dīwān al-ʿAdl} in the time of al-Maʾmūn, and composed a book on algebra and equations in which is the Indian arithmetic. He composed a book on algebra which he called \textit{al-Jabr wa-l-Muqābala}, and he was the first to compose on that. He composed the book of addition and subtraction, and was known for excellence in arithmetic and astronomical science.

\cnote{al-Khwārizmī (c.~780--850 CE) is one of the most important figures in the history of mathematics. His \textit{Kitāb al-Jabr wa-l-Muqābala} (c.~820 CE) gave its name to the discipline of algebra. The ``Indian arithmetic'' refers to his work on the Hindu-Arabic numeral system, which was instrumental in spreading decimal positional notation to the Islamic world and eventually to Europe (via Latin translations in 12th-century Spain). The term ``algorithm'' derives from a Latin corruption of his name \textit{al-Khwārizmī}.}
\end{englishcol}

%% THABIT IBN QURRA

\subsection{Thābit ibn Qurra (\textarabic{ثَابِتُ بْنُ قُرَّةَ})}

\noindent
\begin{arabiccol}
\beginR
ثَابِتُ بْنُ قُرَّةَ

ثَابِتُ بْنُ قُرَّةَ بْنِ مَارِينُسَ الْحَرَّانِيُّ، كَانَ مِنْ أَهْلِ حَرَّانَ، وَكَانَ عَالِمًا بِالْعُلُومِ الْإِنسَانِيَّةِ وَالرِّيَاضِيَّةِ، وَصَنَّفَ كُتُبًا كَثِيرَةً فِي الْعِلْمِ الطَّبِيعِيِّ وَالْعِلْمِ الْإِلَهِيِّ وَالْهَيْئَةِ، وَقَدْ نَقَّحَ كِتَابَ أُقْلِيدَسَ وَكِتَابَ الْمَجِسْطِيِّ، وَكَانَ حَسَّانُ الْقَوْلِ فِي النَّظَرِيَّاتِ، وَكَانَ مِنْ أَفْضَلِ مَنْ كَانَ فِي وَقْتِهِ فِي عِلْمِ الْهَيْئَةِ.
\endR
\end{arabiccol}
\hfill
\begin{englishcol}
\textbf{Thābit ibn Qurra ibn Mārīnus al-Ḥarrānī:} he was from the people of Ḥarrān, and was a scholar in the human and mathematical sciences. He composed many books on natural science, divine science, and geometry. He revised the book of Euclid and the book of the \textit{Almagest}, and was eloquent in his discourse on theories. He was among the most excellent of those in his time in the science of geometry.

\cnote{Thābit ibn Qurra (836--901 CE) was a polymath from Ḥarrān who made fundamental contributions to mathematics, astronomy, and philosophy. His revision (\textit{taṣḥīḥ}) of Euclid's \textit{Elements} and Ptolemy's \textit{Almagest} was of major importance. In mathematics, he extended the theory of amicable numbers, worked on spherical trigonometry, and made contributions to the foundations of integral calculus. He was a member of the famous Banū Mūsā circle in Baghdad.}
\end{englishcol}

\subsection{al-Karajī (\textarabic{الْكَرَجِيُّ})}

\begin{arabiccol}
\beginR
أَبُو بَكْرٍ الْكَرَجِيُّ

أَبُو بَكْرٍ مُحَمَّدُ بْنُ الْحُسَيْنِ الْكَرَجِيُّ — أَوْ الْكَرْخِيُّ — كَانَ عَالِمًا بِالْحِسَابِ وَالْجَبْرِ، وَصَنَّفَ كِتَابًا فِي الْكَافِي فِي الْحِسَابِ، وَصَنَّفَ كِتَابًا فِي الْبَدْءِ فِي الْجَبْرِ، وَكَانَ يَتَكَلَّمُ فِي الْخُطُوطِ الْمُنْحَنِيَّةِ وَفِي الْجَبْرِ بِطَرِيقَةِ الْهِنْدِ، وَقَدْ نُقِلَ عَنْهُ فِي عِلْمِ الْحِسَابِ أَشْيَاءُ كَثِيرَةٌ.
\endR
\end{arabiccol}
\hfill
\begin{englishcol}
\textbf{Abū Bakr Muḥammad ibn al-Ḥusayn al-Karajī} --- or al-Karkhī --- was a scholar in arithmetic and algebra, and composed a book called \textit{al-Kāfī fī'l-Ḥisāb} and a book called \textit{al-Badīʿ fī'l-Jabr}. He discoursed on curved lines and on algebra in the Indian manner, and many things were transmitted from him on the science of arithmetic.

\cnote{al-Karajī (c.~953--1029 CE) was a Persian mathematician and engineer who worked in Baghdad. His \textit{al-Kāfī fī'l-Ḥisāb} (``Sufficient on Arithmetic'') and \textit{al-Badīʿ fī'l-Ḥisāb} (``The Marvellous in Arithmetic'') are major works. He was the first to systematically develop the algebra of polynomials, including rules for their multiplication, division, and extraction of roots. His work on curved lines (\textit{al-khuṭūṭ al-munḥaniyya}) contributed to the prehistory of analytic geometry.}
\end{englishcol}

\subsection{ʿUmar al-Khayyām (\textarabic{عُمَرُ الْخَيَّامُ})}

He composed a book on algebra that includes the solution of equations by geometric means. He was known for excellence in the science of the stars, and composed the Jalālī calendar. He was among the most excellent of those who discoursed on algebra and equations.

\cnote{ʿUmar Khayyām (1048--1131 CE) is most famous in the West as a poet (the \textit{Rubáiyát}), but was primarily a mathematician and astronomer. His \textit{Kitāb fī'l-Jabr wa-l-Muqābala} (c.~1070) classified cubic equations into 25 types and provided geometric solutions using conic sections --- a method not surpassed until the 16th century. The Jalālī calendar (1079 CE), commissioned by Sultan Jalāl al-Dīn Malikshāh, was more accurate than the later Gregorian calendar.}
\end{englishcol}

\beginR
نَصِيرُ الدِّينِ الطُّوسِيُّ

نَصِيرُ الدِّينِ أَبُو جَعْفَرٍ مُحَمَّدُ بْنُ مُحَمَّدِ بْنِ الْحَسَنِ الطُّوسِيُّ، كَانَ عَالِمًا بِالْعُلُومِ كُلِّهَا، وَصَنَّفَ كُتُبًا كَثِيرَةً فِي الْهَيْئَةِ وَالْفَلَكِ وَالْمَنْطِقِ، وَصَنَّفَ كِتَابَ الْإِشْرَافِ فِي الْهَيْئَةِ، وَنَقَّحَ الْمَجِسْطِيَّ وَأَخْرَجَ عَلَيْهِ التَّحْرِيرَ، وَكَانَ يَعْرِفُ بِالْكَمَالِ فِي عِلْمِ الْهَيْئَةِ، وَبَنَى الْمَرْصَدَ فِي مَرَاغَةَ.
\endR
\end{arabiccol}
\hfill
\begin{englishcol}
\textbf{Naṣīr al-Dīn Abū Jaʿfar Muḥammad ibn Muḥammad ibn al-Ḥasan al-Ṭūsī:} was a scholar in all the sciences, and composed many books on geometry, astronomy, and logic. He composed the \textit{Kitāb al-Ishrāf} on geometry, and revised the \textit{Almagest} and produced the \textit{Taḥrīr} on it. He was known for perfection in the science of geometry, and built the observatory at Maragha.

\cnote{Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī (1201--1274 CE) was one of the most influential scientists in the Islamic tradition. His \textit{Taḥrīr al-Majisṭī} (``Recension of the Almagest'') became the standard version of Ptolemy's work. His \textit{al-Ishrāf} is a major work on mathematics. The Maragha observatory, established under Hülegü Khan c.~1259, was the most advanced astronomical institution of its time. His \textit{Sharḥ al-Ishārāt} was a landmark in philosophical theology. In trigonometry, he was the first to treat it as an independent mathematical discipline.}
\end{englishcol}

\par\vspace{0.5em}

\subsection{Jamshīd al-Kāshī (\textarabic{جَمْشِيدُ الْكَاشِيُّ})}

\noindent
\begin{arabiccol}
\beginR
جَمْشِيدُ بْنُ مَسْعُودٍ الْكَاشِيُّ

جَمْشِيدُ بْنُ مَسْعُودٍ الْكَاشِيُّ — وَكَانَ مِنْ أَهْلِ كَاشَانَ — كَانَ فِي زَمَنِ أُلُوجَايْ بْنِ هُلَاكُو، وَكَانَ عَالِمًا بِالْحِسَابِ وَالْهَيْئَةِ، وَصَنَّفَ كِتَابَ الْمَحْتَضَرِ فِي الْحِسَابِ، وَصَنَّفَ كِتَابًا فِي الْهَيْئَةِ سَمَّاهُ «نُزْهَةَ الْحَدَائِقِ»، وَكَانَ يَتَكَلَّمُ فِي الْعَدَدِ وَالْكَسْرِ، وَكَانَ مِنْ أَفْضَلِ مَنْ تَكَلَّمَ فِي الْحِسَابِ.
\endR
\end{arabiccol}
\hfill
\begin{englishcol}
\textbf{Jamshīd ibn Masʿūd al-Kāshī} --- he was from the people of Kāshān --- was in the time of Uljaytū ibn Hülegü, and was a scholar in arithmetic and geometry. He composed the \textit{Kitāb al-Muḥtaḍar} on arithmetic, and composed a book on geometry which he called \textit{Nuzhat al-Ḥadāʾiq}. He discoursed on number and fractions, and was among the most excellent of those who discoursed on arithmetic.

\cnote{Jamshīd al-Kāshī (c.~1380--1429 CE) was the greatest mathematician of the Islamic world in the late medieval period. His \textit{Miftāḥ al-Ḥisāb} (``Key of Arithmetic'') systematized arithmetic and included the calculation of \pi to 16 decimal places. His \textit{Nuzhat al-Ḥadāʾiq} contains the ``Khāyām--al-Kāshī rule'' for approximating roots, equivalent to what is now known as Horner's method. He worked at the observatory in Samarqand under Ulugh Beg.}
\end{englishcol}

\subsection{Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna) (\textarabic{ابْنُ سِينَا})}

\noindent
\begin{arabiccol}
\beginR
أَبُو عَلِيٍّ بْنُ سِينَا

أَبُو عَلِيٍّ الْحُسَيْنُ بْنُ عَبْدِ اللَّهِ بْنِ سِينَا، كَانَ مِنْ أَهْلِ بُخَارَا، وَكَانَ عَالِمًا بِالطِّبِّ وَالْفَلْسَفَةِ وَالْمَنْطِقِ، وَصَنَّفَ كِتَابَ الشِّفَاءِ فِي الْمَنْطِقِ وَالطَّبِيعِيَّاتِ وَالْإِلَهِيَّاتِ، وَصَنَّفَ الْقَانُونَ فِي الطِّبِّ، وَكَانَ مِنْ أَفْضَلِ مَنْ تَكَلَّمَ فِي الْعُلُومِ، وَكَانَ يَعْرِفُ بِالْكَمَالِ فِي الطِّبِّ وَالْفَلْسَفَةِ.
\endR
\end{arabiccol}
\hfill
\begin{englishcol}
\textbf{Abū ʿAlī al-Ḥusayn ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Sīnā:} he was from the people of Bukhara, and was a scholar in medicine, philosophy, and logic. He composed the \textit{Kitāb al-Shifāʾ} on logic, natural philosophy, and metaphysics; and composed the \textit{Qānūn} on medicine. He was among the most excellent of those who discoursed on the sciences, and was known for perfection in medicine and philosophy.

\cnote{Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna, 980--1037 CE) is the most celebrated philosopher-physician in the Islamic tradition. The \textit{Kitāb al-Shifāʾ} (Book of Healing) is his encyclopedic masterwork, and the \textit{Qānūn fī'l-Ṭibb} (Canon of Medicine) remained the standard medical textbook in both the Islamic world and Europe for over six centuries. His influence on Western scholastic philosophy was immense, especially through Latin translations by Gerard of Cremona and others.}
\end{englishcol}

\subsection{al-Bīrūnī (\textarabic{الْبِيرُونِيُّ})}

\noindent
\begin{arabiccol}
\beginR
أَبُو الرَّيْحَانِ الْبِيرُونِيُّ

أَبُو الرَّيْحَانِ مُحَمَّدُ بْنُ أَحْمَدَ الْبِيرُونِيُّ، كَانَ مِنْ أَهْلِ خُوَارِزْمَ، وَكَانَ عَالِمًا بِالْفَلَكِ وَالْهَيْئَةِ وَالْأَرْضِ، وَصَنَّفَ كِتَابَ الْآثَارِ الْبَاقِيَةِ، وَصَنَّفَ كِتَابًا فِي مَعْرِفَةِ الْهَيْئَةِ، وَكَانَ يَتَكَلَّمُ فِي جَمِيعِ الْعُلُومِ، وَكَانَ مِنْ أَفْضَلِ الْعُلَمَاءِ فِي زَمَنِهِ، وَقَدْ رَحَلَ إِلَى الْهِنْدِ وَعَرَفَ عُلُومَهَا.
\endR
\end{arabiccol}
\hfill
\begin{englishcol}
\textbf{Abū al-Rayḥān Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad al-Bīrūnī:} he was from the people of Khwārizm, and was a scholar in astronomy, geometry, and geography. He composed the \textit{Kitāb al-Āthār al-Bāqiya} (Chronology of Ancient Nations), and composed a book on the knowledge of geometry. He discoursed on all the sciences, and was among the most excellent of the learned in his time. He travelled to India and knew its sciences.

\cnote{al-Bīrūnī (973--1048 CE) was a towering polymath who made contributions to mathematics, astronomy, physics, geography, and anthropology. His \textit{al-Āthār al-Bāqiya} is a comparative study of calendars and chronological systems. His \textit{Kitāb fī Taḥdīd Nihāyat al-Amākin} (``The Determination of the Coordinates of Places'') advanced mathematical geography. His study of Indian science, based on first-hand knowledge, is recorded in his \textit{Taḥqīq mā li-l-Hind}. He collaborated with Ibn Sīnā and is considered the founder of Indology.}
\end{englishcol}
