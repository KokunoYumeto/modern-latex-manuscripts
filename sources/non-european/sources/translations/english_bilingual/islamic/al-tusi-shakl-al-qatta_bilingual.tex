\section*{Editorial Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Editorial Preface}

\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{rulecolor}{white}{%
\parbox{0.85\textwidth}{%
\small This bilingual edition presents \textbf{Nasir al-Din al-Tusi's} (1201--1274)
\textit{Kitab Shakl al-Qatta'} (Treatise on the Quadrilateral), one of the most
important works in the history of trigonometry. Composed originally in Persian
and later translated by the author into Arabic at the request of his students,
the treatise provides the first systematic treatment of plane and spherical
trigonometry independent of its astronomical applications.

\vspace{0.3em}
The \textbf{left column} throughout presents the \textbf{Arabic original text};
the \textbf{right column} gives the \textbf{English translation}.
Mathematical notation is rendered in modern form alongside the original
Arabic terminology, which is preserved in footnotes.

\vspace{0.3em}
This edition is based on the critical edition published by
\textit{Alexandre Pacha Caratheodory} in Constantinople (Istanbul), 1891,
under the authority of the Ottoman Ministry of Public Instruction.
}%
}
\end{center}

As to what follows: I had composed, in years past, a treatise containing all

work with the assistance of the Most High, the All-Powerful.

\section*{Structure of the Treatise / \ar{تَرْتِيبُ الْكِتَابِ}}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Structure of the Treatise}

\noindent This treatise is divided into \textbf{five books}, each containing
several propositions and chapters, as follows:

\begin{center}
\begin{longtable}{lp{8cm}l}
\toprule
\textbf{Book} & \textbf{Contents} & \textbf{Arabic} \\
\midrule
\endhead

\textbf{Book I} & On compound ratios and their rules, in fourteen propositions. & \ar{فِي الْأُصُولِ الْمُرَكَّبَةِ} \\
\addlinespace[0.2cm]

\textbf{Book II} & On the plane quadrilateral figure and the ratios found therein, in eleven chapters. & \ar{فِي شَكْلِ الْقِطَّاعِ الْمُسَطَّحِ} \\
\addlinespace[0.2cm]

\textbf{Book III} & Introduction to the theory of the spherical quadrilateral, in three chapters. & \ar{فِي الْقِطَّاعِ الْكُرَوِيِّ} \\
\addlinespace[0.2cm]

\textbf{Book IV} & On the spherical quadrilateral and the ratios found therein, in five chapters. & \ar{فِي أَنْوَاعِ الْقِطَّاعِ الْكُرَوِيِّ} \\
\addlinespace[0.2cm]

\textbf{Book V} & Methods replacing the theory of the quadrilateral for computing arcs of great circles, in seven chapters. & \ar{فِي الْأَضْرُبِ الْبَدِيلَةِ} \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}
\end{center}

\transnote{The Arabic terms are given in the rightmost column. For key
technical terms, see the Glossary at the end of this edition.}

%% BOOK I -- COMPOUND RATIOS

\section{Book I: On Compound Ratios / \ar{الْبَابُ الْأَوَّلُ: فِي الْأُصُولِ الْمُرَكَّبَةِ}}
\label{sec:book1}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Book I: On Compound Ratios}

\subsection*{General Rule / \ar{الْقَاعِدَةُ الْعَامَّةُ}}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{General Rule}

\bilingualblock{%
وَنَقُولُ: إِنَّ أَصْلَ الْمَعْنَى فِي الْقَوْلِ إِنَّ هٰذَا النِّسْبَةَ مُرَكَّبَةٌ مِنْ هٰذَيْنِ النِّسْبَتَيْنِ أَوْ هٰذَا النِّسْبَةَ مُفَرَّقَةٌ إِلَى هٰذَيْنِ النِّسْبَتَيْنِ: أَنَّ النِّسْبَةَ الْمُرَكَّبَةَ نِسْبَتَانِ مَفْرُوقَتَانِ مِنْ نِسْبَةٍ، فَإِذَا وُضِعَتْ إِحْدَاهُمَا فِي الْأُخْرَى عَلَى التَّكْرَارِ حَصَلَ نِسْبَةٌ مَعْيُنَةٌ
}{%
We say: The basis of the meaning in saying that ``this ratio is composed of
these two ratios,'' or that ``this ratio is decomposed into these two ratios,''
is that the compound ratio consists of two ratios separated from one ratio, so
that when one is placed in the other by repetition, a certain ratio is obtained.
}

\bilingualblock{%
وَأَمَّا التَّفْرِيقُ: فَهُوَ أَنْ تَكُونَ النِّسْبَةُ مَفْرُوقَةً إِلَى نِسْبَتَيْنِ فَيَكُونُ كُلُّ وَاحِدَةٍ مِنْهُمَا بِمَنْزِلَةِ الْجُزْءِ مِنَ الْكُلِّ
}{%
As for decomposition: it is that the ratio be decomposed into two ratios, each
of which is in the position of a part of the whole.
}

\transnote{Al-Tusi begins with the Euclidean theory of compound ratios (Book VI
of the Elements), developing it into a systematic algebraic tool. The
``quantity'' (\textit{qadr}) of a ratio is the number that the antecedent
contains of the consequent.}

%% ========================================================================
%% PROPOSITION I
%% ========================================================================
\subsection{Proposition I / \ar{الْمَسْأَلَةُ الْأُولَى}}
\label{prop:1}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Proposition I}

\bilingualblock{%
فَإِذَا وُجِدَ هٰذَا فَلْنَقُلْ: أَيُّ نِسْبَةٍ كَانَتْ لِثَلَاثَةِ مُتَجَانِسَاتٍ إِلَى إِحْدَاهُنَّ فَهِيَ مُرَكَّبَةٌ مِنْ نِسْبَتِهِ إِلَى الثَّالِثَةِ وَنِسْبَةِ تِلْكَ الثَّالِثَةِ إِلَى الْأُولَى
}{%
\textbf{Proposition.} Given three homogeneous quantities, the ratio of any
one of these three quantities to a second is composed of the ratio of the
first to the third and of that of the third to the second.
}

\bilingualblock{%
فَلْيَكُنْ ثَلَاثَةُ مُتَجَانِسَاتٍ أ وَ ب وَ ج، فَأَقُولُ: إِنَّ نِسْبَةَ أ إِلَى ب تُسَاوِي نِسْبَةَ أ إِلَى ج فِي نِسْبَةِ ج إِلَى ب، وَنِسْبَةَ أ إِلَى ج تُسَاوِي نِسْبَةَ أ إِلَى ب فِي نِسْبَةِ ب إِلَى ج، وَنِسْبَةَ ب إِلَى ج تُسَاوِي نِسْبَةَ ب إِلَى أ فِي نِسْبَةِ أ إِلَى ج، وَهَكَذَا
}{%
Let there be three homogeneous quantities A, B, C. I say that the ratio of A
to B is composed of the ratio of A to C and the ratio of C to B; and the
ratio of A to C is composed of the ratio of A to B and the ratio of B to C;
and the ratio of B to C is composed of the ratio of B to A and the ratio of A
to C; and so on. In modern notation:
\[
\frac{A}{B} = \frac{A}{C} \times \frac{C}{B}, \quad
\frac{A}{C} = \frac{A}{B} \times \frac{B}{C}, \quad
\frac{B}{C} = \frac{B}{A} \times \frac{A}{C}
\]
}

\vspace{0.3cm}

\bilingualblock{%
فَلْنَفْرِضْ أَنَّ وَاحِدَةً تَقِيسُ هٰذِهِ الْقِدْرَ، فَلْتَقِسْ د كَمَا أَقَاسَتْ أ ج، وَتَقِسْ ط كَمَا أَقَاسَتْ ج ب، وَتَقِيسَ هَـٰ كَمَا أَقَاسَتْ أ ب، فَتَكُونُ د كَمِيَّةَ نِسْبَةِ أ إِلَى ج، وَط كَمِيَّةَ نِسْبَةِ ج إِلَى ب، وَهَـٰ كَمِيَّةَ نِسْبَةِ أ إِلَى ب
}{%
\textit{Demonstration.} Let us suppose a unit that measures these quantities.
Let it measure D as A measures C; let it measure T as C measures B; let it
measure H as A measures B. Then D is the \textit{quantity} of the ratio
$\frac{A}{C}$; T is the \textit{quantity} of the ratio $\frac{C}{B}$; and H is
the \textit{quantity} of the ratio $\frac{A}{B}$.
}

\transnote{The ``quantity'' (\textit{qadr} \ar{قَدْر}) of a ratio is the
number that the unit measures in the same way that the antecedent measures
the consequent. The ``same name'' (\textit{ism mushtarak}) of a ratio is the
number that is its very quantity. Al-Tusi here establishes the fundamental
property that underlies all subsequent trigonometric identities.}

\subsection{Proposition II / \ar{الْمَسْأَلَةُ الثَّانِيَةُ}}
\label{prop:2}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Proposition II}

\bilingualblock{%
وَنِسْبَةُ أَحَدِ الثَّلَاثَةِ أَوْ لَاَخَرِ كَانَتْ مِنْهَا إِلَى الثَّالِثِ فَنِسْبَتُهُ إِلَى الثَّالِثِ فِي نِسْبَةِ الثَّالِثِ إِلَى ذٰلِكَ الْآخَرِ
}{%
\textbf{Proposition.} The ratio of any one of the three quantities A, B, C to
any other is composed of the ratio of the first to the third and of that of
the third to the second.

That is:
\[
\frac{A}{B} = \frac{A}{C} \times \frac{C}{B} = \frac{C}{B} \times \frac{A}{C}
\]
for the composition of ratios is like the multiplication of numbers; one may
transpose the factors.
}

\ornament

%% ========================================================================
%% PROPOSITIONS III--XIV SUMMARY
%% ========================================================================
\subsection{Summary of Propositions III--XIV / \ar{خُلَاصَةُ الْمَسَائِلِ ٣--١٤}}
\label{sec:prop3-14}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Summary: Propositions III--XIV}

\noindent The remaining twelve propositions of Book I develop the algebra of
compound ratios in full generality:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item[\textbf{III.}] Any ratio composed of two ratios can also be composed of
two other ratios derived from these by means of homogeneous quantities taken
from the first and second ratios.
\item[\textbf{IV.}] If any quantity measures two others, the ratio of these two
quantities to each other is equal to the ratio composed of the inverse of the
first ratio with the second ratio.
\item[\textbf{V.}] The ratio composed of two ratios is also composed of the
inverse of the first with the inverse of the second.
\item[\textbf{VI.}] Any compound ratio can be decomposed into simple ratios in
two ways: either as fractions with numerator unity, or as expressions with
denominator unity.
\item[\textbf{VII.}] If one has six quantities whose compound ratios give rise
to other compound ratios, then to decompose any compound ratio it suffices to
know the quantities of a single column.
\item[\textbf{VIII.}] Any compound ratio gives rise to 9 other compound ratios
and to 18 expressions of component ratios, or with their inverses 36
expressions.
\item[\textbf{IX.}] Two ways of decomposing a compound ratio: by preceding the
two terms of the second ratio, or by making them follow the two terms of the
first ratio.
\item[\textbf{X.}] The count can be extended to 72 expressions.
\item[\textbf{XI.}] If two of six quantities, each taken in one of the two
members, are equal, the 4 others are in proportion.
\item[\textbf{XII.}] If the two equal quantities belong to the same member, the
other 4 are not proportional.
\item[\textbf{XIII.}] Any simple ratio can be considered as composed if one
multiplies it by an identity ratio, e.g. if $\frac{A}{B} = \frac{C}{D}$, then
also $\frac{A}{B} = \frac{C}{D} \times \frac{D}{D}$.
\item[\textbf{XIV.}] Any simple identity ratio equals any ratio multiplied by
its inverse: $\frac{A}{A} = \frac{X}{X} \times \frac{X}{X}$.
\end{enumerate}

\transnote{These propositions constitute a complete algebraic theory of ratios,
far surpassing Euclid's treatment in generality and systematic power. They form
the foundation upon which al-Tusi builds the trigonometric theorems of the
later books.}

This figure is called the \textbf{figure of secants} (\textit{shakl
al-qa\d{t}\d{t}a`}) because it is generated from four intersecting lines,

}{%

}

\transnote{Hussam al-Din al-Salar (d. c. 1250), author of an earlier treatise

from 9 to 12 figures, noting that some geometers extend the count to 48,
which reduce four-by-four to the twelve fundamental configurations.}

\section{Book III: Introduction to the Spherical Quadrilateral / \ar{الْبَابُ الثَّالِثُ: فِي تَقْدِمَةِ الْقِطَّاعِ الْكُرَوِيِّ}}

\subsection*{On Chords, Arcs, and Sines / \ar{فِي الْأَوْتَارِ وَالْأَقْوَاسِ وَالْجُيُوبِ}}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{On Chords, Arcs, and Sines}

\bilingualblock{%
وَقَدْ وَجَدَ الْعُلَمَاءُ أَنَّ مُثَلَّثَاتِ الدَّائِرَةِ الْكُرَوِيَّةِ يَنْحَلُّ بِطَرِيقَيْنِ: طَرِيقُ الْأَوْتَارِ وَالْأَقْوَاسِ، وَطَرِيقُ الْأَقْوَاسِ وَالْجُيُوبِ. وَهٰذَا أَخِفُّ وَأَسْهَلُ
}{%
The scholars have found that spherical triangles can be solved by two methods:
the method of \textbf{chords and arcs} (\textit{al-awtar wa-l-aqsus}), and the
method of \textbf{arcs and sines} (\textit{al-aqsus wa-l-juyub}). The latter is
lighter and easier.
}

\bilingualblock{%
وَقَدْ بَيَّنَ أَبُو الرِّيحَانِ أَنَّ جِيبَ قَوْسِ أَرْكَانِ الْمُثَلَّثِ الْمُسَطَّحِ لَهَا نِسْبَةٌ مُسَاوِيَةٌ لِنِسْبَةِ جِيَابِ أَضْلَاعِهِ
}{%
Abu Rayhan (al-Biruni) demonstrated that the sines of the arcs of a plane
triangle have a ratio equal to the ratio of the sines of its sides. This is
the \textbf{Law of Sines}:
}

\begin{proposition}[The Law of Sines for Plane Triangles]
In any plane triangle, the ratio of the sides is equal to the ratio of the
sines of the opposite angles:
\[
\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C}
\]
\end{proposition}

\transnote{This is one of the earliest explicit statements of the Law of Sines.
The term \textit{jayb} (\ar{جَيْب}, lit. ``pocket'' or ``fold of a garment'')
is the Arabic word for sine, derived from the Sanskrit \textit{jiva} through
the mistranslation \textit{jiba} (\ar{جِيبَ}) as \textit{jayb} (pocket) by
Arab scholars reading unvocalized manuscripts.}

\subsection{The Fundamental Problem / \ar{الْمَسْأَلَةُ الْأَصْلِيَّةُ}}
\label{sec:fundamental-problem}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{The Fundamental Problem}

\bilingualblock{%
مَعْلُومُ جَمْعِ قَوْسَيْنِ أَوْ تَفْرِيقِهِمَا وَمَعْلُومُ نِسْبَةِ جِيبِ أَحَدِهِمَا إِلَى جِيبِ الْآخَرِ، فَنُرِيدُ إِيجَادَ الْقَوْسَيْنِ

of one of them to the sine of the other. \textbf{Find:} the two arcs.
}

\bilingualblock{%
وَقَدْ اسْتَنَبَ الْأَمِيرُ أَبُو نَصْرِ بْنُ عِرَاقٍ طَرِيقَةً فِي حَلِّ هٰذِهِ الْمَسْأَلَةِ بِالْأَشْكَالِ الْهَنْدَسِيَّةِ، وَنَحْنُ نُبَيِّنُ طَرِيقَةً فِي حَلِّهَا بِالْجَبْرِ
}{%
The Emir Abu Nasr ibn Iraq devised a method for solving this problem by

\textbf{algebra} (\textit{al-jabr}).
}

\transnote{This fundamental problem --- given $\alpha \pm \beta$ and
$\frac{\sin\alpha}{\sin\beta}$, find $\alpha$ and $\beta$ --- is of central
importance in spherical astronomy. Al-Tusi gives a complete algebraic solution,
reducing it to the solution of a quadratic equation. Abu Nasr ibn `Iraq had
previously solved it geometrically.}

\subsection*{Definition of the Spherical Quadrilateral / \ar{تَعْرِيفُ الْقِطَّاعِ الْكُرَوِيِّ}}

\subsection{Ptolemy's Theorems Applied / \ar{قَوَاعِدُ بَطَلْمَيُوسَ فِي الْقِطَّاعِ الْكُرَوِيِّ}}
\label{sec:ptolemy}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Ptolemy's Theorems}

\bilingualblock{%
وَقَدْ أَثْبَتْنَا أَنَّ الْقَوَاعِدَ الْمُسَطَّحَةَ تُطْبَقُ عَلَى جِيَابِ الْقِطَّاعِ الْكُرَوِيِّ، وَذٰلِكَ أَنَّ جِيَابَ الْأَقْوَاسِ فِي دَائِرَةِ الْكُرَةِ لَهَا نِسَبٌ كَنِسَبِ الْخُطُوطِ الْمُسَطَّحَةِ فِي الدَّائِرَةِ الْمُسْتَوِيَّةِ
}{%
We have proved that the plane theorems apply to the sines of the spherical
quadrilateral; that is, the sines of arcs in the circle of the sphere have
ratios like those of plane lines in a plane circle.
}

\begin{proposition}[The Sine Theorem for Spherical Triangles]
In any spherical triangle, the sines of the sides are in the same ratio as
the sines of the opposite angles:
\[
\frac{\sin a}{\sin A} = \frac{\sin b}{\sin B} = \frac{\sin c}{\sin C}
\]
\end{proposition}

\bilingualblock{%
وَقَدْ خَرَجَ مِنْ هٰذَا الْبَحْثِ سَبْعٌ وَعِشْرُونَ صُورَةً، فَرَدَدْنَاهَا إِلَى ثَلَاثَةَ عَشَرَ صُورَةً وَوَاحِدَةٌ
}{%
From this investigation, twenty-seven cases arise; we have reduced them to
thirteen cases plus one (making fourteen in total), and examined each case
separately with its own demonstration.
}

\transnote{The reduction from 27 cases to 13+1 is a remarkable achievement of
systematic analysis. Al-Tusi shows that the apparently distinct cases are
related through the \textbf{polar triangle} (\textit{al-muthallath al-aqibi}),
a concept he develops fully: if $ABC$ is a spherical triangle, its polar
triangle $A'B'C'$ has sides $a' = 180^\circ - A$, $b' = 180^\circ - B$,
$c' = 180^\circ - C$. This allows conversion between problems involving
angles and problems involving sides.}

\subsection{On Inclinations and the Sine Theorem / \ar{فِي الْمَيْلِ وَقَانُونِ الْجِيَابِ}}
\label{sec:inclinations}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{On Inclinations and the Sine Theorem}

\bilingualblock{%
وَفِي هٰذِهِ الْمُثَلَّثَاتِ يُسَمَّى قَوْسُ ب ج مَيْلَ قَوْسِ أ ج، وَهٰذَا الْمَيْلُ هُوَ حِصَّةُ قَوْسِ أ ج فِي الْمَيْلِ الْكُلِّيِّ لِدَائِرَةِ أ د عَلَى دَائِرَةِ أ هـ الَّذِي يَقِيسُهُ زَاوِيَةُ أ
}{%
In these triangles, arc BC is called the \textbf{inclination} (\textit{mayl}
\ar{مَيْل}) of arc AC; this inclination is the portion of arc AC in the total
inclination of circle AD to circle AH, which is measured by angle A.
}

\bilingualblock{%
وَإِذَا نَسَبْنَا قَوْسَ ب ج إِلَى قَوْسِ أ ب سُمِّيَ (ب ج) مَيْلًا ثَانِيًا. وَهٰذَا مَا يُسَمِّيهِ أَبُو الرِّيحَانِ عَرْضًا، فَقَوْسُ ب ج هُوَ الْمَيْلُ الْأَوَّلُ لِقَوْسِ أ ج، وَالْعَرْضُ لِقَوْسِ أ ب
}{%
If we relate arc BC to arc AB, then BC is called the \textbf{second
inclination}. This is what Abu Rayhan calls the ``breadth'' (\textit{`ard}).
Thus arc BC is the \textbf{first inclination} with respect to arc AC, and the
\textbf{breadth} with respect to arc AB.
}

\bilingualblock{%
وَفِي هٰذَا الِاسْتِعَارَةِ نَقُولُ: إِنَّ نِسَبَ جِيَابِ الْمَيْلِ مُسَاوِيَةٌ لِنِسَبِ جِيَابِ أَقْوَاسِهَا، وَأَنَّ نِسْبَةَ جِيبِ أَيِّ مَيْلٍ إِلَى جِيبِ قَوْسِهِ تُسَاوِي نِسْبَةَ جِيبِ مَيْلٍ آخَرَ إِلَى جِيبِ قَوْسِهِ
}{%
In this manner of speaking, we say: the ratios of the sines of the
inclinations are equal to the ratios of the sines of their arcs, and the ratio
of the sine of any inclination to the sine of its arc is equal to the ratio
of the sine of another inclination to the sine of its arc.
}

\transnote{In modern notation: if $BC$ is the inclination of $AC$, and $LM$ is
the inclination of $AE$, then:
\[\frac{\sin BC}{\sin LM} = \frac{\sin AE}{\sin LA}\]
This is the spherical sine theorem in its most general form.}

%% ========================================================================
%% THE EIGHT DEMONSTRATIONS
%% ========================================================================
\subsection{The Eight Demonstrations of the Sine Theorem / \ar{ثَمَانِيَةُ بُرُوهَانِ لِقَانُونِ الْجِيَابِ}}
\label{sec:sine-theorem}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Eight Demonstrations}

\noindent Al-Tusi provides \textbf{eight demonstrations} of the sine theorem
for spherical triangles:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item[\textbf{1--2.}] Two demonstrations concerning the equality of the ratios
of the sines of the arcs and the sines of the angles.
\item[\textbf{3--4.}] Two demonstrations using the tangents of the angles and
the sines of the arcs.
\item[\textbf{5--6.}] Two demonstrations concerning the ratios of the tangents
and the sines.
\item[\textbf{7--8.}] Two general demonstrations applying to all spherical
triangles, regardless of whether the angles are acute or obtuse.
\end{enumerate}

\transnote{The eight demonstrations represent a tour de force of mathematical
rigor. Each demonstration handles a different configuration of the spherical
triangle. Objections had been raised against the use of tangents because of
their rapid growth beyond 45\degree; al-Tusi refutes these objections and
analyzes the cases where tangent must be replaced by cotangent.}

%% ========================================================================
%% THE FIGURE OF SHADOWS
%% ========================================================================
\subsection{The Figure of Shadows (Tangent Theorem) / \ar{شَكْلُ الزِّلَالِ (قَانُونُ الظِّلَالِ)}}
\label{sec:shadows}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{The Figure of Shadows}

\bilingualblock{%
وَالظِّلُّ الْأَوَّلُ هُوَ الظِّلُّ، وَالظِّلُّ الثَّانِي هُوَ ظِلُّ التَّمَامِ. وَقَدِ اسْتَخْرَجَ أَبُو الْوَفَاءِ هٰذَا الشَّكْلَ وَبَيَّنَ قَوَاعِدَهُ
}{%
The \textbf{first shadow} is the \textbf{tangent}; the \textbf{second shadow}
is the \textbf{cotangent}. Abu al-Wafa' invented this figure and demonstrated
its principles.
}

\bilingualblock{%
وَالظِّلُّ لَيْسَ إِلَّا جِيبُ ارْتِفَاعِ الْقَوْسِ أَوْ مَا يُسَمِّيهِ الْمُتَأَخِّرُونَ جَيْبَ التَّمَامِ. وَالظِّلُّ الْأَوَّلُ هُوَ الظِّلُّ، وَالظِّلُّ الثَّانِي هُوَ ظِلُّ التَّمَامِ
}{%
The \textbf{shadow} of an arc is the sine of its altitude, or what the moderns
call its \textbf{cosine}. The \textbf{first shadow} is the \textbf{tangent};
the \textbf{second shadow} is the \textbf{cotangent}.
}

\transnote{The ``shadow'' (\textit{zill} \ar{ظِلّ}) terminology comes from the
shadow cast by a gnomon on a sundial. The first shadow (tangent) is the ratio
of the gnomon to the horizontal shadow; the second shadow (cotangent) is the
ratio of the gnomon to the vertical shadow. Abu al-Wafa' al-Buzjani (940--998)
was the first to introduce all six trigonometric functions in their modern
form.}

%% APPLICATIONS
%% ========================================================================
\subsection{Applications to Spherical Astronomy / \ar{التَّطْبِيقَاتُ فِي عِلْمِ الْفَلَكِ}}
\label{sec:applications}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Applications to Astronomy}

\bilingualblock{%
وَقَدْ اسْتَخْرَجْنَا بِقَانُونِ الْجِيَابِ وَقَانُونِ الظِّلَالِ جَمِيعَ أَنْوَاعِ حَلِّ الْمُثَلَّثَاتِ الْكُرَوِيَّةِ، وَقَدْ تَعَدَّدَتِ الطُّرُقُ فِي ذٰلِكَ وَاخْتَلَفَتْ
}{%
We have derived, by means of the sine theorem and the tangent theorem, all
types of solutions for spherical triangles; and the methods in this are
multiple and various.
}

\bilingualblock{%
وَأَمَّا مَا يَتَعَلَّقُ بِمَعْرِفَةِ الْأَضْلَاعِ مِنَ الزَّوَايَا الثَّلَاثِ فَلَمْ أَعْرِفْ طَرِيقَةً فِي حَلِّهِ بِالظِّلَالِ
}{%
As for what concerns finding the sides from the three angles [of a spherical
triangle], I do not know a method for solving it by means of tangents.
}

\transnote{Al-Tusi here admits the limitation of his method: given three
angles of a spherical triangle, he cannot find the sides using the tangent
theorem alone. This case requires the polar triangle method: if $A$, $B$, $C$
are the three angles, the sides of the polar triangle are $a' = 180^\circ - A$,
$b' = 180^\circ - B$, $c' = 180^\circ - C$. The problem is thus reduced to
the case of three sides, which al-Tusi can solve. This is one of the earliest
uses of the polar triangle in mathematical history.}

\transnote{The term \textit{jayb} (\ar{جَيْب}) for ``sine'' is one of the
most fascinating etymologies in mathematics. The Sanskrit word \textit{jiva}
(``chord'') was transliterated into Arabic as \textit{jiba} (\ar{جِيبَ},
unvocalized as \ar{جيب}). Later Arabic scholars, reading the unvocalized text,
misinterpreted it as \textit{jayb} (\ar{جَيْب}), meaning ``pocket'' or
``fold of a garment.'' When European scholars translated Arabic texts into
Latin, they rendered \textit{jayb} as \textit{sinus} (``bay'' or ``fold''),
giving us the modern term ``sine.''}

\end{itemize}

\subsection*{The Significance of the Work}

Al-Tusi's \textit{Kitab Shakl al-Qatta'} represents one of the most important
contributions to trigonometry in the medieval Islamic world:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item \textbf{Systematized the theory of compound ratios} (Book I), providing a
complete algebraic foundation for ratio theory that went beyond Euclid's
\textit{Elements}.
\item \textbf{Analyzed the complete quadrilateral figure} (Book II), classifying
all twelve configurations generated by four intersecting lines and deriving
the fundamental proportional relationships.
\item \textbf{Established the foundations of spherical trigonometry} (Books
III--IV), including the sine theorem for spherical triangles and its systematic
application.
\item \textbf{Developed practical computational methods} (Book V) for solving
spherical triangles, which were essential for astronomical calculations.
\end{enumerate}
