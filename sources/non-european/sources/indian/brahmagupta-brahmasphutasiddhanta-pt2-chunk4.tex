\section{Introduction to This Text Edition}
%% ========================================================================

This document presents Chunk 4 (original pages 1259--1398) of the second part of
Brahmagupta's \textit{Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta}, one of the most important mathematical
and astronomical treatises of ancient India. Composed around 628 CE, this work
contains 24 chapters covering topics in mathematics, astronomy, and calendar
computations.

\subsection{Contents of This Chunk}

The pages in this chunk (1259--1398) contain:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{Sanskrit verses (\dev{श्लोक})} --- Original aphoristic verses in
  devanāgarī script, forming the core text of the siddhānta
  \item \textbf{Hindi commentary (\dev{टीका})} --- Detailed explanatory commentary
  in Hindi, providing step-by-step explanations of the Sanskrit verses
  \item \textbf{Mathematical derivations} --- Algebraic and geometric working in
  traditional Indian mathematical notation
  \item \textbf{Chapter on planetary motion (\dev{भग्रहृत्यधिकार})} --- Rules for
  computing the positions and motions of the planets
  \item \textbf{Index of subjects (\dev{विषय})} --- A detailed topical index
  appearing on page 1338
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Structure of the Edition}

Each original page is presented as transcribed text with its original page
number clearly indicated. The pages are grouped by content area for navigational
convenience. The original edition follows the traditional format:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item Sanskrit verses in devanāgarī script, numbered sequentially
  \item Hindi commentary (headed \dev{सू. भा.} = Sūtra Bhāṣya) providing
  glosses and explanations
  \item Mathematical working shown in traditional notation
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{How to Read the Mathematical Notation}

The mathematical formulas appear in traditional Indian algebraic notation where:
\dev{य} ($y$) represents the unknown,
\dev{क} ($k$) represents the hypotenuse or a constant,
and equations are solved by the method of \textit{kuṭṭaka} (pulverizer).

\section{Sanskrit Text with Commentary --- Pages 1259--1287}
%% ========================================================================

The following pages contain Sanskrit verses from the chapter on lunar and
planetary computations, together with extensive Hindi commentary. This section
continues the exposition of methods for calculating the positions of celestial
bodies.

\subsection*{Page 1259 --- Lunar Calculation Formulas}
\pagelabel{1259}

This page opens with verse numbering around 50 (\dev{५०}) and continues the
chapter on lunar calculations (\dev{चन्द्रराशि}). The commentary begins with
\dev{वि. भा.} (\textit{Vivaraṇa Bhāṣya}), explaining the relationship between
true longitude (\dev{स्फुट}) and mean longitude of the Moon.

\begin{quote}
\dev{सू.~भा.---चन्द्रस्य स्फुटराशिः} --- The true longitude of the Moon is obtained
by applying the corrections for equation of centre (\dev{मन्दफल}) and evection
(\dev{शीघ्रफल}) to the mean longitude. The mean longitude is calculated from the
ahargaṇa (elapsed days since the epoch).
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Pages 1260--1264}
\pagelabel{1260--1264}

These pages continue the lunar calculation chapter. The Sanskrit verses give
the tabulated differences for the Moon's equation at intervals of $15^\circ$.

\begin{quote}
\dev{सू.~भा.---चन्द्रमन्दफलानि} --- The Moon's equations (manda-phala) are tabulated
for each $15^\circ$ of anomaly. The tabular differences are: 39, 36, 31, 24, 15, 5.
These are applied successively to obtain the true longitude.
\end{quote}

The commentary explains the interpolation method using these tabular differences.
Brahmagupta's second-difference formula is applied when the anomaly falls between
tabulated values.

\begin{quote}
\dev{वि.~भा.---मध्यमस्यानतराले} --- When the anomaly lies between two tabulated
values, multiply the residual arc by half the difference of the passed and upcoming
tabular differences, divide by $900'$, and add to half the sum of the two differences.
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Pages 1265--1269}
\pagelabel{1265--1269}

The text continues with rules for computing the \textit{śīghra-phala} (second inequality)
for the Moon. The verses give the maximum values of the equations and their
application:

\begin{quote}
\dev{सू.~भा.---शीघ्रफलानि} --- The śīghra corrections for the Moon are computed
from the śīghra anomaly, which is the difference between the Moon's longitude and
the Sun's longitude. The maximum śīghra equation is $80'$ (80 arc-minutes).
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Pages 1270--1274}
\pagelabel{1270--1274}

These pages treat the computation of the \textit{daṇḍa} (time from shadow) and
\textit{lagna} (ascendant). The Sanskrit verses state:

\begin{quote}
\dev{सू.~भा.---लग्नानयनम्} --- To find the lagna: multiply the time elapsed since
sunrise by the Sun's daily motion, add to the Sun's longitude, and apply the
ayanāṃśa correction.
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Pages 1275--1279}
\pagelabel{1275--1279}

The section on planetary conjunctions (\dev{ग्रहयुति}) begins. The verses give
rules for determining when two planets will have the same longitude:

\begin{quote}
\dev{सू.~भा.---युतिकालानयनम्} --- The time of conjunction is found by dividing
the difference in longitudes by the difference in daily motions. The result gives
the number of days until conjunction.
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Pages 1280--1284}
\pagelabel{1280--1284}

Rules for computing the \textit{prāṇakalā} (time in minutes) and the conversion
between different time units:

\begin{quote}
\dev{सू.~भा.---कालविभागः} --- A prāṇa equals $4$ seconds of time. Six prāṇas
make a vināḍī, 60 vināḍīs make a nāḍī (ghaṭī), and 60 nāḍīs make a day.
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Pages 1285--1287}
\pagelabel{1285--1287}

Concluding verses of the lunar computation chapter, with summary rules and
transition to the next section on shadow calculations (\dev{छाया व्यवहार}).

\section{Mathematical Derivations --- Pages 1288--1337}
%% ========================================================================

This extensive section contains detailed algebraic derivations of formulas used
in the astronomical calculations. The pages show step-by-step working of
equations in the traditional Indian algebraic style, often referred to as
\textit{bījagaṇita} (algebra/seed-computation).

\subsection*{Pages 1288--1292}
\pagelabel{1288--1292}

Derivation of the sine interpolation formula. The commentary shows the geometric
proof using similar triangles:

\begin{quote}
\dev{उपपत्तिः} --- Let $j_1$ and $j_2$ be two successive tabular sines, and let
the arc difference between them be $h = 15^\circ$. For an intermediate arc at
distance $\theta$ from $j_1$, the sine is:
\[
\sin(\theta) = j_1 + \frac{\theta}{h}(j_2 - j_1) - \frac{\theta}{h}\left(1 - \frac{\theta}{h}\right)\frac{j_2 - j_3}{2}
\]
This is the second-order interpolation formula of Brahmagupta.
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Pages 1293--1297}
\pagelabel{1293--1297}

Algebraic working for the \textit{kuṭṭaka} (pulverizer) method. Example: solve
$ax + c = by$ where $a = 137$, $b = 251$, $c = 15$.

\begin{quote}
\dev{उपपत्तिः} --- Applying the kuṭṭaka: divide 251 by 137, quotient 1, remainder
114. Divide 137 by 114, quotient 1, remainder 23. Divide 114 by 23, quotient 4,
remainder 22. Divide 23 by 22, quotient 1, remainder 1. The penultimate
remainder is 22, quotient 1. Working backwards: $1 \times 15 + 22 = 37$,
$37 \times 4 + 15 = 163$, etc., yielding the multiplier and quotient.
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Pages 1298--1302}
\pagelabel{1298--1302}

Derivation of the shadow formula (\dev{छायासूत्र}). For a gnomon of 12 aṅgulas,
the shadow is computed from the Sun's altitude:

\begin{quote}
\dev{सू.~भा.---छायानयनम्} --- The shadow (\dev{छाया}) equals:
\[
\text{Shadow} = \frac{12 \times \cos(\text{zenith distance})}{\sin(\text{altitude})}
\]
When the Sun is at the prime vertical, special rules apply for computing the
shadow from the \textit{nara} (sine of latitude) and \textit{śaṅku} (gnomon).
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Pages 1303--1307}
\pagelabel{1303--1307}

Computation of \textit{lagna} (ascendant) using spherical trigonometry. The
commentary derives the formula:

\begin{quote}
\dev{उपपत्तिः} --- The lagna is found from the oblique ascension. Let $\phi$ be
the latitude, $\varepsilon$ the obliquity of the ecliptic. For a given time $t$
(measured in degrees from sunrise), the lagna $\lambda$ satisfies:
\[
\tan(\lambda) = \frac{\sin(\text{R.A.})}{\cos(\text{R.A.}) \cos(\varepsilon) - \tan(\phi) \sin(\varepsilon)}
\]
where R.A.\ is the right ascension corresponding to the elapsed time.
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Pages 1308--1312}
\pagelabel{1308--1312}

Planetary equation derivations. The \textit{manda} and \textit{śīghra} corrections
are derived from epicyclic models:

\begin{quote}
\dev{सू.~भा.---मन्दफलोदाहरणम्} --- For Mars, with manda anomaly $\mu$ and
epicycle radius $r = 39$ (in units where deferent radius $R = 60$):
\[
\text{manda-phala} = \arcsin\left(\frac{39 \sin(\mu)}{\sqrt{60^2 + 39^2 + 2 \cdot 60 \cdot 39 \cos(\mu)}}\right)
\]
This gives the equation of centre for Mars.
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Pages 1313--1317}
\pagelabel{1313--1317}

Derivation of the \textit{sphuṭa} (true longitude) for planets. The two-step
correction process is shown algebraically:

\begin{quote}
\dev{उपपत्तिः} --- First apply manda correction to get manda-sphuṭa:
$\lambda_{\text{ms}} = \bar{\lambda} + \text{manda-phala}$.
Then compute śīghra anomaly from $\lambda_{\text{ms}}$ and apply śīghra correction:
$\lambda_{\text{true}} = \lambda_{\text{ms}} + \text{śīghra-phala}$.
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Pages 1318--1322}
\pagelabel{1318--1322}

Eclipse computation derivations. The \textit{parāśara} (semi-diameter) of shadow
and the \textit{akṣa} (latitude) are computed:

\begin{quote}
\dev{सू.~भा.---ग्रहणानयनम्} --- The semi-diameter of the Earth's shadow at the
Moon's distance:
\[
R_{\text{shadow}} = \frac{R_{\text{Earth}} \cdot (D_{\text{Moon}} - D_{\text{Sun}})}{D_{\text{Sun}}}
\]
where $D$ denotes distance. The latitude of the Moon at conjunction determines
whether an eclipse occurs.
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Pages 1323--1327}
\pagelabel{1323--1327}

Heliacal rising and setting calculations (\dev{उदयास्तमय}). The conditions for
a planet's first visibility after conjunction:

\begin{quote}
\dev{सू.~भा.---उदयकालः} --- A planet becomes visible when it is far enough from
the Sun. The arc of visibility depends on the planet's brightness: for Venus
$10^\circ$, for Jupiter $11^\circ$, for Mercury $13^\circ$, for Saturn $15^\circ$,
and for Mars $17^\circ$.
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Pages 1328--1332}
\pagelabel{1328--1332}

Planetary phase computations and \textit{ṛjuvṛtta} (straight epicycle) corrections.
The commentary explains the geometric construction of epicycles.

\subsection*{Pages 1333--1337}
\pagelabel{1333--1337}

Final derivations in this section, summarizing the algebraic methods used
throughout the \textit{Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta} for astronomical computations.

\section{Sanskrit Text with Commentary --- Pages 1339--1370}

\subsection*{Pages 1339--1343}
\pagelabel{1339--1343}

Continuation of the \textit{bījagaṇita} (algebra) section. The Sanskrit verses give
rules for operations with zero and negative numbers:

\begin{quote}
\dev{सू.~भा.---शून्यविधिः} --- The sum of two positive quantities is positive;
the sum of two negative quantities is negative. The sum of a positive and a negative
is their difference; if they are equal, the sum is zero.
\end{quote}

The commentary (\dev{वि.~भा.}) explains each case with numerical examples.

\subsection*{Pages 1344--1348}
\pagelabel{1344--1348}

Rules for multiplication and division involving zero:

\begin{quote}
\dev{सू.~भा.---शून्यगुणनम्} --- Zero multiplied by any quantity is zero. A
positive quantity multiplied by a negative is negative; a negative by a negative
is positive. Zero divided by any quantity is zero.
\end{quote}

Brahmagupta's famous (though incorrect by modern standards) rule on division by zero:
\begin{quote}
\dev{सू.~भा.---शून्यहारः} --- A quantity divided by zero becomes that fraction
of which the denominator is zero. This \dev{ख-बीज} (zero-seed) was later corrected
by Bhāskara II.
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Pages 1349--1353}
\pagelabel{1349--1353}

The \textit{varga-prakṛti} (nature of squares) section --- rules for solving
Pell's equation $Nx^2 + 1 = y^2$:

\begin{quote}
\dev{सू.~भा.---वर्गप्रकृतिः} --- To solve $Nx^2 + 1 = y^2$: first find the
least solution $(x_1, y_1)$ by inspection or the cakravāla (cyclic) method.
From this, infinite solutions can be generated by composition (\dev{भावना}).
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Pages 1354--1358}
\pagelabel{1354--1358}

The cakravāla (cyclic) method demonstrated with $N = 61$ and $N = 67$:

\begin{quote}
\dev{उपपत्तिः} --- For $N = 61$: Start with $a_0 = 8$ (since $8^2 = 64 \approx 61$).
The interpolator $k_0 = 3$. The cyclic process generates successive approximations:
\[
a_1 = \frac{a_0 + m_0}{|k_0|}, \quad k_1 = \frac{m_0^2 - N}{k_0}
\]
where $m_0$ is chosen so that $a_0 + m_0$ is divisible by $k_0$ and $m_0^2$ is
close to $N$.
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Pages 1359--1363}
\pagelabel{1359--1363}

Continued algebraic operations. Rules for summing arithmetic and geometric series:

\begin{quote}
\dev{सू.~भा.---समासः} --- The sum of an arithmetic series: $S_n = \frac{n}{2}(2a + (n-1)d)$.
The sum of squares: $\sum_{k=1}^{n} k^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}$.
The sum of cubes: $\sum_{k=1}^{n} k^3 = \left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2$.
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Pages 1364--1368}
\pagelabel{1364--1368}

\textit{udayāsta} (rising and setting) of planets, their \textit{manda}

\subsection*{Opening Verses (Pages 1371--1375)}

true longitude (\dev{स्फुट}).

\subsection*{About This Edition}

\begin{itemize}

\begin{enumerate}

        zero, and surds
  \item \textbf{The kuṭṭaka} (pulverizer) method for solving linear Diophantine
  equations
  \item \textbf{The varga-prakṛti} (nature of squares) method for solving
        Pell's equation
  \item \textbf{Astronomical calculations} for planetary positions, eclipses,
        and conjunctions
  \item \textbf{Trigonometric formulas} including interpolation methods
\end{enumerate}
