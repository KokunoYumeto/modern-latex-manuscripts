RESEARCHES ON THE PRIME DIVISORS OF A CLASS
OF FORMULAE OF THE FOURTH DEGREE.
1st Memoir.
In the literary announcements of Göttingen (11 April 1825) one finds the extract of a memoir of
indeterminate analysis which Mr. Gauss presented to the Royal Society of Sciences of that city, but
which has not yet been printed. This memoir is the first of a series of memoirs which the illustrious
author of the Disquisitiones arithmeticae proposes to give on the theory of biquadratic residues,
and its object is to determine the distinctive characters of the prime divisors of the formula x4 −2.
The author establishes in it two extremely elegant theorems which can serve to decide whether a
prime number, divisor of x2 −2, divides or does not divide the preceding formula. Having learned,
in the course of the year just ended, of the cited extract, which contains only the statements of the
two theorems just mentioned and of some auxiliary propositions, I wished to demonstrate on my
side the beautiful theorems discovered by Mr. Gauss. The researches which I made with this aim
led me to find a proof founded on extremely simple considerations and probably entirely different
from that of Mr. Gauss, which appears to require very delicate and rather extensive preliminary
researches. I then applied analogous considerations to other questions and particularly to the search
for the properties which distinguish the prime divisors of the formula αx4 + βx2 + γ; and I thus
arrived at a large number of interesting theorems. The rapid exposition of part of the results to
which these researches have led me is the object of the present memoir. I begin by setting down
some definitions and by stating some theorems very easy to establish and on which we shall have
to rely in what follows.

1.
“If one can assign to the indeterminate x a value such that x4 −A becomes divisible by B, A
will be called a biquadratic residue with respect to B.”
It is easy to see that, if a number A is a biquadratic residue with respect to a number B, or in
other words, if B is a divisor of x4 −A, each prime factor of B will likewise be a divisor of x4 −A,
and reciprocally, that if this condition holds with respect to every prime factor of the number B,
B will itself be a divisor of x4 −A. Thus, when it is a question of assigning all the numbers which
divide the formula x4 −A, one can confine oneself to considering prime numbers.
It is no less evident that, for a number to divide the formula x4 −A, it is necessary that this
number be a divisor of x2 −A. There are therefore only the prime divisors of this latter formula to
examine, that is to say, the prime numbers with respect to which A is a quadratic residue. One may
add that, relative to those among these latter which are of the form 4n + 3, the question presents
no diﬀiculty; for one verifies by a very simple reasoning that every prime number 4n + 3, divisor of
x2 −A, also divides the formula x4 −A.

“p designating a prime number 8n + 1, and having written p = φ2 + ψ2 (where ψ is supposed

It has doubtless been observed that the statements of Theorems I and II are such that only the
root ψ of the even square ψ2 obtained by decomposing p into two squares enters into them. It would
be easy to modify these statements so that they contained only the root φ of the odd square. One
would achieve this by following a procedure entirely similar to that which we have set out in what
precedes.
The results (δ′) and (ϑ′), on which we have relied to establish Theorems I and II, themselves
contain infinitely many interesting theorems analogous to the first of M. Gauss’s theorems. One
deduces, for example, from the statement (ϑ′), on supposing a = 5 in it:
“p denoting a prime number 20n + 1, if one puts p = t2 + 5u2, 5 will or will not be a biquadratic
residue with respect to p, according as u is even or odd; on the contrary, if p denotes a prime number
20n + 9 and one likewise puts p = t2 + 5u2, 5 will or will not be a biquadratic residue with respect
to p, according as u is odd or even.”
The theorems (δ′) and (ϑ′) concern the equations:
t2 −bu2 = ps2,
t2 + au2 = ps2.
By considerations of the same kind one would find analogous results concerning the equations
t2 + bu2 = ps2, t2 −au2 = ps2.
Treating the first of these equations and then putting, to give an example, b = 3, one would
obtain this particular theorem:
“p denoting a prime number 12n + 1, if one puts p = t2 + 3u2, t will be an odd number. This
being so, I say that 3 will or will not be a biquadratic residue with respect to p, according as t is of
the form 12n ± 1 or of this one: 12n ± 5.”
