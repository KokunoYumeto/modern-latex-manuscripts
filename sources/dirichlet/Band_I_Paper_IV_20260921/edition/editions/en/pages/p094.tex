\begin{equation*}\tag{$\vartheta'$}
\begin{minipage}{0.84\textwidth}
If $u$ is even, $a$ is or is not a biquadratic residue with respect
to $p$, according as one has:
\[
\leg{t}{a}=1\quad\text{or}\quad\leg{t}{a}=-1;
\]
if $u$ is odd, $a$ is or is not a biquadratic residue with respect
to $p$, according as one has:
\[
\leg{t}{a}=(-1)^{\frac{a-1}{4}}\quad\text{or}\quad\leg{t}{a}=-(-1)^{\frac{a-1}{4}}.
\]
\end{minipage}
\end{equation*}
% U:p094.u02 | paragraph-start
\par\indent
Let us decompose $p$ into two squares, putting $p=\varphi^2+\psi^2$ ($\psi$ being supposed
even). Substitution of this value in the equation $(\eta')$ gives:
\[
(t+\psi s)(t-\psi s)=\varphi^2s^2-au^2.
\]
We shall now distinguish two cases according as $\varphi$ is or is not divisible by $a$.
% U:p094.u03 | paragraph-start
\par\indent
First case: $\varphi$ is not divisible by $a$.
% U:p094.u04 | paragraph-start
\par\indent
Since $\varphi$ is odd and not divisible by $a$, the greatest common divisor
of $\varphi$ and of $u$, which we shall denote by $m$, will also be odd and not divisible
by $a$. If we put $\varphi=m\varphi'$, $u=mu'$, the preceding equation
changes into this one:
\[
(t+\psi s)(t-\psi s)=m^2(\varphi'^2s^2-au'^2);
\]
$s$ is prime to $au$ and consequently also to $au'$, $\varphi'$ is likewise prime
to $au'$; thus $\varphi's$ and $au'$ have no common divisor. This being so, it follows
from a known theorem (\emph{Theory of Numbers} no. 197) that every odd divisor
$R$ of $s^2\varphi'^2-au'^2$ is such that one has $\leg{R}{a}=1$.
% U:p094.u05 | paragraph-start
\par\indent
Denoting four indeterminates by $E$, $K$, $F$ and $L$, we may replace
the last equation by those which I shall write:
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
t+\psi s=EK,&m^2=EF,\\
t-\psi s=FL,&(s\varphi')^2-au'^2=KL.
\end{array}
\]
One readily ascertains that the odd numbers $E$ and $F$ are relatively
prime. It follows from this that each of them is a square, so that one has:
\[
\leg{E}{a}=1,\quad\leg{F}{a}=1.
\]
