The results we have just obtained stand as do those that
we established in paragraph 4; they still hold even when $p$
cannot be put under the form $t^2-au^2$, and presuppose only that the

indicated towards the end of paragraph 4, and to rely on the auxiliary
proposition discussed there, whose statement was given the generality
necessary for it to serve as a basis, at the same time, for the
demonstration of the general theorem at the end of paragraph 4 and for that of the theorem we
are about to state. These two theorems properly form only one, and
by proceeding as was said in the cited place, that is, by applying to
the equation $t^2\pm Mu^2=p$, where $M$ denotes the product of any number of
distinct prime numbers, considerations analogous to those employed
in this paragraph and in the preceding ones, one will demonstrate
these two theorems simultaneously, or rather one will arrive at a theorem in whose statement
they are united.

\sourceheading{Theorem II.}
% U:p084.u03 | paragraph-start
\par\indent
“$a$ denoting a prime number $4n+1$, and $p$ another prime number
$4n+1$, such that $\leg{a}{p}=1$, if one puts $p=\varphi^2+\psi^2$ (where $\psi$ is supposed even), one
will be able to decide in the following way whether $a$ is or is not a biquadratic
residue with respect to $p$. If $\varphi$ is divisible by $a$, $a$ will or will not be a
biquadratic residue with respect to $p$, according as $a$ is of the form $8n+1$ or of
this one: $8n+5$. If $\varphi$ is not divisible by $a$, one will seek a number $\chi$ such
that $\chi^2\equiv p\md{a}$. This being done, $a$ will or will not be a biquadratic
residue with respect to $p$, according as one has $\leg{\chi(\chi+\psi)}{a}=1$ or
$\leg{\chi(\chi+\psi)}{a}=-1$.”
