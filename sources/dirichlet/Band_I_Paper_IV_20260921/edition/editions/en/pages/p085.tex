\par\indent
In indicating above a method that could serve to establish
theorems I and II in all their generality, we said that, in following this
method, one found oneself obliged to rely on a subsidiary
proposition that appears rather difficult to demonstrate with complete rigour. On
reflecting on this difficulty, I recognized that it was very easy to remedy it by
modifying slightly the procedure outlined above. This modification is
extremely slight and consists in this: instead of considering the equations $t^2-bu^2=p$,
$t^2-au^2=p$, which may fail to be possible although one has $\leg{b}{p}=1$, $\leg{a}{p}=1$,
one must apply considerations of the preceding kind to the more general equations
$t^2-bu^2=ps^2$, $t^2-au^2=ps^2$ (one advantageously replaces the latter
by this one: $t^2+au^2=ps$, which can be treated more simply), which
can always be satisfied when the conditions $\leg{b}{p}=1$, $\leg{a}{p}=1$ hold,
as follows from the beautiful theorem that Mr. \textsc{Legendre} gave for deciding
the possibility or impossibility of the equation $\alpha x^2+\beta y^2=\gamma z^2$ (\emph{Theory of
Numbers}, no. 27).

\par\indent
The demonstration thus modified, besides being entirely rigorous,
has the further advantage of greater simplicity, as any reader who has fully
grasped the spirit of the preceding considerations will be able to judge by developing
this demonstration according to the indication just given.
