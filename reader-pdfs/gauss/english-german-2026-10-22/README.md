contents, the manuscript appendix, the historical editor's closing note and
both imprints. Historical page references retain the source pagination.

Volume II contains the historical title matter, its first five complete papers:

The complete self-reviews of May 12 and September 19, 1808, March 10, 1817, April 11, 1825, and April 23, 1831, follow these five
papers.

Seven complete reviews of other writings follow: March 11, 1809; March 23, 1812; November 3, 1814; November 7, 1816; August 9, 1817; December 19, 1825; and July 9, 1831. They concern a work on irrational radicals, the factor tables of Chernac and Burckhardt, Erchinger’s construction of the regular seventeen-sided polygon, and Seeber’s positive ternary quadratic forms.

The complete manuscript work *Solution of the Congruence X^m−1 ≡ 0*, the first part of Chapter VI of *Analysis of Residues* (Articles 237–252), follows the reviews.

*General Investigations of Congruences*, Chapter VIII of *Analysis of Residues*, contains Articles 330–375 as printed, including the unfinished ending of Article 375. R. Dedekind's complete historical remarks follow. This portion treats congruences between functions, division and common divisors, prime functions, factorization and counting, power transformations and periods, two proofs of quadratic reciprocity, equal roots and divisors, and separation of factors by degree.

*Further Development of the Investigations of Pure Equations* follows in full as printed, with its title, Articles 1–19 and R. Dedekind's historical remarks. It treats proper and improper roots, products and rational expressions formed from their powers, consistent radical choices, periods, division of ratios and angles, indices and least residues, power expansions, trigonometric period expressions, worked numerical examples and the sign ambiguity in quadratic sums.

The fragment *Proof of Some Theorems Concerning the Periods of Classes of Binary Quadratic Forms* contains both theorems, the unfinished ending of the second proof and Stern's historical remark.

The texts marked [I.] and [II.] of *On the Connection between the Number of Classes into Which Binary Quadratic Forms Are Distributed and Their Determinant, First Paper* are each included in full as printed, with their titles and Articles 1–8. The text marked [II.] was presented in 1837. These texts treat counts of integer points in regions, limiting ratios of counts to areas, and mean numbers of representations by binary quadratic forms. Text [I.] includes prime-factor rules, series and infinite products. Text [II.] gives the boundary-square argument, distinguishes approximation of ratios from approximation of differences, and treats counts with congruence and parity conditions.

Fragments III–X follow in full as printed, with their historical editorial notes. They treat representation counts, average and actual numbers of classes, and the distribution of quadratic residues in eighths and twelfths, including the historical tables. The complete historical remarks on [I.]–[X.], signed by R. Dedekind, discuss circle-counting formulas and quoted rules, divisor and representation counts, the series for π/4, prime elimination and mean values, infinite products and the qualification concerning their rigor, trigonometric summations and class numbers, and hyperbolic sectors for positive determinants. They also give the biquadratic-character argument for 2 and the full class-number relations for quadratic residues in eighths and twelfths, with the correspondence tables and both congruence cases.

*Geometrical Aspect of Ternary Forms* follows with its complete geometric definitions and relations, the ellipsoid and tetrahedron notes, the crystallization law, transformation tables and complex projections, and the hexakisoctahedron formulas, value table and two editable diagrams. Schering’s complete historical remarks include the crystal measurements, the quoted theorem on transformations of an indefinite ternary form, its parameter conditions and the account of the transformation table. 

The posthumous texts [I.] and [II.] of *On the Theory of Biquadratic Residues* follow. Text [I.] contains its title and complete Articles 1–19, including all five editable diagrams. Text [II.], *Preparations for the General Theory of Biquadratic Residues*, contains both preparatory sections (1) and (2) and complete Articles 1–12 as printed. They treat oriented lines, winding and boundary conventions, residue classes, decidents and their composition, the residue 1+i, and the point, square and ligature constructions for biquadratic characters. Their originally German prose is rendered in modern German in the German reader; the original German bodies and diagrams are supplied separately.

Text [III.], *Theory of Biquadratic Residues*, follows in full as printed, with its title and complete Articles 1–14, including the joint heading 7–8. It treats least-residue classes, decidents and the stated reciprocity theorem, coordinate bounds, parity sums and all boundary exceptions. Text [IV.] follows in full as printed, Articles 1–5, with its coordinate relations, two bound tables, intensor sums and sign table; its second method ends after the fourth step. The German reader renders the original German in modern German; the historical originals are supplied separately.

Text [V.], *Theory of Biquadratic Residues*, follows in full as printed, Articles 1–17, with its editable lattice diagram, numerical examples, parity tables and 128-case table. Text [VI.] follows in full as printed, with its title and complete Articles 1–6: complete residue systems, associated quarter systems, the character and its independence, boundary conventions and the four-case construction. Their original German prose is rendered in modern German in the German reader; the historical originals are supplied separately.

Schering’s complete historical remarks on the six biquadratic manuscript texts [I.]–[VI.] follow, with his dating qualifications, geometric interpretations, summation derivations and signature. The originally German remarks are rendered in modern German in the German reader; their historical originals are supplied separately.

On the Theory of Complex Numbers follows in full as printed: six fragments [I.]–[VI.] on the decomposition of cubes, the nearest integer, measures of numbers, units and congruences, followed by Schering’s historical remarks. The originally German passages are rendered in modern German in the German reader; their historical originals are supplied separately.

The complete historical table of the quadratic character follows: primes from 2 to 997 as residues with respect to the primes from 3 to 503 as divisors. All historical entries and blanks are retained in editable tables.

The complete historical table for converting common fractions with denominators from the first thousand into decimal fractions follows, including every printed expansion and the primitive-root bands. The complete historical table of the frequency of prime numbers follows, with its matrices, integral values and closing observations.

The complete letter from Gauss to Enke of 24 December 1849 follows, with its tables and discussion of prime-number frequency. The German reader renders the originally German letter in modern German; its historical original is supplied separately.

The complete historical table of the number of classes of binary quadratic forms follows, including negative and positive determinant panels, hundred and thousand groups, and summary tables. Its headings and labels are translated; historical values and notation are retained. The German reader renders the originally German title in modern German; its source text is supplied separately.

The complete historical Table for Circle Division (Cyklotechnie) follows, with all nine factor-table bands and prime-index lists. A New Quadrature of the Circle includes the four printed calculations and the table of arcs; On Calculating Common Logarithms includes its fraction tables and historical editorial account. The First Ten Thousand Squares, Indices of Primes in the Higher Number Domain and the auxiliary table for solving the indeterminate equation by the method of exclusion follow in full as printed. Historical values, blanks and notation are retained.

The printed Eighth Section, Articles 367–373, follows, including its interruption in Article 371 and the complete ending of Article 373. Both complete letters from Gauss to Dirichlet of 13 September 1826 and 30 May 1828 follow, with their tables, dates and signatures. The originally German prose is rendered in modern German in the German reader; the historical originals are supplied separately.

Schering’s complete historical closing remarks follow, with his account of the published papers, manuscript sources, tables and historical corrections, followed by the complete historical contents and Göttingen imprint. Every historical table entry, expression and reference is retained; historical page references use the source pagination. The German reader gives a modern German rendering of the originally German passages; their historical originals are supplied separately. The reported historical corrections remain part of Schering’s account.

Volume III contains the historical title matter and the complete 1799 dissertation, Articles 1–24, all ten historical footnotes, the closing October 1797 statement and all four figures in editable LaTeX. Gauss’s discussion of earlier proofs, geometric proof and concluding alternative argument retain their formulations and qualifications. Its originally German title matter is rendered in modern German in the German reader, with the historical German and Latin source bodies and editable figures supplied separately.

The complete second proof follows with its title matter, Articles 1–20 and both historical footnotes. Presented in 1815 and published in 1816, it develops Gauss’s determinant, symmetric-function and degree-reduction argument, including his criticism of circular proofs, the polynomial divisibility identities and the concluding real linear or quadratic factor argument.

The complete third proof of 1816 follows as a supplement to the preceding paper, with its title matter, introduction, Articles 1–4 and historical footnote. Presented on 30 January 1816 and published that year, it gives the positivity bounds, double-integral argument and real-factor conclusion, followed by Gauss’s discussion of finiteness, continuity and multivalued inverse tangents. His mathematical formulations and qualifications are retained.

The complete 1828 paper Proof of an Algebraic Theorem follows with its historical title and entire argument about positive and negative roots, sign changes and sign continuations of coefficients, including all cases of missing terms. It is a separate work about the rule of signs. The German reader gives a modern German rendering of the original German text; that original is retained separately.

The complete Contributions to the Theory of Algebraic Equations follows with its title, introduction, First Division (Articles 1–10), Second Division (Articles 11–20), two authorial footnotes and complete editable plate. The work was read on 16 July 1849 and published at Göttingen in 1850, in Volume IV of the Abhandlungen der Königlichen Gesellschaft der Wissenschaften. It treats polynomial factorization and the determination of the real and imaginary roots of equations with three terms, with the complete historical auxiliary-logarithm tables and worked calculations. The German reader is a modern German rendering of the historical German text, which is preserved separately.

The algebraic Reviews division contains the complete reviews of 23 December 1815, 2 March 1816 and 30 July 1849 from the Göttingische gelehrte Anzeigen, including the handwritten remarks accompanying the review of 1816. The complete reviews of Coytier of 6 March 1813 and Fourier of 25 February 1833 follow. The review of 1815 discusses the form and existence of roots and the different foundations of Gauss’s proofs. The review of 1816 gives the double-integral proof in full, its discussion of finite values and multivalued inverse tangents, and the accompanying root and equilibrium statements. The review of 1849 discusses the distinct foundations of the proofs, the nature of complex quantities, series and practical methods for equations with three terms. The review of Coytier treats the meanings of analysis and synthesis, roots, equations and functions. The review of Fourier discusses his numerical solution methods, coefficient sign continuations, real-root bounds and the qualifications concerning imaginary root pairs. Their historical formulations and qualifications remain those of the source. The German reader renders the original German reviews in modern German; the historical originals are supplied separately.

The complete available First Part (Pars prior) of General Investigations of the Infinite Series follows. Presented on 30 January 1812 and published at Göttingen in 1813, it contains the full series title, Introduction (Articles 1–6), First Section (7–11), Second Section (12–14), Third Section (15–37), both authorial notes and the complete editable table of 102 printed data rows. It treats contiguous functions, continued fractions, sums at unity, the functions Pi and Psi, infinite products, logarithmic series and definite integrals. The First Part retains the introduction’s promise of a fourth section; that promised section is not supplied in this available First Part.

The complete A New Method for Finding the Values of Integrals by Approximation (Methodus nova integralium valores per approximationem inveniendi), presented on 16 September 1814 and published at Göttingen in 1816, follows with its title, Articles 1–23, both authorial footnotes, all seven editable coefficient tables and all seven numerical examples for the integral of dx/log x from 100000 to 200000. It treats equally and unequally spaced interpolation values, integral coefficients, correction series, polynomial remainders, continued fractions and the choice of evaluation points. Historical formulas, numerical values and qualifications remain those of Gauss’s source. Newton and Cotes retain their historical roles in Gauss’s account. Gauss cites Bessel’s calculation of the closing integral.

## Translation sources and terminology

These English and German texts are AI-translated from Gauss's original-language
editions. Earlier translations are consulted to standardize mathematical
terminology and usage; they are not reproduced as this edition's running text.
The original mathematical formulations remain those of Gauss's source.

H. Maser, German editor and translator, Carl Friedrich Gauss' Untersuchungen
über höhere Arithmetik, Berlin: Julius Springer, 1889, is a reference for
German arithmetical terminology and corresponding passages.
https://archive.org/details/carlfriedrichga00gausgoog

James Caddall Morehead and Adam Miller Hiltebeitel, translators, General
Investigations of Curved Surfaces of 1827 and 1825, Princeton University
Library, 1902 (Project Gutenberg 36856), and Paul Taylor's English translation
of Gauss's second proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra, with corrections by Bernard
Leak, 1983, are references for English mathematical terminology and usage.
Taylor–Leak is a corresponding translation of the 1815 second proof, and a related terminology reference for the arithmetic papers and 1799 dissertation. Morehead–Hiltebeitel supplies related geometrical terminology, including continuity, integration and signed-area usage for the third proof of 1816. Taylor–Leak is not a parallel translation of that third proof. Related Taylor–Leak and Maser terminology was also consulted for the 1828 sign-rule work; neither is a parallel translation of it and no human running prose is adopted. Neither replaces Gauss’s original mathematics or exposition.
